# The Game

## Overview

The Game by Terry Schott presents a science-fiction novel in which Tygon players live a complete lifetime on Earth, a virtual world administered by the Mainframe computer, and receive a ranking on Tygon after the avatar lifetime ends. The nine lessons in BOOK.md convert the novel premise into a practical philosophy: a person works toward a named finish and a written set of rules by playing a lifetime deliberately, by focusing attention on good moments, by preparing resources before a challenge begins, and by naming the reader's next action after an unplanned event instead of waiting for another person to direct the reader. The Game also warns readers that a system built for education can drift into entertainment, addiction, and lethal risk when participants forget the purpose of the system and forget the real person behind an avatar.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When a reader faces a project, a career, or a course of study and the reader needs the written play name, the written rules, the daily move score, and the named finish that Lesson 1 Steps 1 to 4 define
- When a reader stores a painful event in memory and the reader replays the painful event while ignoring good events from the same day, week, or year
- When a reader prepares for a new job, a new school term, a competition, or a move to a new city and the reader can spend money, time, and attention before the start date
- When a reader enters a challenge alone and the reader needs patrons, mentors, teammates, family members, and an audience recruited before the challenge reaches a crisis
- When a reader receives a weak signal (a coincidence, a repeated thought about a named person, a sudden silence or a shift in a familiar place, or a sudden preference for one named option that the reader cannot yet support with a recorded reason) and the reader needs a disciplined method for testing whether a low-cost test of the signal produces a recorded outcome that advances the finish named in Lesson 1, as Lesson 6 Steps 2 and 3 define
- When a plan fails in public and the reader must decide whether Brandon Strayne's response (issuing orders to a command team that could not stop, guide, or cause the decisive events, Epilogue on PDF pages 275-276) or Trew Radfield's response (the three-part instruction to focus on the good, forget the bad, and move calmly through the filler, Epilogue on PDF page 275) fits the reader's situation
- When a reader uses a platform, a game, a workplace system, or a school system and the reader needs to audit whether the system still serves the stated original purpose of the system
- When a reader treats hardship, rivalry, or loss as proof of failure and the reader needs a method for extracting information and motivation from contrast
- When a reader risks real money, real health, or real reputation inside a simulated, online, or competitive environment and the reader needs exit rules, monitoring rules, and a named person with authority to stop participation

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Play Life as a Game on Purpose

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Danielle Benton Reaches the Top 10,000 Without a Credit-Heavy Plan

In Chapter 30 on PDF (Portable Document Format) page 135, Brandon Strayne reviews Danielle Benton from Brandon Strayne's penthouse balcony. Brandon Strayne states that Trew Radfield spent credits to achieve results and that Danielle Benton achieved comparable results with seeming ease. Brandon Strayne states the reason directly: Danielle Benton played with passion and purpose at every activity Danielle Benton chose, and playing as if life was a game made Danielle Benton successful. The floating standings place Danielle Benton inside the top 10,000 gamers during the review in Chapter 30.

##### Example 2: George Knight Regrets Forgetting How to Play

In Chapter 22 on PDF page 100, an excerpt from the Earth book titled The Game Is Life presents George Knight judging George Knight's own lifetime. George Knight writes that George Knight wasted youth by sitting idle, wasted middle years by neglecting exercise and food, and allowed George Knight's mind to sleep after the world told George Knight to grow up. George Knight concludes that growing up made George Knight forget how to play games and that forgetting how to play removed George Knight's chance of succeeding if life is a game. The excerpt gives Lesson 1 a negative case in George Knight's own list: youth spent sitting idle, middle years spent neglecting exercise and food, and a mind allowed to sleep after the world told George Knight to grow up.

##### Example 3: Zack Chooses a New Name for a Final Play

In Chapter 11 on PDF pages 50-51, Zack prepares for Zack's final play and rejects the free tradition of keeping the first name Zack. Zack chooses the new name Trew Radfield because Zack associates first attempts and new names with good luck. Zack treats the entry as a deliberate play with a goal, a chosen identity, and a finish ranked number one, rather than treating the entry as a passive return to Earth.

#### Step 1: Name the Play, the Player, and the Finish

The reader writes one sentence naming the play, naming the reader as the player, and naming the finish the reader will accept as completion. A valid sentence reads: the reader will play the autumn semester as a learning play, the reader will attend every scheduled class, and the reader will finish the semester with a submitted portfolio on the final class date. The reader posts the sentence in a place the reader sees each morning. The reader knows Step 1 worked when the reader can recite the play, the role, and the finish without reading the sentence.

#### Step 2: Write the Rules the Player Will Obey During the Play

The reader lists five rules that govern conduct during the play. Each rule names an action, a trigger, and a consequence. A valid rule reads: when a setback occurs during the play, the reader records the setback in a notebook within one hour, the reader writes one lesson from the setback, and the reader returns to the next scheduled task on the same day. The reader reviews the five rules every Sunday evening. The reader knows Step 2 worked when a setback occurs and the reader follows a written rule instead of improvising a reaction driven by embarrassment or anger.

#### Step 3: Play Each Chosen Activity With a Stated Purpose and No Second Activity

The reader selects one activity for the day, states the purpose of the activity in one sentence before starting the activity, and performs no second activity and answers no messages until a timer the reader sets before starting ends the activity, or until the reader completes a unit of work the reader named before starting (one chapter, one report section, or one exercise set). Danielle Benton models the standard in Chapter 30 on PDF page 135: Danielle Benton chose activities and Danielle Benton played each chosen activity with passion and purpose. The reader knows Step 3 worked when the reader can state, after the activity ends, what the reader attempted, what result the activity produced, and what move the reader will make next.

#### Step 4: Score the Day and Adjust the Next Move

The reader ends each day by writing three lines in a notebook: the best move the reader made, the weakest move the reader made, and the next move the reader will make on the following day. The reader labels one move best when the move completed a written rule from Step 2 or a scheduled task toward the finish named in Step 1, and labels one move weakest when the move completed no written rule and no scheduled task. The reader does not score the day with a global judgment about personal worth. The reader scores moves only. The reader knows Step 4 worked when the notebook contains thirty consecutive daily entries and when a later entry shows a changed move that responds to an earlier weakest move.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Name the play and the finish before the play begins, as Zack names the goal of finishing ranked number one before Zack enters the Game as Trew Radfield
- ✅ Give a chosen activity full attention for a defined period, as Danielle Benton gives chosen activities passion and purpose in Chapter 30 on PDF page 135
- ✅ Score moves, not personal worth, so a weak move becomes information for the next move
- ❌ Do not treat a setback as a final verdict on the player; Brandon Strayne treats Danielle Benton as a problem to remove in Chapter 20 on PDF page 94, and Brandon Strayne's control plan fails
- ❌ Do not stop playing in middle years, as George Knight reports in Chapter 22 on PDF page 100; George Knight links stopped play to a sleeping mind and a neglected body

#### Keep In Mind

- Trew Radfield inside Earth does not know Trew Radfield is Zack; the reader likewise plays without full knowledge of every force shaping the reader's lifetime, and Lesson 1 requires deliberate play inside incomplete knowledge
- Danielle Benton believes Danielle Benton is inside a game and will return to a real body after death inside Earth; Brandon Strayne calls Danielle Benton reckless for the belief in Chapter 30 on PDF pages 136-137, and Lesson 9 sets the safety boundary for Lesson 1
- Danielle Benton's game frame accompanies Danielle Benton's persistence in Chapter 30 on PDF page 135, where Brandon Strayne states that Danielle Benton played as if life was a game and reached the top 10,000; a game frame does not remove pain, cost, or consequence from real actions

#### Security & Safety Notes

- A reader must not use the game frame to justify risking real health, real savings, or real relationships; Danielle Benton's belief that death inside the Game returns a player to a real body is true inside the fiction of The Game and false for a real person on real Earth
- A reader must not treat another person as a non-player character; every person the reader meets during a play is a real person with rights, and Zack's support plan in Chapter 11 uses consenting players who spent credits willingly
- A reader who notices compulsive play, sleepless monitoring of scores, or inability to stop an activity must stop the activity and seek help from a counsellor licensed in the reader's country, or a person the reader named in writing before the play began as the person the reader will contact for help; Tygon society in Chapter 15 on PDF page 65 shows a population that reorganised an entire economy around watching the Game

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** The reader calls life a game but writes no finish, no rules, and no score, so the game frame becomes a slogan without behaviour change
  **Solution:** The reader completes Step 1 and Step 2 in writing before the play begins and reviews the written rules every Sunday evening
- **Problem:** The reader plays recklessly because the reader assumes a reset will erase consequences, mirroring the recklessness Brandon Strayne attributes to Danielle Benton in Chapter 30 on PDF pages 136-137
  **Solution:** The reader applies Lesson 9 before Lesson 1: the reader names the real cost of failure, names an exit rule, and refuses any move whose real cost the reader cannot bear
- **Problem:** The reader quits an activity after a first failure and calls the quit a strategic decision, mirroring George Knight's regret in Chapter 22 on PDF page 100
  **Solution:** The reader records the failure as a scored move under Step 4 and makes one adjusted next move before the reader may end the play

### Lesson 2: Focus on the Good, Release the Bad, and Search the Filler

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Trew Radfield States the Three-Part Instruction in the Epilogue

In the Epilogue on PDF page 275, an excerpt from an interview during Trew Radfield's 40th birthday celebration presents Trew Radfield's advice in Trew Radfield's own words. Trew Radfield states that each day contains good moments, bad moments, and filler, and Trew Radfield gives the secret to a happy life as a three-part instruction: focus on the good, forget the bad, and move calmly through the filler without boredom taking control. Trew Radfield adds a fallback instruction for readers who find the three-part instruction complicated: smile and laugh as much as the reader possibly can.

##### Example 2: Trew Radfield Explains Why Viewing a Life as Good Changes the Life

In the Epilogue on PDF page 275, Trew Radfield states that Trew Radfield viewed Trew Radfield's life as good and that the viewing helped Trew Radfield live a life that Trew Radfield judges to be great. Trew Radfield contrasts Trew Radfield's choice with a different choice: a person can live through painful moments, decide the moments were terrible, store bitterness and resentment, and allow bitterness and resentment to shape the person's view of later events. Trew Radfield chose the view stated in the previous sentence: Trew Radfield viewed Trew Radfield's life as good, and Trew Radfield names that view as one cause of the result Trew Radfield names in the same Epilogue passage: Trew Radfield judges Trew Radfield's life to be great.

##### Example 3: George Knight Advises Building Reserves During Good Periods

In Chapter 48 on PDF page 218, an excerpt from the Earth book titled The Game Is Life presents George Knight advising readers to slow down and enjoy good periods. George Knight states that a good period can last for years and that a reader must not waste one precious moment of a good period, because a bad period will come and a reader who built no reserves during the good period may not survive the bad period to reach the next good period. George Knight's reserve advice gives Lesson 2 a preparation function: stored good moments become a resource during a later bad period.

#### Step 1: Record a Happy Moment While the Happy Moment Is Present

When a happy moment occurs, the reader stops activity for one minute, smiles, and writes the happy moment in a dedicated notebook with a date, a place, and the names of the people present. Trew Radfield instructs readers in the Epilogue on PDF page 275 to tuck the happy moment away in memory for later use. The reader knows Step 1 worked when the notebook contains a dated entry written on the day of the happy moment, not a reconstruction written weeks later.

#### Step 2: Let a Bad Moment Pass Without Storing the Bad Moment

When a bad moment occurs, the reader names the bad moment aloud in one factual sentence, completes any required practical action, and then deliberately turns attention to the next scheduled task. Trew Radfield instructs readers in the Epilogue on PDF page 275 to avoid living in a bad moment, to let the bad moment pass as quickly as the bad moment can pass, and to avoid grabbing the bad moment and tucking the bad moment away in memory. The reader knows Step 2 worked when, one week later, the reader can state the practical lesson from the bad moment without rehearsing grievance, blame, or humiliation in detail.

#### Step 3: Search Filler Periods for a Happy Moment and a Bad Moment

During a filler period such as a commute, a queue, a routine chore, or a quiet work hour, the reader searches the filler period deliberately for one happy element the reader can notice and enjoy. Trew Radfield instructs readers in the Epilogue on PDF page 275 to search earnestly for happy moments during filler and to realise that happy moments and bad moments both float inside filler, waiting for a person who chooses to focus on a happy moment or a bad moment. The reader chooses the happy element, names the happy element in the notebook from Step 1, and continues the filler task calmly. The reader knows Step 3 worked when the notebook contains entries that originated during commutes, queues, and chores, not only entries from celebrations.

#### Step 4: Use the Fallback Instruction During a Day With No Obvious Good Moment

On a day when the reader finds no obvious good moment, the reader applies Trew Radfield's fallback instruction from the Epilogue on PDF page 275: the reader smiles and laughs as much as the reader possibly can. Concretely, the reader watches or reads a comedy source the reader already enjoys, calls a friend who makes the reader laugh, and smiles deliberately during a walk. The reader knows Step 4 worked when the reader ends the difficult day having performed the fallback actions, even though the reader's mood improved only slightly or not at all.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Write a happy moment down on the day the happy moment occurs, following Trew Radfield's instruction to tuck a happy moment away in memory
- ✅ Extract a practical lesson from a bad moment and release the emotional rehearsal of the bad moment, following Trew Radfield's instruction to let a bad moment pass quickly
- ✅ Treat filler as searchable territory, following Trew Radfield's statement that happy moments and bad moments both wait inside filler for a person who chooses a focus
- ❌ Do not store bitterness and resentment as a lens for later events; Trew Radfield warns in the Epilogue on PDF page 275 that bitterness and resentment shape how a person views upcoming events
- ❌ Do not waste a good period without building reserves; George Knight warns in Chapter 48 on PDF page 218 that a bad period follows a good period and an unprepared reader may not reach the next good period

#### Keep In Mind

- Focusing on the good directs the reader's notebook entries and memory to good moments; focusing on the good does not instruct the reader to state that a bad moment did not occur; Trew Radfield acknowledges tough, challenging, and painful times inside Trew Radfield's own life in the Epilogue on PDF page 275
- A bad moment can require practical action, medical care, an apology, or a report to an authority; Lesson 2 governs memory and attention after the reader completes the required practical action
- Reserves built during a good period include memories, friendships, savings, health, and skills; George Knight's excerpt in Chapter 48 addresses time and attention, and the reader builds reserves in the five categories named in the first clause of this bullet: memories, friendships, savings, health, and skills

#### Security & Safety Notes

- A reader must not use Lesson 2 to ignore abuse, injury, illness, or danger; Trew Radfield's instruction to forget the bad applies to storing resentment, and Trew Radfield's instruction does not cancel a duty to report harm or to seek treatment
- A reader who cannot release a traumatic event must seek support from a person the reader named in writing before the play began as the person the reader will contact for help, a counsellor licensed in the reader's country, or a medical professional; Danielle Benton's crisis in Chapter 49 on PDF pages 223-224 and Chapter 50 on PDF page 225 shows a family emergency that required a 911 call and hospital care, not positive thinking alone
- A reader must not pressure another person to smile or laugh during grief; Trew Radfield gives the fallback instruction as advice for the reader's own conduct, and Trew Radfield sits beside Danielle Benton in hospital in Chapter 50 without demanding cheerfulness from Danielle Benton

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** The reader rehearses a bad moment nightly and calls the rehearsal learning, so resentment grows while no new lesson appears
  **Solution:** The reader writes the practical lesson from the bad moment in one sentence, closes the notebook, and redirects attention to the next scheduled task each time the rehearsal begins
- **Problem:** The reader treats filler as dead time and fills every filler period with anxious scrolling, so the reader notices bad elements by default
  **Solution:** The reader assigns Step 3 to two named filler periods per day, the morning commute and the evening chore period, and records one happy element from each named period
- **Problem:** The reader uses Lesson 2 to avoid a necessary difficult conversation, repair, or medical appointment
  **Solution:** The reader completes the required practical action first, schedules the action on a calendar with a date and a time, and applies Lesson 2 only to memory and attention after the action is complete

### Lesson 3: Treat Belief as the Limit on Action

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Players' Handbook Excerpt Defines Belief as a Limit

Chapter 60 on PDF page 267 opens with an excerpt from A Players' Handbook for the Game of Life. The excerpt states that belief is a remarkable thing, that a person who believes the person can do an action can do the action, and that a person who believes the person cannot do an action cannot do the action. The excerpt states that a belief system limits a person, shapes the person's entire life, and shapes the lives of people around the person.

##### Example 2: The Heretic in the Chapter 60 Excerpt Advances a Limit by Persisting

The Chapter 60 excerpt on PDF page 267 describes a Heretic who holds a belief the masses reject. The excerpt states that people scorn, mock, laugh at, challenge, and dismiss the Heretic, and the Heretic continues to believe. The excerpt states that one or two people join the Heretic first, and after persistent sharing of the message the group adopts the Heretic's belief, and the limit the group previously stated advances.

##### Example 3: Brandon Strayne States That Zack's Avatar Must Believe the Goal Is Possible

In Chapter 11 on PDF page 50, Brandon Strayne tells Zack that the goal of finishing ranked number one is possible and adds the condition: Brandon Strayne and Zack must get Zack's avatar to believe the goal is possible, and then Zack retires number one. Brandon Strayne treats the avatar's belief as a practical input to performance, separate from credits, placed helpers, and purchased advantages.

#### Step 1: Write the Limiting Belief in a Testable Sentence

The reader writes one limiting belief as a sentence with a measurable claim. A valid sentence reads: the reader believes the reader cannot speak in public without freezing, and freezing means the reader stops speaking for more than ten seconds. The reader knows Step 1 worked when the sentence contains an action, a predicted failure, and a measurable sign of the predicted failure.

#### Step 2: Gather Counter-Evidence From the Reader's Own Record

The reader searches the reader's own history for two events that contradict the limiting belief. The reader writes each event with a date and a factual description. The reader might record a school presentation completed without a ten-second freeze and a work meeting where the reader answered a question aloud. The reader knows Step 2 worked when the reader holds written counter-evidence that the reader personally produced, not encouragement borrowed from another person.

#### Step 3: Run a Small Test That the Limiting Belief Predicts Will Fail

The reader designs a test that lasts less than one hour, carries a cost the reader can bear, and directly challenges the measurable claim in Step 1. The reader gives a two-minute talk to three listeners the reader named in writing before the test. The reader records the result factually: duration, number of freezes longer than ten seconds, and one observation from a listener. The reader knows Step 3 worked when the reader possesses a recorded result, whether the result confirms the limiting belief or contradicts the limiting belief.

#### Step 4: Revise the Belief Sentence to Match the Recorded Result

The reader rewrites the belief sentence from Step 1 using the recorded result from Step 3. If the test contradicts the limiting belief, the reader writes a capability sentence with the same measurable terms. If the test confirms a genuine skill gap, the reader writes a training sentence naming the skill, the training method, and the retest date. The reader repeats Step 3 and Step 4 monthly. The reader knows Step 4 worked when the belief sentence in the reader's notebook matches evidence the reader can point to, line by line.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ State a belief as a measurable claim, following the Chapter 60 excerpt on PDF page 267, which links belief directly to whether a person can or cannot perform an action
- ✅ Persist through mockery only when the belief rests on evidence and a testable claim, following the Heretic pattern in the Chapter 60 excerpt, where persistence plus persuasive sharing changes a group limit
- ✅ Treat a performer's belief as a trainable input, following Brandon Strayne's condition in Chapter 11 on PDF page 50 that Zack's avatar must believe the number-one goal is possible
- ❌ Do not repeat an affirmation that the reader's own record contradicts; a false affirmation collapses at the first real test and damages trust in every other belief the reader holds
- ❌ Do not label every critic a persecutor of a Heretic; the Chapter 60 excerpt describes scorn directed at a belief that later proves persuasive and useful, and a belief that fails repeated tests that meet the Step 3 conditions (a test lasting less than one hour, a cost the reader can bear, and a recorded result) requires revision, not martyrdom

#### Keep In Mind

- Belief limits the actions a person attempts, as the Chapter 60 excerpt states on PDF page 267; belief does not replace skill, preparation, or resources, and Zack pairs belief with credits, placed helpers, and a command team in Chapter 11
- A belief held by a leader shapes the lives of people around the leader, per the Chapter 60 excerpt on PDF page 267; a leader carries extra responsibility for testing beliefs before imposing beliefs on a team
- Danielle Benton's belief that Danielle Benton is inside a game supports Danielle Benton's performance and also produces recklessness that worries Brandon Strayne in Chapter 30; Lesson 3 and Lesson 9 must operate together

#### Security & Safety Notes

- A reader must not use belief to override medical limits, safety rules, or professional qualifications; a belief that a person can perform surgery, fly an aircraft, or climb without equipment does not create the competence the task requires
- A reader must distrust any teacher who demands belief without tests and who punishes questions; the Heretic in the Chapter 60 excerpt persuades by sharing a message repeatedly, and coercion plays no part in the Heretic pattern
- A reader must not use a limiting-belief diagnosis to blame a person facing real barriers such as poverty, discrimination, illness, or injury; Alexandra Montoyas loses every credit through a 0.02 percent failure event in Chapter 7 on PDF pages 27-28, and no belief revision restores Alexandra Montoyas's credits

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** The reader treats belief as magic and skips preparation, expecting confidence alone to produce a result that credits, training, and helpers produced for Zack
  **Solution:** The reader pairs every belief revision under Step 4 with a preparation action under Lesson 4 and a support action under Lesson 5
- **Problem:** The reader holds a limiting belief as an identity statement, for example the reader says the reader is simply not a mathematics person, so no test ever occurs
  **Solution:** The reader converts the identity statement into the measurable sentence required by Step 1 and runs the small test required by Step 3 within seven days
- **Problem:** The reader adopts a belief that the reader can perform a task at a level higher than the highest level recorded under Step 3 after one success
  **Solution:** The reader raises the test level by one step at a time, records each result under Step 3, and advances only after two recorded successes at the current level


### Lesson 4: Invest Resources Before the Play Starts

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Zack Enters the Game With Parents, Helpers, and Purchased Advantages Already Placed

In Chapter 11 on PDF pages 47-49, Zack prepares for Zack's final play. Brandon Strayne reports that Kyle and Marcie, Zack's two best friends, already live inside the Game as a married couple expecting a first child, and Kyle and Marcie will be the parents of Zack's avatar. Brandon Strayne reports that over 546 additional players will interact with Zack's avatar, positively and negatively, to steer Zack's avatar toward the chosen direction. Brandon Strayne explains that a teacher placement, a one-month girlfriend placement, a car sale at age thirty, and a robbery at age forty each cost an individual helper only a few thousand extra credits, and each helper gains wealth if the plan succeeds.

##### Example 2: The Gamers Manual Lists Purchasable Attributes, Power-Ups, Scenarios, and Skills

Chapter 14 on PDF page 63 opens with an excerpt from Gamers Manual 7. The excerpt lists common attribute selections: Longevity, Health, Intelligence, Strength, Love, Relationships, Focus, Spirituality, Intuition, and Aptitude. The excerpt states that attributes, power-ups, scenarios, and skills combine, and the combinations make each avatar different from every other avatar, and the excerpt warns that the Mainframe makes a clear path to the top rankings impossible, because a strategy that works for one player will not work for another player and repeating a strategy does not produce identical results for the same player on a later play.

##### Example 3: Alexandra Montoyas Spends Every Credit on One Plan and Loses Every Credit

In Chapter 7 on PDF pages 27-28, Alexandra Montoyas and Zack plan a shared play and Alexandra Montoyas spends every credit Alexandra Montoyas owns on the plan. A low-ranked player spends credits to become a doctor, positions the doctor avatar to deliver Alexandra Montoyas's avatar, and lacks the experience and the credits spent on luck, the purchasable category named in Lesson 4 Keep In Mind at line 249, that experienced players purchase for a doctor role. Alexandra Montoyas's avatar dies during childbirth, the 0.02 percent failure event occurs, and Alexandra Montoyas wakes in the white room (the room on Tygon where a player wakes after a play ends, Chapter 1 on PDF page 4) ten minutes after entry with no credits remaining, not even enough credits to buy admission back into the Game or admission into a public school as a paying student.

#### Step 1: List the Starting Conditions the Reader Can Choose Before the Start Date

Before a job, a term, a competition, or a project begins, the reader writes two columns. Column A lists starting conditions the reader can choose: location, schedule, tools, teachers, savings, sleep routine, and practice time. Column B lists starting conditions the reader cannot choose. The reader spends preparation effort only on Column A entries. The reader knows Step 1 worked when Column A contains at least five entries and each Column A entry carries a named owner and a completion date.

#### Step 2: Allocate a Preparation Budget Across Attributes the Play Will Test

The reader names the attributes the upcoming play will test, using the Chapter 14 list on PDF page 63 as a prompt: health, focus, relationships, intuition, aptitude, and strength. For each named attribute the reader writes one preparation action with a number (hours practised, sessions completed, or a test score) before allocating money or hours. The reader allocates a fixed budget of money and hours across the named attributes, with a written amount beside each attribute. The reader protects a reserve of at least twenty percent of the total budget and assigns the reserve no purpose in advance. The reader knows Step 2 worked when the written budget balances, when no single attribute consumes the entire budget, and when the reserve remains unspent one week before the start date.

#### Step 3: Place Helpers in Advance With Specific Roles

Following Brandon Strayne's placement method in Chapter 11 on PDF pages 48-49, the reader recruits helpers before the start date and gives each helper one specific role, one specific date, and one specific deliverable. A valid placement reads: a named friend will review the reader's portfolio draft on the second Tuesday of the term and return written comments within two days. Vague promises of support do not count as placements. The reader knows Step 3 worked when each helper confirms the role, the date, and the deliverable in writing.

#### Step 4: War-Game the Failure Event and Cap the Downside

The reader writes the worst plausible failure event for the play, following the Alexandra Montoyas case in Chapter 7, where a single early failure ended a lifetime plan in ten minutes. The reader then writes a cap: a maximum loss of money, time, or reputation that the reader can bear while still retaining the ability to enter a next play. The reader adjusts spending until the worst plausible loss sits at or below the cap. The reader knows Step 4 worked when the written plan shows a reserve, a cap, and a survivable outcome after the worst plausible failure event.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Choose parents, teachers, location, and tools before the start date when the choice is available, as Brandon Strayne places Kyle and Marcie as Zack's avatar parents before Zack enters the Game in Chapter 11
- ✅ Spread a preparation budget across at least three of the attributes named in Step 2, as the Gamers Manual excerpt in Chapter 14 on PDF page 63 presents attributes in combination rather than in isolation
- ✅ Keep a reserve unspent, because the Chapter 14 excerpt on PDF page 63 states that repeating a strategy does not produce identical results for the same player on a later play, and a play will present a cost the reader did not predict
- ❌ Do not spend every available credit on one plan; Alexandra Montoyas spends every credit in Chapter 7 and a single childbirth failure leaves Alexandra Montoyas with no route back into the Game
- ❌ Do not copy another player's exact preparation as a guaranteed path; the Chapter 14 excerpt states that a strategy that works for one player will not work for another player

#### Keep In Mind

- Preparation changes starting conditions; preparation does not control events after the start, and Brandon Strayne admits in the Epilogue on PDF page 276 that Brandon Strayne's team could not stop, guide, or cause the decisive events of Trew Radfield's life
- A helper who spends resources on the reader's play expects a return when the play succeeds; Brandon Strayne tells Zack in Chapter 11 on PDF page 49 that each placed helper will become wealthy in credits if the plan succeeds, and a reader must state the return for helpers honestly in advance
- Inside the fiction of The Game, a player can spend credits on luck; in real life, no seller can deliver a named favourable outcome to the reader in exchange for payment; a reader replaces a luck purchase with a reserve, insurance that covers a loss named in the Step 4 failure event, and a downside cap under Step 4

#### Security & Safety Notes

- A reader must never stake money, housing, food, or medical care that the reader needs for survival on a single play; Alexandra Montoyas loses housing and food security when Alexandra Montoyas's patron exercises an escape clause after the failed play in Chapter 7 on PDF page 28
- A reader must verify any person who sells preparation advantages, courses, or placements; Tygon patrons operate under contracts with escape clauses, and a real reader must read every contract clause about refunds, exits, and obligations before paying
- A reader must not purchase advantages that violate the rules of a school, an employer, a competition, or a law; Chapter 20 on PDF page 93 states that attempting to hack the Game is the most serious crime on Tygon and carries a death punishment inside the fiction, and real rule violations carry real penalties without any fictional reset

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** The reader spends the entire preparation budget on one attribute, such as equipment, and leaves health, relationships, and focus unfunded
  **Solution:** The reader applies Step 2, writes an amount beside each named attribute, and refuses to approve a budget where one attribute consumes more than half of the total budget
- **Problem:** The reader prepares for the previous play and repeats a strategy that succeeded earlier, expecting identical results
  **Solution:** The reader heeds the Chapter 14 warning on PDF page 63, lists three differences between the earlier situation and the upcoming situation, and adjusts one preparation choice for each listed difference
- **Problem:** The reader recruits helpers with vague promises and discovers during a crisis that no helper agreed to a specific task
  **Solution:** The reader applies Step 3 and obtains a written confirmation naming the role, the date, and the deliverable for each helper before the start date

### Lesson 5: Build the Team and the Audience Before the Reader Needs the Team and the Audience

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Michelle Reports That Building a Fan Base Is Very Difficult

### Lesson 6: Listen for Weak Signals and Let Communication Escalate Through Practice

#### Examples

##### Example 1: George Knight Describes Communication From the Master Computer as an Escalating Form

In the Chapter 49 excerpt on PDF page 222, George Knight states that a person who senses a creator's presence during small moments becomes ready to handle more direct contact as time passes. George Knight states that a person who practises may participate and interact when more direct communication arrives. Chapter 12 on PDF page 52 presents an excerpt from Religion in the Game. #### Step 1: Record Weak Signals in a Signal Log on the Day a Signal Occurs

The reader keeps a signal log with four columns: date and time, signal description, action the reader took, and outcome after seven days. A valid entry describes a coincidence, a repeated thought about a named person, a sudden silence or shift in a familiar place, or a sudden preference for one named option that the reader cannot yet support with a recorded reason, using George Knight's examples in Chapter 49 on PDF page 222 as categories. The reader writes the entry within one hour of noticing the signal. The reader knows Step 1 worked when the log contains ten dated entries and each entry separates the observed event from the reader's interpretation of the observed event.

#### Step 2: Test a Signal With a Low-Cost Action Before Trusting the Signal

When a signal suggests an action, the reader chooses the lowest-cost action that can test the suggestion. A thought about a friend the reader has not contacted for at least one calendar year becomes a short message to the friend, not a major life decision. A sudden preference for one named option that the reader cannot yet support with a recorded reason, when the preference concerns a workplace decision, becomes a question to a mentor under Lesson 5, not a resignation. The reader records the test and the result in the signal log. The reader knows Step 2 worked when the log shows a test action and a recorded result for at least half of the logged signals.

#### Step 3: Review the Signal Log Monthly and Promote Only Signals With a Track Record

On the last day of each month, the reader counts signals in the log, counts tested signals, and counts tested signals whose suggested action produced an outcome the reader recorded as advancing the finish named in Lesson 1. The reader writes the label TRUSTED in the log beside a signal category with a recorded success rate above half of the tested entries in the category, and writes the label UNPROVEN beside a category with no tests or with a success rate at or below half of the tested entries in the category. The reader knows Step 3 worked when the monthly review exists in writing and when the reader can name which signal category earned trust and which signal category remains unproven.

#### Step 4: Practise Quiet Attention for Ten Minutes Daily

The reader sits in a familiar place for ten minutes daily without a phone, a feed, or a task, and notices sounds, silences, movements, and thoughts as George Knight's excerpt directs attention to wind, silence in a park, and animal behaviour. After the ten minutes end, the reader writes one observation in the signal log. The reader knows Step 4 worked when the log contains daily observations for fourteen consecutive days and when the reader can describe a difference between a noticed observation and an imagined interpretation without blending the observation and the interpretation together.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Begin with small signals, following George Knight's escalation order in Chapter 49 on PDF page 222: wind, silence, animal behaviour, and coincidence precede direct communication
- ✅ Separate observation from interpretation in every log entry, so a later review can test the interpretation against outcomes
- ✅ Use a signal as a prompt for a low-cost test, never as a command for a high-cost action
- ❌ Do not dismiss a weak signal category without a single test; George Knight states in Chapter 49 that people who laugh at the first steps never reach the larger steps of communication
- ❌ Do not treat a signal as proof of a supernatural claim in front of other people; the Chapter 12 excerpt on PDF page 52 shows Earth citizens building entire religions around a sensed presence, and a reader who skips testing can build a costly system on an untested interpretation

#### Keep In Mind

- Inside the fiction of The Game, the sensed presence is real and the sensed presence is a computer; a real reader cannot verify an equivalent source, and Lesson 6 therefore trains attention, pattern recognition, and intuition testing rather than asserting a theology
- Trew Radfield practises a related attention skill in Chapter 35 on PDF pages 159-160, where Trew Radfield's parents train Trew Radfield to ask questions and listen, and Trew Radfield reports that people reveal information when a listener shows genuine interest
- A signal log becomes more useful as the log grows; the reader judges each signal category only by the Lesson 6 Step 3 rule: a category is TRUSTED when the tested entries in the category record a success rate above half, and UNPROVEN in every other case

#### Security & Safety Notes

- A reader must not follow a signal, a voice, a dream, or an intuition into danger, illegality, or harm to another person; a signal never overrides law, medical advice, or the explicit refusal of another person
- A reader who hears commanding voices, who cannot sleep because of perceived messages, or who feels compelled to obey a perceived presence must seek medical and professional help promptly; George Knight's claim in Chapter 49 on PDF page 222 that the ability to communicate can save a person from madness applies to a practised sense of connection during trouble, and distressing compulsion is a medical matter, not a spiritual achievement

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** The reader notices coincidences and assigns meaning to every coincidence, so noise overwhelms signal and the reader makes decisions from random events
  **Solution:** The reader applies Step 2 and Step 3, tests signals at low cost, and promotes only a category with a recorded success rate above half of tested entries
- **Problem:** The reader keeps no log, remembers only the signals that later prove useful, and forgets the signals that later prove useless, so intuition appears more accurate than intuition is
  **Solution:** The reader writes every signal in the log under Step 1 on the day the signal occurs, including signals that later prove useless, and the monthly review counts every entry
- **Problem:** The reader waits for a dramatic message and ignores quiet signals during daily routines
  **Solution:** The reader performs Step 4 daily for fourteen days and records one quiet observation per day, training attention on the small end of George Knight's escalation scale

The book ends the Zack storyline with an instruction about the body that machines keep alive: do not unplug Zack. Lesson 9 answers the question in advance: a named person must hold authority over stopping a system that sustains a real person, and the authority must be decided before a crisis.

#### Step 1: Write the Real Stakes Beside the Simulated Stakes Before Entry

Before entering any competitive, virtual, financial, or physically demanding system, the reader writes two columns. Column A lists simulated stakes: score, ranking, avatar condition, and in-system currency. Column B lists real stakes: money the reader cannot replace, health effects, reputation effects, legal exposure, and effects on family members. The reader signs and dates the two columns. The reader knows Step 1 worked when Column B names at least one real stake that no simulated reward can repay.

#### Step 2: Set Exit Rules With a Named Authority and a Trigger

The reader writes an exit rule for each real stake in Column B. Each exit rule names a trigger, an action, and a person with authority to enforce the exit when the reader cannot or will not enforce the exit personally. A valid rule reads: if the reader loses the capped amount named in Lesson 4 Step 4, the reader stops participation for thirty days, and the mentor named in Lesson 5 holds the reader's access credentials during the thirty days. The reader gives a copy of every exit rule to the named authority. The reader knows Step 2 worked when the named authority confirms receipt of the exit rules and can state the trigger and the action without asking the reader.

#### Step 3: Install Monitoring That the Participant Does Not Control Alone

The reader arranges monitoring by a person or a system outside the reader's sole control: a teammate reviews activity weekly, a bank sends transaction alerts to a second person, a coach receives training load data, or a platform sends session-time reports to a patron. Zack's command team models outside monitoring in Chapter 15, watching feeds Zack cannot see and reporting an attempt to kill Trew Radfield. The reader knows Step 3 worked when the monitor produces a report the reader did not edit and when the report reaches the named authority from Step 2 on schedule.

#### Step 4: Decide the Plug Question in Advance and Record the Decision

The reader answers, in writing, the question the Epilogue forces on Brandon Strayne: under what conditions may participation be stopped while stopping causes a loss named in the reader's Column B list from Step 1, and who decides. For medical participation, the reader completes an advance directive and names a decision-maker according to local law. For financial or online participation, the reader names the condition under which accounts close even when closure destroys a ranking, a streak, or a following. The reader reviews the recorded decision annually. The reader knows Step 4 worked when the decision exists in writing, the named decision-maker holds a copy, and the decision names conditions rather than relying on judgment during a crisis.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Separate simulated stakes from real stakes in writing before entry, because a Sever Spike collapses the separation inside the fiction of The Game and kills Zack permanently in the Epilogue
- ✅ Give exit authority to a named person outside the emotional centre of a crisis, following Angelica's arrangement in Chapter 50 on PDF page 226, where Raphael and Stephanie guard Danielle Benton in rotation
- ✅ Respect system security rules absolutely; Chapter 20 on PDF page 93 presents hacking as the most serious crime on Tygon, and a real reader must treat unauthorised access to any system as prohibited conduct with real legal consequences
- ❌ Do not assume a system operator can always intervene; Brandon Strayne fears in Chapter 42 on PDF page 192 that the Mainframe may have moved beyond control, and the Epilogue proves that the command team cannot prevent an assassination prepared inside the Game
- ❌ Do not let entertainment value override a participant's safety; viewers celebrate Zack's number-one finish while Zack's body lies in a lower-level room kept alive by machines, per the Epilogue on PDF pages 277-278

#### Keep In Mind

- A participant remembers pain and joy experienced during a play after the participant exits, as a Game fan concedes in the Chapter 50 interview on PDF page 225; simulated experience produces real memory and real emotional consequence
- Addiction to a system is a structural risk, not a personal weakness alone; Chapter 46 on PDF page 210 states that Tygon mandates Game Days off work per year so employees can follow the Game
- Eternals interfering in a player's life, including Carl targeting elderly people in Chapter 30, show that a system can contain participants whom other participants cannot identify in advance; a reader entering a system with participants the reader cannot identify must write safety rules under Lesson 9 Steps 1 to 3 instead of assuming every participant follows published rules

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Never attempt to hack, exploit, or secretly alter a system that holds real stakes for any participant; Chapter 20 on PDF page 93 states the fictional punishment as death, and real jurisdictions impose criminal penalties, civil liability, and permanent bans for unauthorised access
- Keep real identity, home address, financial credentials, and medical information separated from avatar identity and public audience channels; Lilith's blocked interviews in Chapter 12 show that an unnamed actor can suppress a player's publicity, so every reader must treat public information about the reader as information that a competitor or a blocker can use, including a blocker whose name the reader does not know, as in the Chapter 12 account on PDF pages 52-53
- Treat any weapon, tool, or exploit that can harm a real person through a simulated environment as a lethal risk requiring immediate reporting to authorities; a Sever Spike is presented on PDF pages 145-147 as a weapon that severs a player from the Game permanently (Glossary / Index entry: Sever Spike), and delay in reporting a comparable real threat endangers the target
- Agree in advance who may authorise stopping life-sustaining or high-stakes participation, record the agreement in writing, and store copies with the named authority and with a family member; the Epilogue leaves Brandon Strayne to decide alone in a room with Zack's body, and Lesson 9 exists so a real reader never improvises a comparable decision alone

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** The reader treats a high-stakes system as harmless entertainment because the interface presents avatars, scores, and playful language
  **Solution:** The reader completes Step 1 and reads Column B aloud to the named authority from Step 2 before entry; entry proceeds only when both the reader and the authority accept Column B in writing
- **Problem:** The reader sets an exit rule but keeps sole control of credentials, accounts, and equipment, so the reader overrides the exit rule during an emotional moment
  **Solution:** The reader applies Step 2 fully and transfers enforcement power to the named authority, including credential custody where the system allows credential custody
- **Problem:** The reader assumes the operator of a system will detect every attack and protect every participant, so the reader installs no personal monitoring
  **Solution:** The reader applies Step 3, arranges monitoring outside the operator and outside the reader's sole control, and reviews the monitor's unedited report on a fixed schedule
