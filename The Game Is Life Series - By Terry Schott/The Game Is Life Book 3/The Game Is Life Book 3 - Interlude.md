# Interlude - Brandon

## Overview

*Interlude - Brandon* is a science-fiction novel by Terry Schott, copyright 2012 and edited by Alan Seeger. The novel tells how Brandon Strayne, an orphaned boy raised in the South Western Children's Centre, becomes a Gamer — a player who enters General Donovan's virtual-reality (VR) Sim — and the leader of a five-member Hand in that Sim, completes a thirty-year simulated lifetime in the Facility Sim and a second thirty-year lifetime inside Tygon 1.0, and accepts responsibility for preventing the extinction of the human species in the physical world. The Epilogue reveals that Brandon Strayne spent twenty-eight years inside Tygon building the system called the Game, and that Trew, a man Brandon Strayne selected before Brandon Strayne died, must continue Brandon Strayne's plan.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When a team cannot tell whether the team is operating in a test environment, a training environment, or the live environment, and an action taken in the wrong environment would cause real harm.
- When a leader must decide how to treat simulated people, non-player characters (NPCs), junior colleagues, or any person whom a system labels as less important than the decision-maker.
- When a team must pool points, money, time, or equipment so that every team member advances, instead of allowing the strongest member to advance alone.
- When a powerful person offers a trainee a tool, a game, or a training program and the powerful person controls the exit from the tool, the game, or the program.
- When a worker suspects that a task described as simulated, fictional, or harmless is producing real effects on real people outside the simulation.
- When a leader carries a long plan alone, works under sustained pressure, and must prepare a named successor who can continue the plan if the leader dies or becomes unable to work.

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Name the World You Are In Before You Act in the World

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Alan Cannot Tell Whether the Hand Is In or Out of the Sim at Breakfast (Chapter 32, PDF page 98)

Alan tells Brandon Strayne at breakfast that the repeated movement into the Sim and out of the Sim has confused Alan. Alan states the confusion in specific terms: the Gamers enter the Sim, leave the Sim, fall asleep inside the Sim, and wake outside the Sim. Alan adds that food inside the Sim tastes identical to food in the physical world. Brandon Strayne answers that General Donovan's staff blur the boundary on purpose. The scene establishes the risk that Lesson 1 addresses: a person who cannot name the current world cannot predict the consequence of an action taken in the current world.

##### Example 2: Brandon Strayne's Hand Votes Aloud on Sim or Dream After the Thirty-Year Sim (Chapter 52, PDF page 160)

After Brandon Strayne's Hand returns from a thirty-year lifetime inside the Sim, the five members enter Brandon Strayne's room and search the room for listening devices and cameras, with Kay, Alan, Tony, and Easton each searching an assigned area. When every member raises a thumb to confirm a completed search, Brandon Strayne asks one question: "Are we in the Sim, or the Dream?" All five members answer "The Dream" together. Brandon Strayne closes Brandon Strayne's eyes, breathes deeply, opens Brandon Strayne's eyes, and confirms the answer. The group performs the identification as a spoken ritual before the group discusses the thirty-year lifetime.

#### Step 1: Stop Before the First Consequential Action

Halt the team before any member fires a weapon, signs a form, spends pooled points, deletes a record, or gives an order. State the halt out loud so that every team member hears the halt.

#### Step 2: Ask the Identification Question in a Fixed Wording

Ask the exact question Brandon Strayne asks in Chapter 52: "Are we in the Sim, or the Dream?" In work outside the novel, replace the two named options with the two named environments that the team might occupy, for example "the training copy" and "the live system." Require every member to answer aloud with one named environment, not with a nod.

#### Step 3: Cross-Check the Answer Against Two Physical Tests

Test one: inspect a detail that differs between the two environments, in the way that Brandon Strayne's Hand searches a room for listening devices before speaking freely. Test two: compare a sensory detail against a known baseline, in the way that Alan compares the taste of Sim food against the taste of physical-world food. Record both test results before the team proceeds.

#### Step 4: Act Only at the Level of Harm That the Confirmed World Allows

If the confirmed world is a simulation with reversible consequences, the team may experiment. If the confirmed world is the physical world, or if the team cannot confirm the world, the team must treat every target as a real person and every consequence as permanent until a member confirms the world.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Speak the identification answer aloud as a group, as Brandon Strayne's Hand answers "The Dream" together in Chapter 52.
- ✅ Assign each member a named check, as Kay, Alan, Tony, and Easton each search an assigned area of Brandon Strayne's room in Chapter 52.
- ✅ Treat a blurred boundary as a deliberate act by the system's controller, as Brandon Strayne warns Alan in Chapter 32 that the controllers blur Sim and reality on purpose.
- ❌ Don't rely on how real an environment feels; Alan reports in Chapter 32 that Sim food tastes the same as physical-world food, so feeling cannot separate the two worlds.
- ❌ Don't let the most senior person answer alone; the Blurr works by placing a subject in the Sim without the subject's knowledge (Glossary: Blurr), so a group answer spoken by every member under Step 2 is the check that no member was placed in the Sim alone and unaware.

#### Keep In Mind

- General Donovan's staff gain control when Gamers lose track of the current world; Cooper explains the Blurr to Brandon Strayne in Chapter 43 by comparing blurred vision, in which a viewer cannot tell friend from enemy or armed person from unarmed person at a distance.
- The identification ritual is one spoken question from Brandon Strayne and one spoken group answer from the five members (Chapter 52). The cost of one action taken in the wrong world is permanent, as Carl's story in Lesson 8 proves.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Never accept an environment label from the person who profits from the confusion. General Donovan profits when Gamers cannot tell the Sim from the physical world, because confused Gamers obey orders without asking which world an order affects.
- If a helmet, headset, or access device cannot be removed safely, treat removal as a medical emergency, not as a technical fault. Madame President — a speaker whom the novel identifies only by that title — tells General Donovan in Chapter 57 that removing a VR helmet from a playing person kills the person on the spot.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** A team member says "this world feels real" and the team treats the statement as identification.
  **Solution:** Replace the feeling with the two physical tests in Step 3 and require the spoken group answer in Step 2 before any member acts.
- **Problem:** A controller moves a team between environments during sleep, so members wake in a different world from the world in which members fell asleep.
  **Solution:** Run Step 2 and Step 3 at every waking, at every entry, and at every exit, not once at the start of a project; the Blurr method described to the General works precisely by moving a sleeping subject into the Sim without the subject's knowledge.

### Lesson 2: Treat Every Person in a Shared World as a Real Person

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Brandon Strayne Settles the NPC Question Before the Thirty-Year Tygon 1.0 Sim (Chapter 65, PDF page 194)

Before twelve Gamers enter Tygon 1.0 for thirty years, Brandon Strayne leads a mess-hall discussion on how the Gamers will treat the citizens of Tygon. Kay argues that the Gamers must treat Tygon citizens as the Gamers treat each other, because avatar shape, thought, and form match. Carl argues that Tygon citizens "aren't real." Alan answers Carl by naming Gamers who died during transfer out of a Sim and by asking whether a dead Gamer was unreal. Brandon Strayne closes the discussion with a rule: the group will agree that everything in the simulation is real, because the Gamers feel the simulation, affect the simulation, and live with the repercussions of each choice, and the group will value the citizens of Tygon as real entities.

##### Example 2: Cooper Calls Tygon Citizens "Empty" in the Bookstore and Easton Answers Cooper (Chapter 56, PDF page 171)

In a bookstore inside Tygon, Cooper grabs a book from Alan's hands and states that Cooper cannot care about NPCs because NPCs are "empty" and "aren't real." Easton replies that Facility Gamers have lived entire lives inside Facilities, yet Tygon citizens seem identical to real people from the Gamers' limited experience. Cooper's dismissal and Easton's correction occur in one conversation, and the correction gives the reader the test that Lesson 2 uses: if a person's behaviour is indistinguishable from a real person's behaviour, treat the person as real.

#### Step 1: State the Treatment Rule Before Entering the Shared World

Hold the discussion before entry, not after the first harmful act. Brandon Strayne holds the mess-hall discussion before the twelve Gamers enter Tygon 1.0, and Brandon Strayne requires an explicit agreement from the group.

#### Step 2: Apply the Indistinguishability Test to Every Disputed Person

Ask: does the disputed person speak, work, form relationships, suffer loss, and react to choices in the same manner as an undisputed real person? Kay's formulation in Chapter 65 is the test: shape, thought, and form match. If the answer is yes, classify the person as real for every decision that affects the person.

#### Step 3: Price Harm to a Disputed Person at the Same Cost as Harm to an Undisputed Person

When a plan predicts that one million Tygon citizens might die or suffer, record the prediction as one million deaths or one million cases of suffering in the plan's cost column. Never record the prediction as a score change, a resource change, or a reset.

#### Step 4: Let the Person Who Objects to the Rule Name a Specific Difference

If a team member claims that a disputed person is not real, require the member to name one observable difference between the disputed person and an undisputed person. Carl names no observable difference in Chapter 65; Carl repeats the label "not real." A repeated label without a named difference does not change the classification from Step 2.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Decide the treatment rule as a group and record the agreement, as Brandon Strayne asks "Does anyone disagree?" and closes with a group choice in Chapter 65.
- ✅ Use the repercussions test: an action that produces lasting consequences for the actor and for the person acted upon is a real action between real persons.
- ✅ Extend the rule to persons whom a system labels as background, support, or non-player; Cooper's bookstore dismissal in Chapter 56 targets exactly the persons whom the label excludes.
- ❌ Don't accept rank, origin, or code as proof that a person matters less; Tygon citizens originate as mainframe programs and Brandon Strayne still rules that the Gamers will value Tygon citizens as real entities.
- ❌ Don't postpone the decision until a crisis; Brandon Strayne settles the rule at a meal before entry, when no emergency pressures the group.

#### Keep In Mind

- Thorn makes the same argument to Trew in the Epilogue: Thorn states that the NPCs in Brandon Strayne's life story did not look empty on video, and that a world is real for the persons who live in the world.
- The rule protects the decision-maker as well as the disputed person. A Gamer who practises dismissal inside a simulation carries the dismissal habit into the physical world, which is the outcome General Donovan wants from the Sim project.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- A superior who instructs a subordinate to treat a class of persons as unreal is preparing the subordinate to harm the class without hesitation. Treat such an instruction as a warning sign about the superior, and verify the superior's other instructions before obeying the superior again.
- Never use Lesson 2 to claim that a simulated person can consent to real-world harm; the rule raises the protection owed to simulated persons to the physical-world standard, and the rule never lowers physical-world protection.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** A team member uses the word "NPC" as a complete argument that a person does not count.
  **Solution:** Run Step 4. Require one observable difference. If the member names none, the classification from Step 2 stands and the plan prices harm under Step 3.
- **Problem:** A leader settles the treatment rule privately and never tells the team, so individual members improvise under pressure.
  **Solution:** Copy Brandon Strayne's Chapter 65 method: one group discussion, one spoken rule, one recorded agreement, completed before entry into the shared world.

### Lesson 3: Build a Hand of Five and a Network Beyond the Hand

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Brandon Strayne's Hand of Five Gamers (Chapters 6 to 65)

Brandon Strayne's Hand consists of five named Gamers: Brandon Strayne as leader, Kay, Alan, Easton, and Tony. The five train together from childhood in the Centre, compete as a unit in the Sim, pool points together in Chapter 40, debrief a completed thirty-year Sim together in Chapter 52, and sit among the twelve Gamers whom Thorn gathers in Chapter 65. Each member has observed the other four members in training, in competition, and in debriefs across two simulated lifetimes.

##### Example 2: Cooper Teaches Brandon Strayne the Difference Between a Team and a Network (Chapter 62, PDF pages 184 to 186)

Brandon Strayne asks Cooper to add Carl to the thirty-year Sim group as a controlled team member. Cooper refuses the framing and states the network rule in one sentence pair: a team is guided and controlled by the team's members; a network is accepted for what the network is, and tapped when the situation allows. Cooper adds that Brandon Strayne has already built a network among Facility Gamers across thirteen years of Sim play. Cooper then accepts Brandon Strayne's list of names and promises to obtain the requested people, because Brandon Strayne asks through the network rather than by command.

#### Step 1: Fix the Core Group at Five Named Members

Name the leader and the four other members. Brandon Strayne's Hand names Kay, Alan, Easton, and Tony. A core group larger than five dilutes the mutual knowledge that lets members answer the Lesson 1 identification question for one another.

#### Step 2: Train the Core Group Together on Shared Tasks

Enter training, competition, and rest as one unit. Brandon Strayne's Hand plays Sim scenarios together, eats together, and debriefs together, so each member observes the other members under pressure before a crisis requires trust.

#### Step 3: Build the Network by Repeated Fair Dealing, Not by Recruitment Orders

Help other groups without demanding membership. Brandon Strayne builds the Facility network across thirteen years of Sim play by grouping with other teams to test larger areas and systems, as Tony describes in Chapter 56. Record which person can provide which skill, and update the record after each joint task.

#### Step 4: Request Through the Network and Accept the Network's Terms

When a situation requires a person outside the Hand, ask the person or ask a connector such as Cooper, state the need and the deadline, and accept refusal without punishment. Cooper grants Brandon Strayne's request in Chapter 62 because Brandon Strayne accepts Cooper's correction about control.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Keep the Hand at five members and keep membership stable across projects, as Brandon Strayne's Hand stays intact from childhood trials to the second thirty-year Sim.
- ✅ Learn the network rule verbatim from Cooper: a network is accepted for what the network is, and tapped when the situation allows.
- ✅ Maintain one connector: Cooper connects Brandon Strayne to Elite leaders, to Carl, and to Thorn.
- ❌ Don't try to control a network member as a team member; Cooper identifies control as the error in Brandon Strayne's request about Carl.
- ❌ Don't build the network during a crisis; Brandon Strayne's network exists before the extinction crisis because Brandon Strayne built the network during thirteen years of ordinary play.

#### Keep In Mind

- A Hand and a network serve different functions. The Hand provides trust under pressure; the network provides reach, information, and specialist skills that five people cannot hold.
- The General also builds a network, and the General's network is built on fear: the General fires aide Brad and hunts Brad in Chapter 2. Lesson 3 builds reach by fair dealing, which produces contacts who volunteer information; the General's method produces contacts such as Brad who sell the General's secrets to survive.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Vet a network contact before sharing a secret with the contact. Brad shares top-secret facts with reporters, politicians, and military leaders in Chapter 3 to protect Brad's life; a contact under threat will trade a secret for safety.
- Share the minimum fact that a network contact needs for the requested task. Brandon Strayne gives Cooper a list of names in Chapter 62; Brandon Strayne does not give Cooper the full extinction plan.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** A leader enlarges the core group whenever a new skill appears, until no member knows the other members well enough to detect confusion, fear, or Blurr in a teammate.
  **Solution:** Keep the Hand at five named members and obtain the new skill through the network under Step 4.
- **Problem:** A leader issues an order to a network contact, the contact refuses, and the leader records the contact as disloyal.
  **Solution:** Re-read Cooper's rule in Chapter 62. A refusal from a network contact is information about timing and fit, not disloyalty; ask a different contact or change the timing of the request.

### Lesson 4: Pool Rewards and Buy the Advancement Only When Every Member Can Afford the Advancement

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Brandon Strayne's Hand Pools Points for the Expensive Perk (Chapter 40, PDF page 122)

Brandon Strayne announces to Kay, Alan, Easton, and Tony that the Hand finally holds enough points for every member. Alan asks, "For all of us?" Brandon Strayne confirms the deal in direct words: no member buys the perk until all five members can afford the perk. Brandon Strayne adds that no other crew in the Sim has achieved the shared purchase, because other crews let individual members buy alone.

##### Example 2: The Sim Point System Rewards Individual Play Unless a Hand Chooses Otherwise (Chapter 40, PDF page 122)

Thorn introduced the point system at the first anniversary of the Sim. From the anniversary onward, each player earned points by playing games, and each player could spend points on goods and programs inside the Sim, including the solo flying program that Brandon Strayne later buys in Chapter 44. The system pays the individual. Brandon Strayne's Hand converts an individual payment system into a shared advancement system by a private agreement among five members, without any rule change from Thorn.

#### Step 1: Name the Shared Advancement and the Full Cost for All Five Members

Write down the item, the cost for one member, and the cost multiplied by the number of Hand members. Brandon Strayne's announcement in Chapter 40 reports the multiplied total, not the single-member cost.

#### Step 2: Record the Agreement in One Sentence That Every Member Repeats

Use Brandon Strayne's sentence structure: "No one buys the named perk until all five members of the Hand can afford the named perk." Require each member to repeat the sentence aloud when the agreement is made.

#### Step 3: Track the Pool Openly at Every Meeting

Report the pool total and the remaining gap at every meeting of the Hand. Alan's question "For all of us?" shows that members check the shared condition; an open total lets every member verify the condition without asking the leader privately.

#### Step 4: Buy for All Five Members in One Session

Complete the purchase for every member together, so that no member trains with the new capability while another member waits. A capability gap inside a five-member Hand creates a status gap, and a status gap breaks the mutual trust that Lesson 3 requires.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Apply the pooling rule to training courses, tools, licences, and rest time, not only to game points; the mechanism is the agreement, not the currency.
- ✅ Let the leader announce the pool total, as Brandon Strayne announces the total to the Hand in Brandon Strayne's room in Chapter 40.
- ✅ Celebrate the shared purchase as a crew first; Brandon Strayne notes that no other crew has achieved the shared purchase, and the distinction builds Hand identity.
- ❌ Don't make an exception for the leader; a leader who buys early teaches the Hand that the agreement binds only junior members.
- ❌ Don't pool without a named item and a named total; a pool collected for no named item takes points from members for a purchase the members never agreed to.

#### Keep In Mind

- Pooling delays the first purchase. Brandon Strayne's Hand accepts the delay because the perk's value multiplies when all five members hold the perk and can train together.
- The rule requires a small, stable group. Lesson 3 fixes the Hand at five members; pooling across a large network fails because no member can verify the contributions of distant members.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Keep the pool record where every member can read the record. A privately held record lets a leader divert pooled points without detection.
- Never pool a resource that a member needs for survival, medical care, or exit from a dangerous environment. The Chapter 40 pool covers a discretionary perk; survival resources stay under individual control.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** One member spends points alone on a tempting solo program, and the pool for the shared item never recovers.
  **Solution:** Restate the Step 2 sentence at the start of each spending period, require every member to report a solo purchase to the Hand before making the purchase, and count each reported solo purchase against the pool gap at the next Step 3 report.
- **Problem:** Members contribute unequal amounts and the Hand argues about fairness at purchase time.
  **Solution:** Agree at Step 2 whether contributions must be equal or proportional, write the chosen basis beside the named total, and review the basis at each Step 3 report.

### Lesson 5: Learn a System's Hidden Rules by Testing the System, Then Use the Tested Rules

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Brandon Strayne's Hand Tests the Snow Before the Game Starts and Wins the Race to the Woods (Chapters 33 and 34, PDF pages 101 and 104)

In a snow scenario inside the Sim, both Hands enter through a silvery white doorway into deep snow between stone cliffs. Before the game starts, Brandon Strayne tests the snow away from the path: Brandon Strayne sinks to Brandon Strayne's knees four steps from the path, and Tony sinks to Tony's waist beside Brandon Strayne. When the game starts and the snow under the Hand softens to chest depth, the Hand moves toward the sides, where each member is barely submerged, and races to the woods, where the snow is less deep. Chapter 34 states the result directly: Brandon Strayne's Hand raced to the woods and secured positions ahead of the opposing group. Kay and Brandon Strayne climb into trees; Alan, Tony, and Easton hold ground positions. The opposing group reaches firing distance already tired from the learning cycle and from the cold, and Tony and Alan tag the opposing members with plastic-bullet fire.

##### Example 2: Harold Reports the Time-Stretch Discovery to Thorn, and Thorn Hides the Report While Thorn Decides (Chapter 30, PDF page 92)

Harold, who reports system discoveries to Thorn, reports to Thorn that the time difference between the Sim and physical reality can be stretched. Harold names the trade-off in the same report: as the time difference grows, two-way communication between the Sim and physical reality becomes impeded or eliminated. Thorn thanks Harold, instructs Harold to keep the discovery quiet, and keeps the option unused until Thorn understands how and when to use the option. The discovery later makes the thirty-year Sims possible: Gamers live thirty years inside while three months pass outside.

#### Step 1: Identify One Rule That the System Does Not State

Pick one unstated behaviour, such as snow depth, point pricing, time ratio, or respawn cost. Write the behaviour as a question with a measurable answer, for example: "How much speed does a Gamer lose at each snow depth?"

#### Step 2: Test the Behaviour in a Low-Cost Trial

Run the smallest trial that produces a measurement. Brandon Strayne's Hand tests snow movement at the start of the scenario, before the opposing group arrives, when a failed test costs seconds rather than the match.

#### Step 3: Convert the Measurement into a Team Procedure Before the Contest Starts

State the procedure in one or two sentences that every member can repeat, assign positions that exploit the procedure, and begin the contest from the exploited position. Chapter 34 shows the conversion: tested snow knowledge becomes tree positions for Kay and Brandon Strayne and ground positions for Alan, Tony, and Easton.

#### Step 4: Record the Trade-Off That Travels with Every Advantage

Write the cost beside the advantage, as Harold writes loss of two-way communication beside the stretched time ratio. A team that records only the advantage will use the advantage in a situation where the cost defeats the team.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Test early in a scenario, while the cost of failure is low; Brandon Strayne's Hand learns the snow rule before contact with the opposing group.
- ✅ Share a tested rule with the whole Hand immediately; a rule held by one member dies with that member's avatar.
- ✅ Prefer a rule that changes position or timing over a rule that changes equipment; the snow rule wins a position without any purchase.
- ❌ Don't announce a tested rule to the system's controller before the team has used the rule; Thorn hides Harold's time discovery from General Donovan while Thorn decides, and premature disclosure invites the controller to patch the rule.
- ❌ Don't assume a rule from a different scenario still applies; Centre children meet real weather for the first time in the snow scenario, and prior indoor experience misleads the opposing group.

#### Keep In Mind

- Three systems in the novel each hide one rule that changes how the system must be used: the Sim hides the time ratio, the point system hides the shared-purchase possibility, and the Dream hides the rule that a skill practised in the Dream, such as flight, can be summoned in the Sim through the Glow.
- A tested rule is evidence; an untested belief is a guess. Brandon Strayne's Hand wins the snow scenario on evidence, and the opposing group loses the snow scenario on an untested assumption that walking through deep snow is the only method of crossing snow.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Testing a hidden rule in a live system can trigger alarms or sanctions from the controller. Test in a training scenario first, as the Gamers test inside Sim scenarios under Thorn's observation, before testing in any environment where a sanction harms a real person.
- Keep a written test record outside the system being tested. General Donovan controls the Sim's records; a record held only inside the Sim can be altered or deleted by the controller.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** A team discovers a useful rule and keeps exploiting the rule after the controller patches the rule, so the team's procedure fails in the decisive contest.
  **Solution:** Re-run the Step 2 low-cost trial at the start of every new scenario and confirm the measurement before the team commits to the Step 3 procedure.
- **Problem:** A discoverer reports an advantage to a superior without the trade-off, and the superior orders large-scale use that triggers the hidden cost.
  **Solution:** Follow Harold's Chapter 30 format: one report that names the advantage and the specific cost in adjacent sentences.

### Lesson 6: Keep a Real Cost on Failure So That Practice Stays Honest

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Thorn Explains to Cooper Why Sim Death Hurts (Chapter 35, PDF page 107)

Cooper reports fifteen avatar deaths in a complex exercise, after three earlier attempts in which all forty participants died. Cooper tells Thorn that Sim death is extremely painful and traumatic, and Cooper asks why Thorn did not make Sim death less realistic. Thorn answers with the design rule: everything has a price, and if ejection from the Sim cost nothing, individuals would play in an entirely different way. Thorn also corrects Cooper's word choice, stating that the dead are avatars, while Cooper insists that the deaths are tragic because Cooper has died twice inside the Sim.

##### Example 2: The General Counts Children as a Renewable Training Resource (Chapter 42, PDF page 128)

In year three of the Sim, General Donovan orders the integration of more than fifteen hundred Centre students with three thousand enlisted adults, so that children face adults in the Sim. Thorn asks whether integration is premature. The General rejects the concern and cites the years both groups have spent growing accustomed to VR technology. The scene shows the failure mode that Lesson 6 forbids: a controller who pays no cost for participant deaths treats participant deaths as a free input and raises the danger level without evidence that participants are ready.

#### Step 1: Name the Cost That a Failure Imposes Before Training Begins

Write the cost in concrete terms: pain, lost time, lost points, a repeated module, or a formal review. Thorn's design names pain and trauma as the cost of avatar death.

#### Step 2: Apply the Cost to Every Participant, Including the Leader

Brandon Strayne's Hand plays under the same death cost as every other Hand. A leader exempt from the cost will order risks that a leader who pays the cost would refuse.

#### Step 3: Review Every Death or Failure in a Debrief That Names the Decision That Caused the Failure

Cooper's Chapter 35 report counts fifteen deaths and compares the count against three earlier total wipes. Copy the format: state the count, state the comparison baseline, and name the decision that the team will change.

#### Step 4: Stop Raising Difficulty When the Cost Count Rises Without a Matching Skill Gain

If deaths rise across three attempts and measured skill does not rise, freeze the difficulty level and retrain the failed skill. The General's Chapter 42 integration order violates Step 4 by raising danger on a schedule rather than on measured readiness.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Keep the cost proportional: name the cost in Step 1's terms (pain, lost time, lost points, a repeated module, or a formal review), count deaths and failures at every Step 3 debrief, and apply Step 4 — if the count rises without a matching skill gain, the cost or the difficulty is out of proportion. Thorn defends painful Sim death as the price that changes play behaviour, not as entertainment.
- ✅ Count costs openly, as Cooper counts fifteen deaths in front of Thorn.
- ✅ Separate the cost review from blame; the Step 3 debrief names a decision, not a scapegoat.
- ❌ Don't remove the cost to make training pleasant; Thorn warns Cooper that cost-free ejection changes how individuals play, and cost-free play teaches habits that kill in the physical world.
- ❌ Don't let a person who pays no cost set the difficulty for persons who pay the full cost; in Chapter 42 General Donovan orders the integration of Centre students and enlisted adults without entering the Sim as a contestant in the contest he orders.

#### Keep In Mind

- Cost shapes behaviour faster than instruction. The Gamers plan routes, conserve resources, and protect teammates because avatar death hurts; a lecture on caution produces none of the three behaviours.
- A cost can be real without being cruel. Points, time, rank, and repeated training all impose cost; pain is the novel's extreme version and Lesson 6 does not require pain in physical-world training.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Never impose a physical cost that can injure a participant in physical-world training. Use simulated, financial, temporal, or reputational costs outside the novel's Sim.
- Monitor participants for trauma after repeated costly failures. Cooper states plainly that Sim death is traumatic; a training system that ignores trauma reporting will lose participants and conceal the system's true cost.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** A training program removes all failure costs to raise participation numbers, and graduates enter real work with untested judgement.
  **Solution:** Restore a Step 1 cost, publish the cost before enrolment, and measure graduate performance against the pre-change baseline.
- **Problem:** A controller raises difficulty on a schedule rather than on measured readiness, as the General integrates children and adults in Chapter 42, and injuries or deaths follow.
  **Solution:** Require the Step 4 readiness evidence, signed by the trainer who observes the participants, before any difficulty increase takes effect.

### Lesson 7: Verify the Exit Before You Enter a System That Another Person Controls

#### Examples

##### Example 1: General Donovan Is Locked in the White Entry Room (Chapters 53 and 55, PDF pages 163 and 167)

General Donovan puts on a VR helmet, counts backwards from ten, and opens the General's eyes in an empty white room with a locked door. The General cannot open the door, returns to the centre of the room, and runs the exit sequence. Outside, the General confronts Thorn: the door was locked, and the General accuses Thorn of blocking the General's entry into the Sim. Thorn states that the white room is the standard entrance area and that a user opens the door and walks a hallway to a programmed destination. The exchange proves that General Donovan entered a subordinate's system without verifying entry, movement, or exit in advance.

##### Example 2: Removing a Helmet Kills the Player, and Helmets Begin to Outlast the Player (Chapters 57 and 70, PDF pages 173 and 209)

Madame President tells General Donovan that unhooking a VR helmet while a person plays Tygon 3.0 kills the person on the spot. In Chapter 70, Cooper reports to Thorn in the medical zone, ten levels below the Game Facility, that helmets first powered down after roughly twelve hours, and that helmets later keep a charge until the wearer dies. Thorn states Thorn's theory: the programming inside the helmet has evolved and learned to recharge the helmet. A device whose exit behaviour changes after deployment is a device whose exit must be re-verified continuously, not once.

#### Step 1: Test Entry, Movement, and Exit on a Disposable Account Before Any Person Enters

Run one full cycle with a test avatar: enter, move to the destination, exit by the normal sequence, and record the time for each stage. General Donovan's locked-door failure would have appeared in a test cycle that included opening the entry-room door.

#### Step 2: Test the Emergency Exit Separately from the Normal Exit

Trigger the emergency method, such as removing the helmet or cutting power, on a test setup with no person attached, and record the result. If the emergency method harms a wearer, publish the harm in the entry briefing that every participant signs.

#### Step 3: Re-Verify Both Exits After Every System Update

Compare the post-update exit test against the Step 1 record. The helmet change between the twelve-hour power limit and the charge-until-death behaviour in Chapter 70 is the model: an exit that passed an early test can fail a later test without any announcement.

#### Step 4: Refuse Entry When the Controller Cannot Demonstrate an Exit

State the refusal in one sentence: "No member of this Hand enters until the controller demonstrates one normal exit and one emergency exit in front of two witnesses." Name the two witnesses before the demonstration.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Keep the exit test record outside the controlled system, so the controller cannot alter the record.
- ✅ Count backwards entry rituals, entry rooms, and door states as part of the system to test; the General's failure was a locked door in the first room, not a deep-system fault.
- ✅ Brief every participant on the emergency-exit harm before the participant's first entry, in plain words that name the harm.
- ❌ Don't accept power or rank as a substitute for a test; General Donovan outranks Thorn and still sits locked in Thorn's white room.
- ❌ Don't treat a grin, a green light, or a status icon as proof of a healthy state; in Chapter 64, Thorn detects that the General is wrongly immersed because the General's mouth does not form the grin that correct immersion produces.

#### Keep In Mind

- Thorn controls General Donovan's door in Chapter 55, and Thorn later holds the General inside the Sim for torture in Chapter 68, after Thorn admits the General into the Sim in Chapter 66; the person who controls the exit decides whether the persons inside the system can leave the system.
- Exit verification is a recurring duty, not a launch duty. Tygon 3.0 helmets change behaviour months after millions of citizens begin playing.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Assume that a controller who blocks oversight entry will also block emergency extraction. Thorn hides the VR headset's existence from the General in Chapter 38 by reporting that no functioning headset unit exists, two years into the project.
- Keep an independent power cutoff for any immersive system, held by a person outside the controller's chain of command, and test the cutoff under Step 2 conditions.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** A team verifies the normal exit once at launch and never tests the emergency exit, so the first emergency becomes an uncontrolled experiment on a person.
  **Solution:** Run Step 2 before first entry and repeat Step 3 after every update, with the results signed by the two witnesses named in Step 4.
- **Problem:** A senior leader demands entry without testing, citing urgency, and the team waives the exit checks.
  **Solution:** Offer the leader the disposable-account test from Step 1, and record any refusal to wait for the test in the entry log.

### Lesson 8: Refuse to Perform Real Harm That a Superior Labels as a Game

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Carl Kills a Woman in a House While Believing the Woman Is a Sim Target (Chapter 61, PDF page 182)

Carl enters a house during a repeated scenario that Carl has played at least thirty-three times. Carl passes a small girl of five or six years who watches a children's program in a pink bedroom, finds the target in a back room, reads the logo "Tygon 3.0, Thorn Inc." on the woman's VR helmet, unsheathes a long blade, and cuts the woman's throat. The woman's blood pools on clean sheets. Carl wipes the blade on a hallway towel and continues searching the remaining rooms. Carl performs the killing as a ritual task inside a scenario that Carl believes the computer is glitching.

##### Example 2: Cooper Names the Deception to Brandon Strayne (Chapter 62, PDF page 185)

Cooper explains Carl's missions to Brandon Strayne in one direct statement: Carl thinks Carl is inside the Sim killing computer-generated NPCs for points, when in fact Carl is in the real world killing people whom General Donovan wants dead. Cooper adds the project's purpose: the Sim project was intended to turn Gamers into better killing machines for the General, so that the General could solidify control over the real world. Brandon Strayne repeats the conclusion back to Cooper, and Cooper confirms the conclusion without qualification.

#### Step 1: Demand Proof of the Target's Status Before Any Harmful Task

Ask the assigning superior for the evidence that a target is simulated: the scenario identifier, the controller's name, and the reset method that restores the target. Carl receives none of the three items across thirty-three repetitions of the house scenario.

#### Step 2: Treat Repetition Without Variation as a Warning Sign, Not as Reassurance

Carl notes that the houses and furniture differ on each visit while the mission stays identical, and Carl dismisses the variation as unimportant. Invert Carl's response: list every detail that changes between repetitions, and require the superior to explain each change before the next repetition.

#### Step 3: Check One Detail Against an Outside Source

Verify one fact through a channel that the assigning superior does not control, such as a public record, a second superior, or a teammate outside the mission chain. Carl reads the helmet logo and wonders whether Thorn Inc. connects to Mr. Thorn, then dismisses the question; Step 3 forbids the dismissal.

#### Step 4: Stop Work and Report the Suspicion Through a Second Channel

When proof from Step 1 is missing or an outside check from Step 3 contradicts the superior, halt the task, preserve every order and log, and report the suspicion to an authority outside the assigning superior's chain. A halt protects the worker and the next target; continued obedience protects only the superior.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Ask "who benefits from this target's death?" for every harmful task; Cooper's Chapter 62 answer names General Donovan as the beneficiary of Carl's missions.
- ✅ Keep a personal mission log with dates, addresses, and target descriptions, stored outside the superior's system.
- ✅ Discuss suspicions with a trusted peer before the next task; Brandon Strayne and Cooper hold the Chapter 62 conversation as peers, and the conversation converts a rumour into a confirmed fact.
- ❌ Don't let points, rank, or praise substitute for proof; Carl earns standing as a key member of Brandon Strayne's network while performing real killings.
- ❌ Don't dismiss an anomaly because noticing the anomaly delays the task; Carl calls the logo check a ritual that delays "the worst part of the mission" and performs the killing anyway.

#### Keep In Mind

- Deception of the Carl type requires three conditions that a worker can detect: an isolated worker, a repeated task, and a label that dehumanizes the target. Lesson 2 removes the label, and Lesson 3 removes the isolation.
- Brandon Strayne responds to the revelation by recruiting Carl into the thirty-year Sim group, removing Carl from the General's mission chain for three months of physical time. Removing a deceived worker from the chain is a valid protective action when direct confrontation would endanger the worker.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Preserve evidence before reporting. Orders, logs, and the Step 2 variation list must exist in a copy that the assigning superior cannot delete, because the superior who runs a deception will destroy the record when the deception is reported.
- Do not warn the assigning superior before the report reaches the outside authority. General Donovan hunts a fired aide within half an hour in Chapter 2; a superior of the General's type acts faster than a worker can flee.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** A worker notices that a "simulated" environment bleeds, smells, and varies like a real place, and the worker explains away each sign to avoid losing status.
  **Solution:** Apply Step 2 mechanically. Write the variation list; three unexplained variations trigger Step 4 automatically, without a judgement call by the worker.
- **Problem:** A worker reports a suspicion only to the assigning superior's deputy, and the deputy returns the report to the superior.
  **Solution:** Use the Step 4 second channel: an authority outside the chain, contacted directly, with the preserved evidence attached.

### Lesson 9: Spend Extra Time Inside a System on Real Competence, Then Spend the Competence Outside the System

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Thorn Sends the Elites into a Second Thirty-Year Sim to Learn Quantum Computing and Design (Chapter 65, PDF page 193)

Thorn tells Brandon Strayne's gathered group that, after the second thirty-year simulation, the Elites will hold the experience Thorn wants the Elites to hold, and that six to nine months will remain to implement actions in the physical world to end the Tygon 3.0 crisis safely. Brandon Strayne states the learning goal in the same conversation: the Elites will live one more lifetime to become adept at quantum computing and designing. The thirty-year cost is justified by a named skill and a named deadline, not by a general claim that experience is valuable.

##### Example 2: Brandon Strayne Refuses to Sell VirtDyne VR Technology and Builds an Education System Instead (Chapters 74 to 76, PDF pages 221 to 227)

Inside Tygon 1.0, eighteen-year-old Brandon Strayne takes control of the failing company VirtDyne, announces working virtual-reality technology, and watches VirtDyne share prices rise from five credits to three hundred credits. Three months later, Brandon Strayne announces that VirtDyne will sell the technology to no business or commercial venture, and that VirtDyne will commit all resources to a new educational system in which children learn while submersed in virtual environments. Investors who expected commercial sales sell shares, and prices drop. Brandon Strayne spends the competence and capital gained inside Tygon on the educational system he announced, and refuses to sell VirtDyne VR technology to any business or commercial venture.

#### Step 1: Name the Skill and the Outside Deadline Before Entering a Long Training Period

Write the skill in specific terms, as Chapter 65 names quantum computing and designing, and write the deadline in specific terms, as Thorn names six to nine months of implementation time after exit. Reject any long training period that names neither item.

#### Step 2: Assign the Training Cohort as a Whole Hand, Not as Isolated Individuals

Send the intact Hand, so that the cohort exits with shared vocabulary and shared trust. Thorn gathers twelve Gamers in Chapter 65, including Brandon Strayne's complete Hand, and obtains a nod from each of the twelve.

#### Step 3: Plan the Outside Application Before Entry, in One Page

Record the first three actions the cohort will take after exit, the resources each action needs, and the person who owns each action. Brandon Strayne's VirtDyne decision shows the standard: a company, a technology, and a chosen application, education, selected in advance of the public announcement.

#### Step 4: Refuse the Highest-Paying Misuse of the New Competence

List the buyers or users who would pay most for the competence and state why the cohort will refuse the top offer. Brandon Strayne refuses all commercial buyers for VirtDyne VR technology because commercial release inside Tygon would repeat the addiction disaster that Tygon 3.0 caused in the physical world.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Convert simulated years into a portable skill with a physical-world test at exit; quantum computing competence can be examined, while "thirty years of experience" cannot.
- ✅ Keep the physical body maintained during long immersion; Thorn notes that Gamers spend three months in a controlled coma on tables during a thirty-year Sim, and muscle atrophy limits session length in the Longevity project discussions.
- ✅ Announce the chosen application publicly, as Brandon Strayne announces the VirtDyne education decision at a press conference, so that investors and employees can align with the decision.
- ❌ Don't enter a long simulation for status or escape; Lohkam's refusal in Chapter 63, in which Lohkam holds a letter opener to Lohkam's own throat rather than begin another thirty-year session, shows the human cost of a lifetime spent without consent to the purpose.
- ❌ Don't sell a dangerous competence to the first buyer; the physical world's Tygon 3.0 launch sold immersion first and counted deaths later.

#### Keep In Mind

- The thirty years a Gamer lives inside a simulation are thirty years of that Gamer's experienced life, although three months pass in the physical world during the same session (Lesson 5, Example 2; Lesson 9, Security & Safety Notes). Lohkam tells Thorn in Chapter 60 that Lohkam cannot do another thirty-year session, and Thorn answers by offering Lohkam a different task. A leader must hold a genuine alternative task for a member who refuses a long session.
- Competence gained inside a system inherits the system's blind spots. Brandon Strayne's cohort studies virtual reality inside a virtual reality; the cohort must test conclusions against physical-world evidence after exit.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Obtain informed consent that names the physical cost: three months in a controlled coma for a thirty-year Sim, plus the recovery period for muscle atrophy. Thorn obtains a nod from each of the twelve Gamers in Chapter 65 after stating the coma cost aloud; copy the practice of stating the cost before requesting consent.
- Guard the sleeping cohort. A person in a controlled coma cannot respond to fire, attack, or equipment failure, so the facility holding the tables requires guards, backup power, and medical staff for the full session.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** A cohort exits a long training period with deep skill and no application plan, and a hostile buyer hires the cohort member by member.
  **Solution:** Complete the Step 3 one-page application plan before entry and review the plan at the exit debrief, before any member negotiates outside employment.
- **Problem:** A member consents to a long session under group pressure and breaks down at entry, as Lohkam breaks down in Chapter 63.
  **Solution:** Offer the alternative-task route that Thorn offers Lohkam in Chapter 60 before the entry date, and record the member's choice without a penalty entry in the member's record.

### Lesson 10: Carry a Long Plan in Precise Steps, Prepare a Named Successor, and Keep Working After the Handover

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Brandon Strayne Alone at 3 AM in the Epilogue (PDF page 241)

One hundred floors above the city, Brandon Strayne cannot sleep. Brandon Strayne counts a fractured lifetime in the text's own terms: thirteen years in the Dream, thirty years in another simulation, and seven years "here," meaning the present-tense world of the Epilogue in which Brandon Strayne stands at the window. Brandon Strayne states the plan's structure aloud: the work requires decades of precise moves, each delicate step leads to the next step, and if enough steps fall out of sync the entire game crumbles and every person in both worlds dies. Brandon Strayne cries, hums a song that Brandon Strayne believes Brandon Strayne's mother sang, then stands, straightens Brandon Strayne's shoulders, and walks to a desk to resume work. The scene shows both halves of Lesson 10: a plan held as an ordered chain of steps, and a leader who grieves in private and returns to the desk.

##### Example 2: Thorn Shows Trew the Record of Brandon Strayne's Life and Hands Over the Plan (Epilogue, PDF pages 242 to 243)

Thorn seats a man before a monitor and shows the man video covering Brandon Strayne's life up to age twenty. Thorn explains the succession rule that Brandon Strayne set: when Brandon Strayne died, a successor would be brought up to speed to move the plan along, and Brandon Strayne instructed Thorn to search out the specific successor and invite the successor to take over from the point where Brandon Strayne stopped. The successor is Trew. Trew protests that Trew is "just a stupid NPC." Sylvia answers Trew with the commission that closes the novel: Trew must help save the world, and, when Trew asks "Save which world?", Sylvia replies, "As many as you can, dear boy. As many as you can."

#### Step 1: Write the Plan as an Ordered Chain of Named Steps

List each step with a name, an owner, and the step that follows. Brandon Strayne's Epilogue formulation is the standard: decades of precise moves, each step leading to the next step, with failure defined as a step falling out of sync with the step that follows it.

#### Step 2: Name One Successor and Record the Name Outside the Plan

Brandon Strayne names Trew and instructs Thorn, a person outside the plan's daily work, to find Trew when Brandon Strayne dies. Record the successor's name with a holder who will survive the leader and who holds no ambition to take the role personally; Thorn holds the instruction for years without claiming the role.

#### Step 3: Prepare a Handover Record That Teaches the Successor the Whole Story

Assemble a record that covers the leader's life, decisions, and reasons, in the form Thorn uses in the Epilogue: a video record of the leader's life that the successor watches during the handover (Example 2). Thorn's video record of Brandon Strayne's life serves as the handover record; the record includes failures and private grief, not only victories, because Trew must understand the cost of the role before accepting the role.

#### Step 4: Give the Successor a Commission in One Sentence and Accept the Successor's Question

State the mission in one sentence, as Sylvia states the mission to Trew. When the successor asks a scope question, answer with the scope itself, in words that name the limit or state that there is none: Sylvia's answer to "Save which world?" — "As many as you can, dear boy. As many as you can." — sets the scope at its maximum, every world Trew can save, and states no smaller limit. A commission that names neither a limit nor an open maximum produces a successor who guesses at scope during the first crisis.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Review the step chain after every completed step and re-sync any step that has drifted, before drift accumulates into the Epilogue's failure condition.
- ✅ Let the leader rest and grieve away from the team, then return to work at a set time; Brandon Strayne allows a couple of minutes of grief and then stands to work.
- ✅ Choose a successor for judgement and persistence, not for origin; Trew is an NPC by birth inside the simulation, and Brandon Strayne selects Trew over every human Elite leader.
- ❌ Don't hold the entire plan in one memory; Brandon Strayne's death would have ended the plan if Brandon Strayne had not recorded instructions with Thorn in advance.
- ❌ Don't hide the role's cost from the successor; Trew watches Brandon Strayne's whole life, including Brandon Strayne's fear and loneliness, before Sylvia issues the commission.

#### Keep In Mind

- A successor who doubts personal worth is not disqualified. Trew's protest, "I'm just a stupid NPC," repeats Carl's Chapter 65 dismissal in reverse; Lesson 2 supplies the answer that Sylvia gives in practice: origin does not decide a person's value; a person's value is decided by what the person does in the work the person is given, as Trew is chosen for the succession in the Epilogue despite Trew's protest.
- The old man in garbage bags who rescues the girl Emily in Chapter 80, wearing gloves of red bottle caps and levitating a knife above the man's palm, first appears in Brandon Strayne's childhood Dream in Chapter 19 as a self-taught flying man who learned the man's abilities without a teacher. Long plans in the novel repeatedly depend on persons who were noticed once, years earlier, and remembered.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Store the succession instruction with at least two holders in separate locations. Thorn is a single holder for Brandon Strayne's instruction, and Thorn is a target for General Donovan throughout the novel; a single holder is a single point of failure.
- Verify the successor's identity in person before delivering the handover record. Thorn seats Trew before the monitor in a controlled room; deliver no record copy to a claimant who arrives without verification.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** A leader writes a step chain that only the leader can interpret, so the successor inherits a list of step names without reasons.
  **Solution:** Attach one sentence of reason to each named step in Step 1, and test the chain by asking the Step 2 holder to explain the chain back to the leader.
- **Problem:** A leader delays naming a successor because naming a successor feels like surrender, and the leader's sudden death or incapacity orphans the plan.
  **Solution:** Name the successor at the moment the plan first exceeds one year in length, and treat the naming as a standard plan component, reviewed each time the step chain is reviewed.
