# Chapter 8 Analysis: Create a Vision for Your Future

## Actionable Lessons

### Step-by-Step Instructions: The Three Most Important Questions Exercise

**Preparation**
1. Gather a writing tool: journal, computer, smartphone, or anything else.
2. Set a timer for approximately **3 minutes per category**.

**Question 1: What Experiences Do You Want to Have?**
3. Ask yourself: *"If time and money were no object and I did not have to seek anyone's permission, what kinds of experiences would my soul crave?"*
4. Brainstorm across the first four Twelve Areas of Balance:
   - **Love Relationship**: Communication style, shared activities, daily life together, holidays, moral/ethical beliefs, passionate sex.
   - **Friendships**: Ideal friends, perfect weekend, places, conversations, activities.
   - **Adventures**: Amazing adventures you've observed, places you want to see, adventurous activities that make your soul sing.
   - **Environment**: Home description, favorite room, heavenly bed, ideal car, perfect workspace, preferred restaurants and hotels.

**Question 2: How Do You Want to Grow?**
5. Ask yourself: *"In order to have the experiences above, how do I have to grow? What sort of man or woman do I need to evolve into?"*
6. Brainstorm across the next four Twelve Areas of Balance:
   - **Health and Fitness**: How you want to feel/look daily and in 5, 10, or 20 years; eating and fitness systems you'd explore for curiosity; fitness goals for the thrill of accomplishment.
   - **Intellectual Life**: What you need to learn for your experiences; books/movies that stretch your mind; art/music/theater interests; languages to master; learning opportunities where joy is in the learning itself, not a diploma.
   - **Skills**: Job skills you'd enjoy mastering; skills for a professional switch; skills just for fun; skills that would make you happy/proud.
   - **Spiritual Life**: Where you are now vs. where you want to be; deepening current practice or trying new ones; highest aspiration; lucid dreaming, meditation, overcoming fear/worry/stress.

**Question 3: How Do You Want to Contribute?**
7. Ask yourself: *"If I have the experiences above and have grown in these remarkable ways, then how can I give back to the world?"*
8. Brainstorm across the final four Twelve Areas of Balance:
   - **Career**: Visions for career, desired competence level, workplace improvement, contribution to field; reassess if work feels meaningless vs. merely lacking meaning to you.
   - **Creative Life**: Creative activities you love or want to learn (cooking, singing, photography, painting, writing poetry, software development); ways to share your creative self.
   - **Family Life**: Being with family in ways that fill you with happiness; values to embody and pass along; unique contributions; redefine family beyond traditional models (partners, same-sex partners, chosen family).
   - **Community Life**: Contribution to friends, neighborhood, city, state, nation, religious community, or world community; the mark you want to leave.

**Post-Exercise Validation (The Five-Step Process)**
9. Identify a goal.
10. Answer exhaustively: *"When I achieve this goal, I will be able to __, __, __, [etc.]"*
11. Answer exhaustively: *"When I achieve all this, I will feel __, __, __ [etc.]"*
12. Identify true underlying objectives based on answers to 10 and 11.
13. Compare with the original goal:
    - Is this original goal the only way/best way to achieve these objectives?
    - Is this original goal enough to achieve them?
    - Can I achieve them in a more effective way?

**Integration into Life**
14. Stick your completed list on a wall where you can see it.
15. Share it with others (partner, team, workplace) to empower both yourself and them.
16. For teams/companies: photograph each person's blueprint and carry it to give personalized support (e.g., a book that helps achieve their dream).

### Best Practices

- **Set a timer**: Forces logical mind to shut up so intuitive mind can flow freely.
- **Focus on feelings**: End goals are often feelings (to be happy, to be in love, to feel joyous). Happiness should be baked into the pursuit itself.
- **Three-minute rule**: Don't overthink; trust intuition. You can analyze and sort later.
- **Use the "so" test**: If your goal has a "so" attached ("get a good GPA so I can get into college so..."), it's likely a means goal.
- **Visualize vividly**: Imagine experiences in all facets—what you see, feel, hear, and do.
- **Share publicly**: Post blueprints on corkboards/team walls to build transparency, trust, and collaboration.
- **Support others' dreams**: Use knowledge of teammates' blueprints to offer small, genuine gifts that help them grow.
- **Work backward from feelings**: Identify what feelings a goal will bring to determine if it's truly an end goal.

---

### Common Pitfalls

- **Confusing means goals with end goals**: Chasing a degree, job title, income level, or startup exit as an end in itself, when it's merely a means to happiness.
- **Following Brules**: Pursuing goals because society says you "should" (college degree, marriage, family business, entrepreneurship) without questioning if they align with your soul.
- **Choosing a career too early**: Selecting a life path before legally being able to buy a beer, leading to decades in an uninspiring rut.
- **The "life dip" trap**: Being bored, lonely, and missing meaning while checking off externally successful goals (fast-growing business, funding, press, money, titles) but feeling miserable.
- **The retirement fantasy**: Working a miserable job for 40 years to "do what you really want" after retirement, only to realize life has passed you by.
- **Overthinking**: Letting the logical mind override intuition during the exercise, leading to goals that please others or conform to society.
- **Expecting instant results**: The path to end goals is often unexpected (e.g., A-Fest emerged from a spontaneous idea, not a pre-planned goal).
- **Thinking you need a specific label**: Believing you must be an entrepreneur, married, or have kids to have love, freedom, or growth—when these can come in many different forms.

---

### Keep In Mind

- **"A good goal should scare you a little and excite you a lot."** — Joe Vitale. Scary means you're pushing boundaries; excitement means it's genuinely close to your heart.
- End goals are about **experiences**, **growth**, and **contribution**—not money, titles, or objects.
- When you give your mind a clear vision, it will find a way to bring it to you—whether it's a means or end goal.
- **Time flies when you're pursuing end goals**, but they don't feel like "work" and actually recharge you.
- **End goals have happiness baked into the pursuit**; means goals defer happiness to some future date.
- **Steve Jobs**: "You can't connect the dots looking forward; you can only connect them looking backward. So you have to trust that the dots will somehow connect in your future."
- The brain's **reticular activating system** and concepts like synchronicity or the law of attraction describe how vision creates opportunity.
- **A-Fest** was never a goal in itself; it emerged as an evolution of multiple end goals coalescing.
- The author went into debt to buy back his company because the money wasn't worth the misery—proving that alignment with end goals matters more than financial metrics.

---

### Security/Safety Notes

- **Personal privacy**: Blueprints contain deeply personal dreams. Consider privacy settings before sharing publicly.
- **Genuine intent**: When supporting others' dreams, ensure gestures are genuine rather than manipulative.
- **No monopoly on dreams**: Avoid gatekeeping or claiming ownership over others' aspirations.
- **Work-life boundaries**: While the exercise is powerful for workplace culture, ensure it doesn't pressure employees into oversharing or create unrealistic expectations.
- **Financial prudence**: The author went into debt to buy back his company. While this aligned with his end goals, it carries financial risk. Evaluate personal financial safety before making similar decisions.
- **Authenticity check**: Regularly reassess whether goals are truly yours or inherited from cultural Brules.

## Summary

Chapter 8 argues that **conventional goal setting is dangerous** when it trains people to chase societal "Brules" (belief rules) and means goals instead of true end goals. Author Vishen Lakhiani distinguishes **means goals** (stepping stones like degrees, jobs, income, and status, which society tells you to pursue) from **end goals** (inherently rewarding experiences, personal growth, and meaningful contribution). He shares his 2010 wake-up call: despite running a successful startup, raising venture capital, and hitting every metric on his goal list, he was bored, lonely, and miserable. He realized he had been pursuing means goals masquerading as end goals.

He wrote down true end goals centered on travel, friendship, luxury, learning opportunities, meeting legends, teaching, and spectacular fun. Within months, he sold his miserable second business, went into debt to buy back his company (Mindvalley), and had the crazy inspiration to start a festival. From this emerged **A-Fest**, a global event that fulfilled every one of his end goals: friendship, travel, five-star hotels, learning opportunities, meeting experts, and spectacular fun.

The chapter presents **the Three Most Important Questions exercise**: (1) What experiences do you want to have? (2) How do you want to grow? (3) How do you want to contribute? It maps to the Twelve Areas of Balance. Lakhiani provides detailed prompts for each area and a **five-step validation process** (designed by Mia Koning) to ensure goals are truly end goals rather than disguised means goals. He also introduces **Blueprints for the Soul**, where Mindvalley employees publicly post their visions to build transparency, trust, and collaboration—resulting in employees checking off bold dreams in unexpected ways.

The chapter concludes with **Law 8**: extraordinary minds create a vision for their future that is free from cultural expectations and focused on end goals that strike a direct chord with their happiness. Lakhiani emphasizes that trusting your vision allows your brain to "connect the dots backward," creating opportunities and synchronicities that bring your dreams to reality.
