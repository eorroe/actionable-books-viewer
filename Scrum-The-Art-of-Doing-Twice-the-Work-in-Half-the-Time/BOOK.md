# Scrum: The Art of Doing Twice the Work in Half the Time

## Overview

Scrum is a framework for team performance that enables organizations to deliver twice the work in half the time by replacing rigid, top-down planning with iterative, self-organizing teams. The book teaches how to implement Scrum to eliminate waste, accelerate delivery, and create happier, more productive teams. It provides actionable lessons drawn from real-world examples ranging from the FBI to manufacturing to education, showing how Scrum's principles can transform any complex work.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When you need to rescue a failing project using iterative, incremental delivery
- When working with cross-functional teams that need to deliver working product frequently
- When the user asks about replacing Waterfall planning with Agile methodologies
- When you need to improve team velocity and eliminate waste in your organization
- When building or scaling product development in software, hardware, or non-technical domains
- When you want to measure and improve team happiness as a business metric
- When you need to prioritize work to deliver the highest value first
- When implementing Scrum in education, healthcare, government, or non-profit sectors

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Implement the Inspect and Adapt Cycle

#### Examples

##### Example 1: FBI Virtual Case File (VCF) Failure
- **Cost:** $170 million
- **Result:** Zero delivered
- **Method:** Waterfall with Gantt charts and detailed upfront planning
- **Problem:** New defects discovered faster than old ones were fixed; plan was a "complete fabrication"

##### Example 2: FBI Sentinel Success
- **Original Budget:** $451 million
- **Status in March 2010:** $405 million spent, only half done, a year late
- **Scrum Approach:** Brought development in-house, cut staff from ~250 to ~52
- **Result:** Delivered in ~18 months coding + 2 months deployment with roughly $20 million (5% of original budget)
- **Productivity Increase:** Teams increased productivity by a factor of three

##### Example 3: Healthcare.gov
- **Front end:** Built using Scrum, completed in 3 months, worked beautifully
- **Back end:** Planned using Waterfall techniques, involved 20+ contractors
- **Result:** Back end was a debacle; the site didn't work
- **Fix:** The people who fixed Healthcare.gov used Scrum

#### Step 1: Regularly Stop to Review Progress and Improve

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Every little while (e.g., at the end of each Sprint), stop doing what you are doing
2. Review what you have accomplished
3. Ask if what you are doing is still what you should be doing
4. Ask if there are ways to do it better and faster
5. Identify what might be keeping you from improving
6. Adjust your approach based on what you learned

**Key insight:** This requires thought, introspection, honesty, and discipline.

#### Step 2: Prioritize Requirements by Value

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Gather all requirements documentation
2. Review each requirement individually
3. Ask: "What will bring the most value to the project?"
4. Prioritize requirements so the top 20 percent of features (which deliver 80 percent of value) are done first
5. Deliver high-value items before lower-value items
6. Reassess priorities as you learn more

**Key insight:** 80 percent of the value is in 20 percent of the features.

#### Step 3: Run Scrum Sprints

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Decide on a fixed Sprint length (e.g., two weeks)
2. At the beginning of each Sprint, hold a Sprint Planning meeting
3. The team reviews the prioritized list of work items
4. The team decides how many work items they can accomplish during the Sprint
5. Write work items out (often on sticky notes) and put them on the wall
6. Work on those items during the Sprint
7. At the end of the Sprint, demonstrate the finished increment of product to stakeholders and users

**Key insight:** This methodology allows teams to get near real-time feedback on their work and adjust direction as needed.

#### Step 4: Measure and Improve Team Velocity

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. After each Sprint, count how many work items (sticky notes) the team actually completed
2. Compare this to how many items were brought into the Sprint
3. Establish a baseline sense of how fast the team can go—this is the team's velocity
4. Measure velocity over several Sprints
5. Use velocity to forecast completion dates
6. Each Sprint, try to eliminate impediments to increase velocity
7. Do NOT increase velocity by working longer hours—work better and smarter instead

**Key insight:** Jeff Johnson's teams increased productivity by a factor of three by improving how they worked together and eliminating impediments each Sprint.

#### Step 5: Conduct Sprint Retrospectives to Remove Impediments

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. After demonstrating the Sprint's completed work, gather the team
2. Discuss HOW the team worked, not just what they delivered
3. Ask: "How can we work together better in the next Sprint?"
4. Ask: "What was getting in our way during the last Sprint?"
5. Identify all impediments that are slowing the team's velocity
6. Assign someone (ideally in senior management) to actively remove each impediment
7. Make impediment removal a continuous priority—everything that stands in the way of flow is waste

**Key insight:** Taiichi Ohno's Toyota Production System teaches that management's key task is to identify and remove impediments to flow.

#### Step 6: Hold Frequent Demos with Stakeholders and Users

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. At the end of each Sprint, prepare a demonstration of the finished increment of product
2. Invite all stakeholders: customers, users, management, inspectors, external parties
3. Show the actual product working—not slides or documentation
4. Gather feedback directly from the people who will use the system
5. Use this feedback to adjust the next Sprint's priorities
6. Be transparent about progress—show real status, not reports that hide problems

**Key insight:** "Scrum is not about the developers. It's about the customers and stakeholders. Showing the actual product was the most powerful part."

#### Step 7: Abandon Rigid Up-Front Planning (Waterfall)

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Recognize that detailed plans always fail when they meet reality
2. Stop spending months planning every detail of a project
3. Stop trying to restrict change or know the unknowable
4. Accept that every project involves discovery of problems and bursts of inspiration
5. Build into your working method the assumption of change, discovery, and new ideas
6. Use iterative cycles (Sprints) instead of trying to plan the entire project upfront

**Key insight:** Trying to restrict a human endeavor to color-coded charts and graphs is foolish and doomed to failure.

#### Step 8: Cut Staff and Bring Development In-House When Outsourcing Fails

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Recognize when the contractor approach is failing (late, over budget, not delivering)
2. Unravel the contracts with the outsourced vendor
3. Bring development in-house
4. Dramatically cut the number of developers
5. Focus on a small, core team
6. Apply Scrum principles to the in-house team

**Key insight:** The FBI went from a $451M project that was years late, to delivering in less than a fifth of the time with less than a tenth of the budget.

#### Step 9: Establish a Clean and Known Good State Before Full Deployment

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Develop the product incrementally through Sprints
2. Test each increment thoroughly
3. Ensure data integrity is beyond doubt
4. Only turn the system on for everyone once you have a clean and known good state
5. If possible, stage the rollout

**Key insight:** For the Sentinel deployment, the FBI had to turn it on all at once because "in a criminal case or counterterrorism case, something in Los Angeles might be related to something in Chicago."

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do prioritize requirements by value before starting any Sprint
- ✅ Do run Sprints with a fixed duration and a "Done" increment
- ✅ Do measure velocity and use it to forecast completion
- ✅ Do conduct Retrospectives after every Sprint to remove impediments
- ✅ Do demonstrate working product to stakeholders every Sprint
- ❌ Don't use detailed Gantt charts for creative, complex work
- ❌ Don't add more people to late projects (Brooks's Law)
- ❌ Don't increase velocity by working longer hours
- ❌ Don't skip the Retrospective or treat it as a formality
- ❌ Don't allow mid-Sprint scope changes from outside the team

#### Keep In Mind

- Planning is useful, but blindly following plans is stupid
- Inspect and Adapt is the core learning process in Scrum
- Change or die—clinging to old command-and-control methods will bring failure
- Fail fast so you can fix early
- Everything that stands in the way of flow is waste

#### Security & Safety Notes

- For systems used in legal proceedings or critical infrastructure, ensure data integrity is beyond doubt before full deployment
- When bringing development in-house, ensure proper security protocols are established from the start
- Transparency in Scrum should not compromise sensitive information

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Detailed plans always fail when they meet reality
  **Solution:** Use iterative cycles (Sprints) instead of trying to plan the entire project upfront
- **Problem:** Team velocity is not improving
  **Solution:** Identify and remove impediments each Sprint; do not increase hours
- **Problem:** Stakeholders are not getting what they need
  **Solution:** Hold frequent demos with actual working product, not slides or documentation

### Lesson 2: Apply the OODA Loop for Decision-Making Under Pressure

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Vietnam Fighter Pilot Reconnaissance Missions (1967)
Jeff Sutherland's experience flying RF-4C Phantom aircraft at low altitude into enemy territory, applying the OODA Loop to survive and complete missions.

##### Example 2: MidContinent Computer Services ATM Network (1983)
Sutherland's first major application of Scrum-like principles. He formed a separate division, aligned incentives to team performance, and introduced early versions of the Product Owner, Product Backlog, and weekly Sprints. Result: the most profitable division in the company within six months.

##### Example 3: Easel Product Development (1993)
Sutherland scrapped the Gantt chart and implemented the first formal Scrum process based on Takeuchi and Nonaka's paper. The team delivered a new product line on schedule, under budget, and with fewer bugs than any previous delivery — in six months.

##### Example 4: BellSouth Waterfall Failure
BellSouth executed Waterfall perfectly, delivering $10–20 million projects exactly on time and on budget. But the customer no longer wanted what they had requested 18 months earlier. BellSouth subsequently went out of business.

##### Example 5: Paper Airplane PDCA Exercise
Sutherland's training tool for teaching the PDCA cycle. Teams build paper airplanes across three six-minute cycles (1 min Plan, 3 min Do, 2 min Check + Act), achieving 2–3x faster speed and double the quality after just three iterations.

#### Step 1: Observe

Take in the current situation or environment. Gather all available data about what is happening.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Take in the current situation or environment
2. Gather all available data about what is happening
3. Do not jump to conclusions before fully understanding the situation

#### Step 2: Orient

Figure out the best path forward given the current state. Adjust your understanding in the face of unexpected events.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Figure out the best path forward given the current state
2. Adjust your understanding in the face of unexpected events
3. Factor in your knowledge, previous experience, market conditions, and what outcomes are possible
4. Create a menu of alternatives

#### Step 3: Decide

Make a clear decision based on the data and your orientation. Do not hesitate.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Make a clear decision based on the data and your orientation
2. Do not hesitate
3. The decision should be informed by observation and orientation

#### Step 4: Act

Execute the decision decisively. Do not second-guess once you have acted.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Execute the decision decisively
2. Do not second-guess once you have acted
3. The OODA Loop balances speed with informed judgment

**Key insight:** Hesitation can get people killed (or projects killed). The OODA Loop balances speed with informed judgment.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do gather all available data before making decisions
- ✅ Do adjust your understanding when faced with unexpected events
- ✅ Do make clear decisions and act decisively
- ❌ Don't hesitate once you have made a decision
- ❌ Don't make decisions without proper observation and orientation
- ❌ Don't second-guess yourself after acting

#### Keep In Mind

- The OODA Loop balances speed with informed judgment
- Hesitation can kill projects; foolhardiness is also dangerous
- The loop must be repeated continuously for continuous adaptation

#### Security & Safety Notes

- In high-stakes environments, ensure that observation includes security-relevant data
- Orientation should factor in threat assessments and risk analyses
- Decisions should consider security implications before acting

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Hesitation in decision-making
  **Solution:** Use the OODA Loop to balance speed with informed judgment
- **Problem:** Making decisions without proper data
  **Solution:** Always start with thorough observation and orientation
- **Problem:** Second-guessing after acting
  **Solution:** Once a decision is made and acted upon, move to the next OODA cycle

### Lesson 3: Build a "Company Within a Company" to Fix a Broken Organization

#### Examples

##### Example 1: MidContinent Computer Services ATM Network (1983)
At MidContinent Computer Services in 1983, Jeff Sutherland used this approach for the ATM network division. Costs were 30 percent higher than revenue, and the Waterfall-method projects were failing. After restructuring into a separate unit, within six months the division became the most profitable in the company, with revenue 30 percent higher than expenses.

#### Step 1: Identify the Broken Unit

Find the specific product, team, or division that is chronically failing.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Find the specific product, team, or division that is chronically failing

#### Step 2: Request Permission to Form a Separate Organization

Ask leadership to carve out a dedicated unit with its own structure.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Ask leadership to carve out a dedicated unit with its own structure

#### Step 3: Assemble Dedicated Teams

Include everyone involved in the product — your own sales team, marketing team, and finance people. Do not share resources with the broken parent organization.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Include everyone involved in the product — your own sales team, marketing team, and finance people
2. Do not share resources with the broken parent organization

#### Step 4: Stop Counterproductive Behaviors Immediately

Explicitly state to the team: "The first thing we need to do is stop doing stuff that is killing us."

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Explicitly state to the team: "The first thing we need to do is stop doing stuff that is killing us."

#### Step 5: Align Incentives to Team/Company Performance

Base bonuses on total company (or team) performance, not individual performance.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Base bonuses on total company (or team) performance, not individual performance

#### Step 6: Run the Entire Small Company as One Team Split into Sub-Teams

Foster collaboration across functions.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Foster collaboration across functions

**Key outcomes to watch for:** Revenue exceeding expenses, on-time delivery, fewer bugs, team morale improvement.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do form a separate unit with its own structure and resources
- ✅ Do include all functions needed for the product in the same unit
- ✅ Do align incentives to team/company performance
- ❌ Don't share resources with the broken parent organization
- ❌ Don't base bonuses on individual performance

#### Keep In Mind

- A company within a company can turn around a failing division in months
- The key is autonomy, aligned incentives, and stopping destructive behaviors

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Ensure the new unit has proper security protocols independent of the parent
- Protect sensitive data and systems during the transition

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Parent organization interferes with the new unit
  **Solution:** Establish clear boundaries and autonomy for the new unit
- **Problem:** Incentives are not properly aligned
  **Solution:** Base bonuses on team/company performance, not individual performance
