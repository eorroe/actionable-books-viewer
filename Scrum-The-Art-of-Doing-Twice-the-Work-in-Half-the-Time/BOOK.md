# Scrum: The Art of Doing Twice the Work in Half the Time

## Overview

Scrum is a framework for team performance that enables organizations to deliver twice the work in half the time by replacing rigid, top-down planning with iterative, self-organizing teams. The book teaches how to implement Scrum to eliminate waste, accelerate delivery, and create happier, more productive teams. It provides actionable lessons drawn from real-world examples ranging from the FBI to manufacturing to education, showing how Scrum's principles can transform any complex work.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When you need to rescue a failing project using iterative, incremental delivery
- When working with cross-functional teams that need to deliver working product frequently
- When the user asks about replacing Waterfall planning with Agile methodologies
- When you need to improve team velocity and eliminate waste in your organization
- When building or scaling product development in software, hardware, or non-technical domains
- When you want to measure and improve team happiness as a business metric
- When you need to prioritize work to deliver the highest value first
- When implementing Scrum in education, healthcare, government, or non-profit sectors

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Implement the Inspect and Adapt Cycle

#### Examples

##### Example 1: FBI Virtual Case File (VCF) Failure
- **Cost:** $170 million
- **Result:** Zero delivered
- **Method:** Waterfall with Gantt charts and detailed upfront planning
- **Problem:** New defects discovered faster than old ones were fixed; plan was a "complete fabrication"

##### Example 2: FBI Sentinel Success
- **Original Budget:** $451 million
- **Status in March 2010:** $405 million spent, only half done, a year late
- **Scrum Approach:** Brought development in-house, cut staff from ~250 to ~52
- **Result:** Delivered in ~18 months coding + 2 months deployment with roughly $20 million (5% of original budget)
- **Productivity Increase:** Teams increased productivity by a factor of three

##### Example 3: Healthcare.gov
- **Front end:** Built using Scrum, completed in 3 months, worked beautifully
- **Back end:** Planned using Waterfall techniques, involved 20+ contractors
- **Result:** Back end was a debacle; the site didn't work
- **Fix:** The people who fixed Healthcare.gov used Scrum

#### Step 1: Regularly Stop to Review Progress and Improve

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Every little while (e.g., at the end of each Sprint), stop doing what you are doing
2. Review what you have accomplished
3. Ask if what you are doing is still what you should be doing
4. Ask if there are ways to do it better and faster
5. Identify what might be keeping you from improving
6. Adjust your approach based on what you learned

**Key insight:** This requires thought, introspection, honesty, and discipline.

#### Step 2: Prioritize Requirements by Value

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Gather all requirements documentation
2. Review each requirement individually
3. Ask: "What will bring the most value to the project?"
4. Prioritize requirements so the top 20 percent of features (which deliver 80 percent of value) are done first
5. Deliver high-value items before lower-value items
6. Reassess priorities as you learn more

**Key insight:** 80 percent of the value is in 20 percent of the features.

#### Step 3: Run Scrum Sprints

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Decide on a fixed Sprint length (e.g., two weeks)
2. At the beginning of each Sprint, hold a Sprint Planning meeting
3. The team reviews the prioritized list of work items
4. The team decides how many work items they can accomplish during the Sprint
5. Write work items out (often on sticky notes) and put them on the wall
6. Work on those items during the Sprint
7. At the end of the Sprint, demonstrate the finished increment of product to stakeholders and users

**Key insight:** This methodology allows teams to get near real-time feedback on their work and adjust direction as needed.

#### Step 4: Measure and Improve Team Velocity

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. After each Sprint, count how many work items (sticky notes) the team actually completed
2. Compare this to how many items were brought into the Sprint
3. Establish a baseline sense of how fast the team can go—this is the team's velocity
4. Measure velocity over several Sprints
5. Use velocity to forecast completion dates
6. Each Sprint, try to eliminate impediments to increase velocity
7. Do NOT increase velocity by working longer hours—work better and smarter instead

**Key insight:** Jeff Johnson's teams increased productivity by a factor of three by improving how they worked together and eliminating impediments each Sprint.

#### Step 5: Conduct Sprint Retrospectives to Remove Impediments

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. After demonstrating the Sprint's completed work, gather the team
2. Discuss HOW the team worked, not just what they delivered
3. Ask: "How can we work together better in the next Sprint?"
4. Ask: "What was getting in our way during the last Sprint?"
5. Identify all impediments that are slowing the team's velocity
6. Assign someone (ideally in senior management) to actively remove each impediment
7. Make impediment removal a continuous priority—everything that stands in the way of flow is waste

**Key insight:** Taiichi Ohno's Toyota Production System teaches that management's key task is to identify and remove impediments to flow.

#### Step 6: Hold Frequent Demos with Stakeholders and Users

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. At the end of each Sprint, prepare a demonstration of the finished increment of product
2. Invite all stakeholders: customers, users, management, inspectors, external parties
3. Show the actual product working—not slides or documentation
4. Gather feedback directly from the people who will use the system
5. Use this feedback to adjust the next Sprint's priorities
6. Be transparent about progress—show real status, not reports that hide problems

**Key insight:** "Scrum is not about the developers. It's about the customers and stakeholders. Showing the actual product was the most powerful part."

#### Step 7: Abandon Rigid Up-Front Planning (Waterfall)

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Recognize that detailed plans always fail when they meet reality
2. Stop spending months planning every detail of a project
3. Stop trying to restrict change or know the unknowable
4. Accept that every project involves discovery of problems and bursts of inspiration
5. Build into your working method the assumption of change, discovery, and new ideas
6. Use iterative cycles (Sprints) instead of trying to plan the entire project upfront

**Key insight:** Trying to restrict a human endeavor to color-coded charts and graphs is foolish and doomed to failure.

#### Step 8: Cut Staff and Bring Development In-House When Outsourcing Fails

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Recognize when the contractor approach is failing (late, over budget, not delivering)
2. Unravel the contracts with the outsourced vendor
3. Bring development in-house
4. Dramatically cut the number of developers
5. Focus on a small, core team
6. Apply Scrum principles to the in-house team

**Key insight:** The FBI went from a $451M project that was years late, to delivering in less than a fifth of the time with less than a tenth of the budget.

#### Step 9: Establish a Clean and Known Good State Before Full Deployment

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Develop the product incrementally through Sprints
2. Test each increment thoroughly
3. Ensure data integrity is beyond doubt
4. Only turn the system on for everyone once you have a clean and known good state
5. If possible, stage the rollout

**Key insight:** For the Sentinel deployment, the FBI had to turn it on all at once because "in a criminal case or counterterrorism case, something in Los Angeles might be related to something in Chicago."

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do prioritize requirements by value before starting any Sprint
- ✅ Do run Sprints with a fixed duration and a "Done" increment
- ✅ Do measure velocity and use it to forecast completion
- ✅ Do conduct Retrospectives after every Sprint to remove impediments
- ✅ Do demonstrate working product to stakeholders every Sprint
- ❌ Don't use detailed Gantt charts for creative, complex work
- ❌ Don't add more people to late projects (Brooks's Law)
- ❌ Don't increase velocity by working longer hours
- ❌ Don't skip the Retrospective or treat it as a formality
- ❌ Don't allow mid-Sprint scope changes from outside the team

#### Keep In Mind

- Planning is useful, but blindly following plans is stupid
- Inspect and Adapt is the core learning process in Scrum
- Change or die—clinging to old command-and-control methods will bring failure
- Fail fast so you can fix early
- Everything that stands in the way of flow is waste

#### Security & Safety Notes

- For systems used in legal proceedings or critical infrastructure, ensure data integrity is beyond doubt before full deployment
- When bringing development in-house, ensure proper security protocols are established from the start
- Transparency in Scrum should not compromise sensitive information

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Detailed plans always fail when they meet reality
  **Solution:** Use iterative cycles (Sprints) instead of trying to plan the entire project upfront
- **Problem:** Team velocity is not improving
  **Solution:** Identify and remove impediments each Sprint; do not increase hours
- **Problem:** Stakeholders are not getting what they need
  **Solution:** Hold frequent demos with actual working product, not slides or documentation

### Lesson 2: Apply the OODA Loop for Decision-Making Under Pressure

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Vietnam Fighter Pilot Reconnaissance Missions (1967)
Jeff Sutherland's experience flying RF-4C Phantom aircraft at low altitude into enemy territory, applying the OODA Loop to survive and complete missions.

##### Example 2: MidContinent Computer Services ATM Network (1983)
Sutherland's first major application of Scrum-like principles. He formed a separate division, aligned incentives to team performance, and introduced early versions of the Product Owner, Product Backlog, and weekly Sprints. Result: the most profitable division in the company within six months.

##### Example 3: Easel Product Development (1993)
Sutherland scrapped the Gantt chart and implemented the first formal Scrum process based on Takeuchi and Nonaka's paper. The team delivered a new product line on schedule, under budget, and with fewer bugs than any previous delivery — in six months.

##### Example 4: BellSouth Waterfall Failure
BellSouth executed Waterfall perfectly, delivering $10–20 million projects exactly on time and on budget. But the customer no longer wanted what they had requested 18 months earlier. BellSouth subsequently went out of business.

##### Example 5: Paper Airplane PDCA Exercise
Sutherland's training tool for teaching the PDCA cycle. Teams build paper airplanes across three six-minute cycles (1 min Plan, 3 min Do, 2 min Check + Act), achieving 2–3x faster speed and double the quality after just three iterations.

#### Step 1: Observe

Take in the current situation or environment. Gather all available data about what is happening.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Take in the current situation or environment
2. Gather all available data about what is happening
3. Do not jump to conclusions before fully understanding the situation

#### Step 2: Orient

Figure out the best path forward given the current state. Adjust your understanding in the face of unexpected events.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Figure out the best path forward given the current state
2. Adjust your understanding in the face of unexpected events
3. Factor in your knowledge, previous experience, market conditions, and what outcomes are possible
4. Create a menu of alternatives

#### Step 3: Decide

Make a clear decision based on the data and your orientation. Do not hesitate.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Make a clear decision based on the data and your orientation
2. Do not hesitate
3. The decision should be informed by observation and orientation

#### Step 4: Act

Execute the decision decisively. Do not second-guess once you have acted.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Execute the decision decisively
2. Do not second-guess once you have acted
3. The OODA Loop balances speed with informed judgment

**Key insight:** Hesitation can get people killed (or projects killed). The OODA Loop balances speed with informed judgment.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do gather all available data before making decisions
- ✅ Do adjust your understanding when faced with unexpected events
- ✅ Do make clear decisions and act decisively
- ❌ Don't hesitate once you have made a decision
- ❌ Don't make decisions without proper observation and orientation
- ❌ Don't second-guess yourself after acting

#### Keep In Mind

- The OODA Loop balances speed with informed judgment
- Hesitation can kill projects; foolhardiness is also dangerous
- The loop must be repeated continuously for continuous adaptation

#### Security & Safety Notes

- In high-stakes environments, ensure that observation includes security-relevant data
- Orientation should factor in threat assessments and risk analyses
- Decisions should consider security implications before acting

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Hesitation in decision-making
  **Solution:** Use the OODA Loop to balance speed with informed judgment
- **Problem:** Making decisions without proper data
  **Solution:** Always start with thorough observation and orientation
- **Problem:** Second-guessing after acting
  **Solution:** Once a decision is made and acted upon, move to the next OODA cycle

### Lesson 3: Build a "Company Within a Company" to Fix a Broken Organization

#### Examples

##### Example 1: MidContinent Computer Services ATM Network (1983)
At MidContinent Computer Services in 1983, Jeff Sutherland used this approach for the ATM network division. Costs were 30 percent higher than revenue, and the Waterfall-method projects were failing. After restructuring into a separate unit, within six months the division became the most profitable in the company, with revenue 30 percent higher than expenses.

#### Step 1: Identify the Broken Unit

Find the specific product, team, or division that is chronically failing.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Find the specific product, team, or division that is chronically failing

#### Step 2: Request Permission to Form a Separate Organization

Ask leadership to carve out a dedicated unit with its own structure.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Ask leadership to carve out a dedicated unit with its own structure

#### Step 3: Assemble Dedicated Teams

Include everyone involved in the product — your own sales team, marketing team, and finance people. Do not share resources with the broken parent organization.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Include everyone involved in the product — your own sales team, marketing team, and finance people
2. Do not share resources with the broken parent organization

#### Step 4: Stop Counterproductive Behaviors Immediately

Explicitly state to the team: "The first thing we need to do is stop doing stuff that is killing us."

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Explicitly state to the team: "The first thing we need to do is stop doing stuff that is killing us."

#### Step 5: Align Incentives to Team/Company Performance

Base bonuses on total company (or team) performance, not individual performance.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Base bonuses on total company (or team) performance, not individual performance

#### Step 6: Run the Entire Small Company as One Team Split into Sub-Teams

Foster collaboration across functions.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Foster collaboration across functions

**Key outcomes to watch for:** Revenue exceeding expenses, on-time delivery, fewer bugs, team morale improvement.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do form a separate unit with its own structure and resources
- ✅ Do include all functions needed for the product in the same unit
- ✅ Do align incentives to team/company performance
- ❌ Don't share resources with the broken parent organization
- ❌ Don't base bonuses on individual performance

#### Keep In Mind

- A company within a company can turn around a failing division in months
- The key is autonomy, aligned incentives, and stopping destructive behaviors

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Ensure the new unit has proper security protocols independent of the parent
- Protect sensitive data and systems during the transition

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Parent organization interferes with the new unit
  **Solution:** Establish clear boundaries and autonomy for the new unit
- **Problem:** Incentives are not properly aligned
  **Solution:** Base bonuses on team/company performance, not individual performance

### Lesson 42: Use the Daily Three Questions to Force Transparency

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Google's "OKR" Meetings
Google adapted a similar three-question format in their OKR (Objectives and Key Results) process: What did you do? What will you do? What blockers do you have?

##### Example 2: Spotify's Daily Stand-Up
Spotify teams use three-question stand-ups to maintain transparency across squads without excessive ceremony.

#### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. Every day, each team member answers three questions in a stand-up:
   - What did you do yesterday?
   - What will you do today?
   - What is getting in your way?
2. The Scrum Master records and acts on blockers immediately
3. Team members share updates honestly—no embellishment or hiding problems

**Key insight:** Transparency is the backbone of Scrum. Without honest answers, you cannot inspect and adapt.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do keep the three questions simple and direct
- ✅ Do act on blockers within 24 hours
- ✅ Do encourage honest, unfiltered answers
- ❌ Don't turn the stand-up into a status report for management
- ❌ Don't allow blockers to persist for multiple Sprints

#### Keep In Mind

- The purpose is transparency, not reporting
- Blockers should be visible to the whole team immediately

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Blocker discussions should not expose sensitive technical details in shared spaces
- Ensure incident-related blockers are documented appropriately for audits

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Team members hide blockers to avoid looking unproductive
  **Solution:** Foster psychological safety—framing blockers as team impediments, not individual failures
- **Problem:** Stand-up turns into a long status meeting
  **Solution:** Keep it under 15 minutes; parking lot topics for after

### Lesson 43: Protect Team Autonomy from Bureaucratic Pullback

#### Examples

##### Example 1: FBI Sentinel
After initial Scrum success, management tried to re-impose traditional PMO controls. The team resisted and preserved autonomy by demonstrating measurable delivery improvements.

##### Example 2: Toyota Production System
Toyota gave line workers the authority to stop production (Andon cord). Bureaucratic pullback would have destroyed this empowerment.

#### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. Establish a team charter that protects the team's right to self-organize
2. Document agreements in writing with leadership sponsorship
3. When management introduces new controls, demonstrate Scrum's superior results
4. Escalate only as a last resort—leadership sponsorship is key

**Key insight:** Autonomy is fragile. Without active protection, bureaucracy will pull back toward command-and-control.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do document team charters and working agreements
- ✅ Do have an executive sponsor who understands and defends Scrum
- ✅ Do regularly demonstrate Sprint results to leadership
- ❌ Don't argue with bureaucracy emotionally; use data
- ❌ Don't give up autonomy without a fight

#### Keep In Mind

- Autonomy is not the same as anarchy—the team is accountable for delivery
- Bureaucratic pullback often happens when results are not visible

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Ensure autonomy doesn't override mandatory compliance or security controls

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Management re-imposes command-and-control
  **Solution:** Use Sprint demos to show tangible results; let data defend the process
- **Problem:** Team misinterprets autonomy as freedom from accountability
  **Solution:** Clear Sprint goals and "Done" criteria maintain accountability

### Lesson 44: Appoint a Product Owner

#### Examples

##### Example 1: MidContinent ATM Network (1983)
Sutherland appointed a clear Product Owner who represented customer needs. The team had one voice for "what" to build, eliminating conflicting requirements.

##### Example 2: Easel Product Development (1993)
The Product Owner prioritized the backlog and made hard trade-off decisions, enabling the team to deliver on schedule and under budget.

#### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. Identify one person (not a committee) to be the Product Owner
2. The Product Owner is responsible for maximizing the value of the product
3. The Product Owner manages the Product Backlog
4. The Product Owner clearly expresses Product Backlog items
5. The Product Owner orders the items to best achieve goals
6. The Product Owner ensures the Backlog is visible, transparent, and understood

**Key insight:** "A product owner who is unsure or unavailable is the biggest impediment to a Scrum team."

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do appoint a single, authoritative Product Owner
- ✅ Do ensure the Product Owner is available to the team during the Sprint
- ✅ Do give the Product Owner the authority to make priority decisions
- ❌ Don't let a committee replace the Product Owner
- ❌ Don't allow the Product Owner to micromanage the "how"

#### Keep In Mind

- The Product Owner represents stakeholders and customers, not management
- A weak or absent Product Owner is worse than no Product Owner at all

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Product Owner should understand compliance and regulatory requirements that affect backlog priorities

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** No clear Product Owner, resulting in conflicting priorities
  **Solution:** Appoint one empowered individual; document the decision
- **Problem:** Product Owner is unavailable during the Sprint
  **Solution:** Establish commitment to availability; treat absence as an impediment

### Lesson 45: Apply the OODA Loop to Product Decisions

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Jeff Sutherland's Vietnam Reconnaissance Missions
The OODA Loop (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) was developed by fighter pilot John Boyd. Sutherland applied it in combat: observe enemy positions, orient based on terrain and intelligence, decide on the safest path, act decisively.

##### Example 2: MidContinent ATM Network Pivot
When initial assumptions proved wrong, the team used OODA to rapidly re-orient and pivot development focus.

#### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Observe:** Gather real customer feedback, usage data, and market signals
2. **Orient:** Analyze what the data means; re-evaluate assumptions
3. **Decide:** Make a clear product decision based on current understanding
4. **Act:** Ship the decision and start the loop again at the next Sprint

**Key insight:** The team that cycles through OODA fastest wins—not the team with the best initial plan.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do run the OODA loop at every Sprint boundary
- ✅ Do use real customer data in the Observe phase
- ✅ Do make decisions quickly and move on
- ❌ Don't let the Orient phase become analysis paralysis
- ❌ Don't ignore data that contradicts your hypothesis

#### Keep In Mind

- OODA in Scrum happens naturally at each Sprint Review and Retrospective
- Speed of the loop matters more than perfection of each step

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Ensure observation includes security incidents, threats, and compliance signals

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Analysis paralysis during Orientation
  **Solution:** Set a timebox for orientation; decide with 80% of data
- **Problem:** Acting without proper observation
  **Solution:** Start every Sprint with a clear data-driven objective

### Lesson 46: Use Rapid Prototyping to Reduce Technical Risk

#### Examples

##### Example 1: FBI Sentinel UI Prototype
Before committing to a full build, the team built a low-fidelity prototype of the Sentinel UI to validate the interaction model with users in days, not months.

##### Example 2: Healthcare.gov Front End
The healthcare.gov front-end team used rapid prototyping to iterate on the user experience, delivering a working UI in just three months.

#### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. Identify the riskiest or most uncertain part of the product
2. Build a throwaway prototype (spike) in one Sprint
3. Test the prototype with real users
4. Capture learnings and feed them into the Product Backlog
5. Decide whether to proceed, pivot, or stop

**Key insight:** Prototypes are for learning, not for production. Build to throw away.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do timebox prototypes to one Sprint
- ✅ Do involve real users in prototype testing
- ✅ Do throw away the prototype code after learning
- ❌ Don't let prototypes evolve into production code without proper refactoring
- ❌ Don't skip prototyping on high-risk components

#### Keep In Mind

- Technical debt accumulates quickly when prototypes become production
- A spike story must have a clear "Done" definition

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Prototypes handling sensitive data should follow security protocols even in throwaway form
- Ensure prototype data is properly sanitized before disposal

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Prototype code ships to production
  **Solution:** Explicitly mark prototypes as technical debt; schedule proper implementation
- **Problem:** Prototyping takes too long
  **Solution:** Use timeboxes ruthlessly; a Sprint is the maximum

### Lesson 47: Implement Change for Free / Options Thinking

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Toyota Production System
Toyota designs manufacturing systems so that changeovers between product models happen quickly and cheaply, treating flexibility as a strategic option.

##### Example 2: FBI Sentinel Architecture
The Sentinel architecture was designed so that new modules could be added without rewriting existing code—keeping future options open.

#### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. Design systems and processes to keep future options open
2. Avoid irreversible decisions (e.g., hard-coding business rules)
3. Build modular, decoupled architectures
4. Treat deferred decisions as options with value
5. When requirements change, evaluate whether the existing design absorbs the change cheaply

**Key insight:** The cost of change should not increase dramatically over time. If it does, your architecture is wrong.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do design for modifiability from day one
- ✅ Do treat architecture decisions as reversible unless proven otherwise
- ✅ Do use interfaces and abstractions to decouple components
- ❌ Don't make irreversible decisions early
- ❌ Don't optimize for today's known requirements at the expense of tomorrow's flexibility

#### Keep In Mind

- Real Options Theory: an option's value comes from the ability to delay commitment
- Scrum's Sprint structure naturally creates options at every boundary

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Flexibility should not compromise security hardening decisions where they are needed
- Document option boundaries clearly for audit trails

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Architecture locks the team into expensive changes later
  **Solution:** Build in modularity; refactor continuously
- **Problem:** Team refuses to make any decisions
  **Solution:** Balance flexibility with timely decisions; some decisions must be made

### Lesson 48: Use Set-Based Concurrent Engineering

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Toyota Automobile Design
Toyota explores multiple design alternatives in parallel before committing to one, rather than choosing the first viable option.

##### Example 2: FBI Sentinel Feature Selection
The Sentinel team explored three different UI technology options concurrently for two weeks before selecting the best performer.

#### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. When facing a design problem, identify multiple viable solutions
2. Develop all solutions in parallel (within a Sprint or across Sprints)
3. Gather data on each solution's performance, cost, and user acceptance
4. Select the best solution based on real evidence, not opinion
5. Discard the losing designs—their cost was the insurance policy against picking the wrong one early

**Key insight:** Choosing the first idea that works is usually wrong. Running multiple options in parallel is cheaper than being wrong.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do explore at least 2–3 options for high-stakes decisions
- ✅ Do timebox parallel exploration equally across options
- ✅ Do use objective criteria to select the winner
- ❌ Don't commit to a single solution before exploring alternatives
- ❌ Don't let the team argue opinions instead of testing options

#### Keep In Mind

- Set-based design costs more upfront but saves money by avoiding late-stage rework
- The "losing" designs are not wasted—they provided critical information

#### Security & Safety Notes

- When evaluating security solutions, all options must meet minimum security requirements before comparison

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Team picks the first option that works to save time
  **Solution:** Enforce a minimum number of options for high-risk decisions
- **Problem:** Parallel work consumes too many resources
  **Solution:** Timebox exploration; use smaller, focused spikes

### Lesson 49: Create a Product Backlog

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Easel Product Development (1993)
Sutherland created the first formal Product Backlog for the Easel product, capturing all known requirements as user stories prioritized by business value.

##### Example 2: FBI Sentinel Backlog
The Sentinel team maintained a single Product Backlog that replaced multiple conflicting requirement documents.

#### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. Capture all known requirements as Product Backlog items
2. Write each item as a user story or requirement from the user's perspective
3. Assign a rough relative size to each item (story points, t-shirt sizes)
4. Order the Backlog by business value, risk, and priority
5. The Product Owner owns and continuously refines the Backlog
6. Review and reorder the Backlog at every Sprint Review

**Key insight:** The Product Backlog is the single source of truth for what the product will become.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do keep the Backlog visible and accessible to the whole team
- ✅ Do refine (groom) the Backlog continuously
- ✅ Do order by value, not just urgency
- ❌ Don't let the Backlog become a detailed requirements document
- ❌ Don't allow scope creep into the Backlog without Product Owner approval

#### Keep In Mind

- The Backlog is never "complete"—it evolves as you learn more
- Lower-priority items may be discarded before they are ever worked on

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Security requirements should be explicit Backlog items, not implicit assumptions
- Compliance requirements must appear in the Backlog with clear acceptance criteria

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Backlog becomes a frozen requirements document
  **Solution:** Treat the Backlog as a living artifact; reorder every Sprint
- **Problem:** Too many low-value items in the Backlog
  **Solution:** Apply the Pareto principle—focus on the 20% that delivers 80% of value

### Lesson 50: Prioritize the Backlog for Maximum Value

#### Examples

##### Example 1: FBI Sentinel Priority Shift
The Sentinel team initially had 1,000+ requirements. The Product Owner reprioritized so that only the top 20% (highest-value, highest-risk items) were built first.

##### Example 2: MidContinent ATM Network
At MidContinent, the team prioritized features that directly drove revenue, discarding "nice-to-have" items that had no measurable business value.

#### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. List all Backlog items with their business value
2. Estimate the cost (effort) of each item
3. Calculate value-per-effort for each item
4. Prioritize high-value, low-effort items first (MoSCoW or WSJF)
5. Defer or remove low-value items
6. Revisit priorities every Sprint based on new information

**Key insight:** 80% of the value comes from 20% of the features. Find and build those first.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do involve stakeholders in value estimation
- ✅ Do use a consistent prioritization framework (e.g., WSJF, RICE)
- ✅ Do revisit priorities at every Sprint Review
- ❌ Don't let the loudest stakeholder dictate priorities
- ❌ Don't build low-value items just because they are easy

#### Keep In Mind

- Priorities change as you learn—what is critical today may be irrelevant tomorrow
- The Product Owner has final say on priority, but must explain the rationale

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Security and compliance requirements should be weighted appropriately in prioritization
- Do not deprioritize critical security work for feature delivery

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Everything is "Priority 1"
  **Solution:** Force ranking; if everything is critical, nothing is
- **Problem:** Priorities are set once and never revisited
  **Solution:** Re-prioritize at every Sprint Review using real feedback

### Lesson 51: Deliver a "Done" Increment Early

#### Examples

##### Example 1: FBI Sentinel Incremental Delivery
The Sentinel team delivered a working, deployable increment every two weeks, even when the overall system was far from complete. Stakeholders saw real progress every Sprint.

##### Example 2: Spotify Minimum Lovable Product

#### Common Pitfalls

##### Example 1: Toyota Production System (Just-in-Time)
Toyota's goal is continuous flow: one piece at a time, with no waiting, no inventory, no defects. Flow is the opposite of batch-and-queue.

##### Example 2: Scrum Sprint Flow
In Scrum, the ideal is continuous flow within a Sprint: one item is completed before the next is started, minimizing work-in-progress and maximizing throughput.

#### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. Limit work-in-progress (WIP) at every step of the process
2. Break work into small, uniform pieces
3. Make handoffs quick and seamless
4. Eliminate queues, bottlenecks, and waiting
5. Measure lead time from idea to delivery
6. Optimize for flow efficiency, not resource utilization

**Key insight:** Flow maximizes throughput. Batch-and-queue maximizes waste.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do limit WIP to one item per team member where possible
- ✅ Do use small, uniform story sizes
- ✅ Do measure lead time, not just cycle time
- ❌ Don't optimize for resource utilization (busyness)
- ❌ Don't allow large batches to accumulate

#### Keep In Mind

- 100% resource utilization creates 100% bottlenecks
- Flow is about throughput, not activity

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Security reviews and approvals should be embedded in flow, not gatekeeping bottlenecks

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Team has high utilization but low throughput
  **Solution:** Reduce WIP; idle capacity is necessary for flow
- **Problem:** Handoffs between teams create delays
  **Solution:** Embed specialists within teams; reduce cross-team dependencies

### Lesson 70: Eliminate Waste (Muri, Mura, Muda)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Toyota Seven Wastes (Muda)
Toyota identifies seven wastes in manufacturing: overproduction, waiting, transport, extra processing, inventory, motion, and defects. All seven apply equally to knowledge work.

##### Example 2: FBI Sentinel Waste Elimination
The Sentinel team identified and eliminated: overproduction (unused features), waiting (for approvals), extra processing (redundant documentation), and defects (rework).

#### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Muri (Overburden):** Identify and reduce excessive workload on people and systems
2. **Mura (Inconsistency):** Smooth out uneven workloads and irregular processes
3. **Muda (Waste):** Identify and eliminate the seven wastes in your process
4. Map the value stream and highlight non-value-added steps
5. Remove waste one step at a time, continuously

**Key insight:** Waste is anything that does not add value from the customer's perspective.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Do map the value stream regularly
- ✅ Do ask "would the customer pay for this step?"
- ✅ Do eliminate waste continuously, not in one big initiative
- ❌ Don't add new processes to eliminate waste (processes are often waste themselves)
- ❌ Don't confuse busywork with value

#### Keep In Mind

- The three types of waste (Muri, Mura, Muda) are interconnected
- Eliminating Mura (inconsistency) often eliminates both Muri and Muda

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Some "waste" (like security reviews) is actually value—distinguish carefully
- Compliance requirements may appear as waste but are mandatory

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Eliminating necessary quality or security steps as "waste"
  **Solution:** Evaluate waste from the customer's perspective; compliance is value to the customer
- **Problem:** Waste elimination becomes a death by a thousand cuts
  **Solution:** Focus on the biggest sources of waste first
