# Chapter III: You Don't Need Their Approval

## Overview

This chapter teaches that wanting approval is natural and even rewarding, but needing approval is an erroneous zone that robs you of your autonomy, your independent thoughts, and your authentic emotional experience in your exact lived circumstances.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you find yourself adjusting your stated views or actions to align with what another person prefers or expects of you
- When someone's disapproval causes you emotional distress, worry about others' judgments, or uncertainty about your own worth, or triggers the urge to defend or alter yourself
- When your choices about what to wear, say, buy, or do are driven by what others will think rather than what you genuinely want or believe

## Steps

### Step 1: Understand the Difference Between Wanting Approval and Needing Approval

Recognize that approval in itself is not unhealthy; receiving praise feels rewarding. Understand that approval-seeking becomes an erroneous zone only when it becomes a need rather than a want. If you want approval, you are simply happy to have the endorsement of other people. If you need approval, you'll feel unable to function if you don't get it. When approval-seeking becomes a need, you give up a significant portion of your capacity for self-direction to another person whose approval you depend on.

### Step 2: Accept That You Can Never Please Everyone and Expect Disapproval

Accept the fact that you can never please everyone; if roughly half of the people you interact with are satisfied with your choices, you have matched a common approval rate. Know that roughly half the people you regularly interact with will disagree with roughly half the views you express or the things you say. When someone disapproves of something you say, instead of reacting with distress or quickly changing your stated view to gain praise, remind yourself that you've just encountered one of the many people who don't share your position. Knowing that you'll always receive some disagreement for everything you feel, think, say, or do—your complete spectrum of lived experience—is the way out of the cycle of hopelessness that comes from expecting universal agreement, a cycle the book's framework identifies as a core feature of the approval-seeking erroneous zone. Once you expect disagreement, you won't be inclined to damage your own well-being with it, and you'll simultaneously stop equating a rejection of an idea or a feeling with a rejection of you as a person. You can never escape disagreement, even when you wish it would stop. For roughly half of the views you hold, other people hold views that differ from yours.

### Step 3: Use the "You" Strategy to Label Disapproval

When you note that someone is not agreeing with you and is even getting angry, rather than shifting or defending yourself, simply respond with: "You're getting upset, and you feel that I shouldn't think the way I do." This reminds you that disapproval belongs to the other person, not you. The you strategy can be employed in situations where someone is disagreeing with you, and it produces reliable outcomes when you apply the technique. Fight your temptation to start with "I," thereby putting yourself in the position of defending or modifying what you just said in order to gain acceptance.

### Step 4: Label Manipulation Attempts When Others Withhold Approval

If you think someone else is trying to manipulate you by withholding approval, state it plainly. Instead of becoming indecisive or overly accommodating for the purpose of gaining someone's agreement, say out loud: "Generally I would shift my position now in order to get you to like me, but I really believe in what I said, and you'll have to deal with your own feelings about it." Or say: "I guess you'd like me to change what I just said." The act of labeling it keeps you connected to your own thinking and behavior.

### Step 5: Thank Someone for Providing Data Even When You Don't Like It

When someone says something you disagree with about your behavior, rather than trying to please them, simply thank them for pointing it out. The act of thanking ends the approval-seeking impulse in that moment.

### Step 6: Actively Seek Disapproval and Practice Not Being Upset

Select someone whose views differ from yours. In the presence of disapproval, maintain your position calmly. Tell yourself that you expect this disagreeing behavior, that their view is valid for them, and that their opinion really has nothing to do with your own worth. By going after disapproval rather than avoiding it, you'll build up your range of responses for dealing effectively with it. You'll become more skilled at remaining calm, and at not having to alter your own views.

### Step 7: Practice Ignoring Disapproval and Not Paying Attention to Manipulation

Ignore disapproval and choose not to focus on those who attempt to manipulate you with their criticism.

### Step 8: Break the Chain Between Others' Thoughts and Your Self-Worth

Talk to yourself when you encounter disapproval. Say: "This is her own reaction. I expect her to behave that way. It has nothing to do with me." The book's framework establishes that this approach will eliminate the damage to your well-being that you cause when you connect someone else's feelings with your own thoughts.

### Step 9: Ask Yourself Whether Their Agreement Would Make You Better Off

When you encounter disapproval, ask yourself this key question: If they agreed with me, would I be better off? The book's framework establishes that the answer is no: whatever others think can have no effect on you unless you allow it. The book's framework also establishes that you will typically find that you are better liked by people whose opinions matter to you, such as a manager or someone close to you, when you can disagree with them without worry.

### Step 10: Accept That Many Won't Ever Understand You and That It's Okay

Accept the simple fact that some people will never understand you, and you don't need their understanding to move forward. Conversely, accept that you won't understand some people who are close to you, and you don't have to. You don't need them to share your perspective. A fundamental principle is recognizing the limits of your understanding of other people's perspectives.

### Step 11: Refuse to Argue or Try to Convince Anyone of the Rightness of Your Stance

Hold your view without arguing or trying to convince others.

### Step 12: Trust Yourself When Buying Personal Items Without Checking With Others

Trust yourself when buying clothes or other personal items without first checking with someone whose opinion you treat as more important than your own.

### Step 13: Stop Verifying Your Facts Through Others

Stop substantiating your facts by having them verified by another person, including a spouse. Eliminate specific phrases such as: "Isn't that right, honey?" or "Didn't we, Ralph?" or "Just ask Marie, she'll tell you."

### Step 14: Correct Yourself Out Loud When You Behave in an Approval-Seeking Way

Correct yourself out loud whenever you've behaved in an approval-seeking way. This builds your recognition of the pattern and helps you practice alternative responses.

### Step 15: Work at Eliminating Unnecessary Apologies

Work at eliminating the excessive apologies you make even when you aren't genuinely sorry for what you've just said. The book's framework holds that many apologies function as requests for forgiveness, and requests for forgiveness can reflect approval-seeking, which takes the form of: "I know you wouldn't like me if I really meant what I just did, so please say that I'm still okay." The book's framework further establishes that apologizing without genuine remorse is ineffective. If you need someone else to forgive you before you can feel better, then you are giving them control over your feelings. You can decide not to repeat certain behaviors and acknowledge that some past actions were mistakes, but apologizing to gain approval can become a pattern that gives others control over your feelings.

### Step 16: Monitor and Balance Your Speaking Time in Conversations

Clock the amount of time that you speak and compare it with the amount spoken by your partner and acquaintances. Work at remaining passive and silent while others dominate the conversation, rather than holding back until others prompt you to speak.

### Step 17: Observe and Reduce Being Interrupted in Social Settings

Observe at your next group social event whether others interrupt you with more interruptions than you initiate. Notice whether you tend to speak in a dismissive way—speaking over each other simultaneously with another member of the group while overriding or minimizing what they are saying—when you talk simultaneously with another member of the group. Your approval-seeking might appear as timidity. Devise strategies for speaking without being interrupted by naming the behavior as it appears in your social interactions.

### Step 18: Track and Reduce Interrogative Sentences in Favor of Declarative Ones

Track the number of statements you make compared to questions you ask. Notice whether you ask questions, seek permission, or seek others' agreement before stating your own view. For example, the question "Nice day, isn't it?" puts the other person into the problem-solving role—the role of having to evaluate and respond to validate your statement—and you into the approval-seeking position—the position of needing the other person's agreement before you feel comfortable with your own observation. A simple "Nice day" is a declaration, rather than an attempt to seek an answer. If you regularly ask others questions before sharing your own view, you are engaging in approval-seeking in routine, everyday conversations, which can reflect reduced self-assurance in your own ability to take charge of the conversational direction.

### Step 19: Rewrite Approval-Seeking Song Lyrics to Reflect Personal Mastery

The next time a song plays with lyrics—the actual sung words of the song—that frame your worth in terms of others' approval, check for those lyrics which reflect the way you've been taught to feel, that is, that you can't make it if someone disapproves of you or lets you down. Rewrite the songs to fit a mindset of personal mastery rather than approval-seeking.

### Step 20: Understand the Supreme Irony of Approval-Seeking Behavior

Recognize that the people who enjoy the most social approval in life are those who never seek it out, who have no desire for it, and who are not preoccupied with achieving it. If you want that approval, the most effective way to get it is to not want it and to avoid chasing after it and to not demand it from others. By attuning yourself to your inner experience—connected to your own genuine thoughts and feelings rather than focused on others' perceptions—and using your positive self-image—your sense of your own inherent worth independent of external validation—as a guide, approval will come more naturally. When you see yourself as worthy—possessing inherent value that does not depend on others' approval—you're less likely to feel distressed when approval is withheld. Disapproval will be viewed by you as a natural consequence of living on a planet where people hold different perceptions.

### Step 21: Recognize Common Approval-Seeking Behaviors in Yourself

Identify whether you change a position or alter what you believe because someone shows signs of disapproval. Notice whether you sugar-coat a statement to avoid the reaction of displeasure. Observe whether you apple-polish—giving someone undeserved or excessive praise in order to make them like you—in order to make someone like you. Notice whether you feel depressed or anxious when someone disagrees with you. Observe whether you feel insulted or put-down—made to feel inferior or diminished by another person's words—when someone states a contrary sentiment to your own. Notice whether you label someone a snob or "stuck-up," which is just another way of saying "Pay more attention to me." Observe whether you are excessively agreeable and head nodding—nodding in agreement even when you internally disagree—even when you don't agree at all with what is being said. Notice whether you perform chores for someone and feel resentful about not being able to say no. Observe whether you are intimidated by a sharp salesperson and buy something you don't want, or fear taking it back because he won't like you. Notice whether you eat a steak in a restaurant that is not cooked the way you ordered it, because the waiter won't like you if you send it back. Notice whether you say things you don't mean just to avoid being disliked. Notice whether you spread bad news about deaths, divorce, muggings and the like, and enjoy the feeling of being noticed. Observe whether you get permission to speak, or to make a purchase, or to do anything, from a significant other—a spouse or long-term romantic partner—in your life, because you fear the displeasure of that person. Notice whether you apologize for yourself at every turn—the excessive "I'm sorry's" that are designed to have others forgive you and approve of you all of the time. Observe whether you behave in nonconforming ways—acting in deliberately unconventional or attention-grabbing ways that deviate from norms—for the purpose of gaining attention, which is the same neurosis as conforming for the sake of external approval—seeking the notice and validation of others as a substitute for genuine self-approval. Notice whether you are pathologically late—habitually and chronically arriving after the scheduled start time—for all occasions to be seen and distinguished. Observe whether you try to impress others with your knowledge of something that you know nothing about by "faking it." Notice whether you beg for compliments by setting yourself up for approval, and then feel bad when they don't come. Observe whether you are unhappy about someone you respect having a contrary point of view, and expressing it to you.

### Step 22: Recognize the Neurotic Payoffs That Keep You Hooked on Approval-Seeking

Understand that placing responsibility for your feelings on others means they, not you, are responsible for how you feel. Recognize that if they are responsible for how you feel because of withholding their approval, then any change in you also becomes impossible, since it is their fault that you feel the way you do. Thus approval-seeking helps you avoid changing. Recognize that as long as they are responsible and you can't change, you don't have to take any risks. Hanging onto approval-seeking helps you avoid taking risks in your life. Recognize that approval-seeking reinforces a negative self-concept—a diminished or negative self-assessment of your own inherent worth—and encourages self-pity and inaction. Recognize that it reinforces the idea that others must take care of you, and therefore you can revert to dependent behavior and being coddled, protected—and manipulated. Recognize that blaming others for what you are feeling creates a scapegoating pattern for everything you don't like in your life. Recognize that deluding yourself that you are liked by those others you've made more important than yourself lets you feel outwardly comfortable even though there is inner discontent. Recognize that taking solace in the fact that others notice you gives you something to boast about to other approval-seeking acquaintances. Recognize that fitting into the culture which applauds such behavior wins you the approval of many people—approval from a large and diffuse network of casual contacts and unfamiliar people.

### Step 23: Understand the Historical Antecedents of Your Approval-Seeking

Understand that the need for approval is based on a single assumption: "Don't trust yourself—check it out with someone else first." Recognize that our culture reinforces approval-seeking behavior as a widely accepted standard. Recognize that independent thinking is not only unconventional, it conflicts with institutions that exercise authority. Understand that "Don't swear by yourself" is the core motive of the need for others' validation—and a feature of numerous cultural systems and institutions. Recognize that any steps toward self-approval and independence from the good opinion of others move you away from others' control, and get labeled with terms like selfish, uncaring, or inconsiderate, in an effort to keep you dependent.

### Step 24: Recognize Early Family Approval-Seeking Messages

The book's framework establishes that young children truly do need acceptance from primary caregivers in their formative years, but approval should not be contingent on behaving properly. Recognize that a child should never have to get a parent's permission for everything he says, thinks, feels, or does. Self-reliance can be encouraged from early childhood, and approval-seeking ought not be confused with love-seeking. Notice when you were taught to check it out with a parent rather than trusting your own judgment. Recognize that parents who treat children as possessions rather than helping them think for themselves, solve their own problems, and develop trust in themselves, are fostering dependence and the need for approval.

### Step 25: Recognize School Approval-Seeking Messages

The book's framework establishes that school is an institution designed to instill approval-seeking thinking and behavior. Notice how you were taught to ask permission to do everything, never rely on your own judgment, and if you incurred a teacher's disapproval, you were expected to feel guilty for a long time. Recognize that your report card was a message to your parents measuring how much approval you had gained. The book's framework observes that students who are independent, full of self-regard, not susceptible to guilt and worry, are routinely classified as problem cases. Recognize that if you gain the praise of the staff, behave in the ways that they dictate, and study the assigned curriculum, you'll emerge with a strong need for approval, since self-reliance has been discouraged at almost every juncture. The book's framework further describes how by graduation, after twelve years of compulsory schooling in which you are repeatedly told how to think and what to think, you come to value approval and learn that gaining others' validation is treated as the same as being successful and happy. Recognize that when given freedom in college, the approval-seeker panics and asks: "But how many papers do we have to do?" "When are they due?" "Do you want it typed?" The student has been trained to do it for someone else, to please the professor, and to measure up to someone else's standards. He is terrified of thinking for himself.

### Step 26: Recognize Institutional Approval-Seeking Messages

Recognize that organized religion appeals to approval-seeking needs by teaching you to behave because an external authority figure has told you to and because you will face consequences if you don't, not because you know it to be the right behavior for you. The book's framework establishes that using yourself as a guide and not needing the approval of an outside authority is a meaningful personal experience. Recognize that the government uses approval-seeking as a motivator for conformity, telling you "Don't trust yourself. You haven't got the skills and wherewithal to function alone. We'll take care of you." The book's framework describes how there are more written rules than there are people to follow them, and the people who make those rules frequently assert that they know what is good for you more confidently than you do yourself.

### Step 27: Recognize Approval-Seeking Messages in Popular Culture

Be aware that popular songs commonly contain lyrics about seeking others' approval, particularly songs released in the past thirty years. Notice television commercials that reinforce the idea that other people's beliefs are more important than your own. The book's framework describes how advertisers capitalize on your need for acceptance by creating short, carefully crafted vignettes that encourage you to buy their products.

### Step 28: Coax Approval-Seeking Downstairs a Step at a Time

Mark Twain offers a practical description of how to break a habitual pattern such as approval-seeking: "Habit is habit, and not to be flung out of the window by any man, but coaxed downstairs one incremental step at a time." Work at reducing approval-seeking gradually, incrementally. You can vow that you can handle disapproval and that you are not going to demand validation from everyone, but until you encounter actual disapproval, you won't know how you are doing. It will require sustained, disciplined practice, but it is worth the effort you put into it.

## Examples

### Example 1: Ozzie — Shifting Positions Based on Who Is Listening

Ozzie, a middle-aged man, shifted his position on mercy killing depending on whether his listener approved. With his father-in-law showing disapproval, Ozzie modified his position to: "What I meant was, if someone is conscious and actually asks to be killed, then mercy killing is O.K." With his boss showing strong disagreement, he shifted again: "What I meant was, only in extreme cases, when a patient is declared legally dead, then it's all right to pull the plug." With his brother showing agreement, he didn't have to change at all. "There is no Ozzie, only the reactions of others which determine not only how Ozzie feels, but also what he thinks and says as well. Ozzie is whatever others want him to be."

### Example 2: Abraham Lincoln — Expecting and Enduring Disapproval

Abraham Lincoln's reported conversation at the White House, as recorded by Francis B. Carpenter: "If I were to read, much less to answer all the attacks made on me, this shop might as well be closed for any other business. I do the very best I know how—the very best I can; and I mean to keep doing so until the end. If the end brings me out alright, what is said against me won't amount to anything. If the end brings me out wrong, ten angels swearing I was right would make no difference."

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You quickly change your opinions, beliefs, or behavior when someone shows disapproval, losing connection with your genuine thoughts and preferences.
  **Solution:** Before changing anything, use the "you" strategy: say out loud, "You're getting upset, and you feel that I shouldn't think the way I do." Remind yourself that disapproval belongs to the other person, and expect that roughly half the people you encounter will disagree with you on roughly half of what you say.

- **Problem:** You apologize repeatedly and habitually, even when you aren't sorry, as a way to seek forgiveness and maintain others' approval.
  **Solution:** Work at eliminating unnecessary apologies. Recognize that apologizing without genuine remorse is ineffective. If you need someone else to forgive you before you can feel better, you are giving them control over your feelings. Resolve not to behave in certain ways again, but stop letting another person's forgiveness control your feelings.

- **Problem:** You make decisions about what to buy, wear, eat, or say based on what others will think, rather than what you genuinely want.
  **Solution:** Trust your own judgment without checking with others first. When someone gives you feedback you didn't ask for, thank them for it and decide for yourself. The book's framework establishes that asking "If they agreed with me, would I be better off?" leads to the answer: no—what others think has no effect on you unless you let it.

- **Problem:** You are unaware of the common everyday methods by which you seek approval—through interrogative speech, self-put-downs, excessive agreeableness, and withholding your own needs.
  **Solution:** Track your interrogative versus declarative sentences, monitor how often you speak in social settings, and watch for self-put-down behaviors. Correct yourself out loud the moment you catch yourself. Gradually, incrementally, reduce the approval-seeking habit incrementally.
