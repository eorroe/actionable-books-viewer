# Chapter V: The Useless Emotions—Guilt and Worry

## Overview

This chapter explains that guilt and worry function as immobilizing emotions that prevent effective action in the present moment—guilt anchors you in past regrets while worry locks you in fear of future events—and provides specific strategies to identify, defuse, and reduce them from your daily life. It distinguishes healthy learning from guilt, identifies the cultural, parental, institutional, and psychological forces that promote guilt, and then offers a set of techniques to reclaim your present moment. The chapter closes by extending the same logic to worry, showing that guilt and worry cannot change past events or future outcomes, and that living in the present moment is one approach to reducing their impact.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you find yourself unable to act due to regret over something you did (or didn't do) in the past
- When anxiety about future events prevents you from acting in the present moment
- When you suspect that guilt is being used—by others or by yourself—as a tool of manipulation or avoidance

## Steps

### Step 1: Recognize That Guilt and Worry Are Useless

Recognize that guilt immobilizes you in the present over past behavior, and worry immobilizes you in the present over future events. Understand that guilt and worry serve a similar function of preventing action in your present moment. Robert Jones Burdette expressed it plainly: "It isn't the experience of today that drives men mad. It is the remorse for something that happened yesterday, and the dread of what tomorrow may disclose." As for worry, the chapter asks: "Is there anything that will ever change as a result of my worrying about it?" The answer is no.

### Step 2: Distinguish Guilt From Learning From Your Past

Identify whether you are simply learning from your past and vowing to avoid the repetition of some specific behavior (this is NOT guilt). Determine if you are unable to take action now as a result of having behaved in a certain way previously (this IS guilt). Learning from mistakes supports growth. Guilt uses energy in the present feeling hurt, upset, and depressed about a historical happening. Guilt cannot undo past events.

### Step 3: Identify the Two Basic Origins of Guilt

Identify whether your guilt is "Leftover Guilt" — emotional reactions carried from childhood memories such as "Daddy won't like you if you do that again," "You should feel ashamed of yourself," or "Oh, all right, I'm only your mother." Identify whether your guilt is "Self-imposed Guilt" — guilt imposed on yourself for breaking an adult rule or moral code, such as having told someone off and disliking yourself for it, feeling drained because of shoplifting, not going to church, or having said the wrong thing in the past. In either case, guilt does not achieve its intended purpose.

### Step 4: Reject Cultural Messages That Promote Guilt

Recognize certain cultural attitudes that say, "If it's fun, you're supposed to feel guilty about it." Learn to experience pleasure without guilt. See yourself as someone capable of doing anything that fits your own value system and does not harm others — and doing it without guilt. If you do something and don't like it or yourself after having done it, vow to change that behavior in the future rather than experiencing guilt.

### Step 5: Recognize Parental Guilt Tactics

Identify parental guilt manipulation techniques. Examples include: "Never mind then, I'll do it—with my bad back. You just sit there and enjoy yourself." Recognize the "I sacrificed for you" mentality (e.g., "I went through eighteen hours of labor just to bring you into the world," "I stayed married to your father because of you"). Notice when parents use phrases like "We'll stay here by ourselves. You just enjoy yourself the way you've always done. Don't worry about us." Be aware of the "You disgraced us" tactic or "What will the neighbors think?" Recognize parental illness guilt: "You've made my blood pressure go up," "You're killing me," "giving me a heart attack." Notice sexual guilt imposed by parents (e.g., "God forbid you should masturbate. It's bad," "You should be ashamed for reading such magazines"). The book illustrates this with Donny's mother using her bad back to manipulate him into bringing up chairs, and the 52-year-old pediatrician who keeps his marriage a secret from his 85-year-old mother because he fears it might "kill her."

### Step 6: Recognize Lover- and Spouse-Related Guilt Tactics

Identify when someone uses the "If you loved me" guilt tactic to punish a partner for particular behavior. Recognize that grudges, long silences, and hurtful looks are methods of engendering guilt (e.g., "I'm not talking to you, that'll fix you"). Notice when past incidents are recalled to maintain guilt (e.g., "But don't forget what you did in 1951"). Identify guilt used to make a love partner conform (e.g., "If you were responsible, you would have called me"). Examples of guilt-inducing phrases include: "How do you expect me to be loving after what you've done?" and "How could I ever trust you again, when you let me down before?"

### Step 7: Recognize Children-Inspired Guilt Tactics

Recognize when children use guilt to manipulate parents (e.g., "Sally's father lets her do it," "You don't love me. If you did you wouldn't treat me this way," "I must be adopted. My real parents wouldn't treat me like this"). Understand that children learn this behavior by watching adults use it. A child's public tantrum may result in the child receiving what they want because the parent does not want to be seen as a "bad parent."

### Step 8: Recognize School-Inspired Guilt Tactics

Identify guilt messages from teachers and authority figures (e.g., "Your mother is really going to be disappointed in you," "You should be ashamed of yourself for getting a C—a smart boy like you," "How could you hurt your parents like that, after all they've done for you?"). Remember that even as an adult, you are a product of those schools and may still be affected by these messages. For example: "You failed the test because you didn't study, now you'll have to live with yourself."

### Step 9: Recognize Church-Related Guilt Tactics

Identify guilt messages tied to religious doctrine (e.g., "If you loved God, you wouldn't behave that way," "You won't get into heaven unless you repent for your sins," "You should feel bad because you haven't gone to church every week"). Recognize that these messages use the threat of divine punishment to create present-moment guilt. For example: "You've disobeyed one of God's rules and you should feel ashamed of yourself."

### Step 10: Recognize Other Institutional Guilt Producers

Recognize that prisons operate on the guilt theory — the idea that if a person sits long enough thinking how bad they have been, they will improve because of the guilt. Notice how tipping has become a practice reflecting the guilt of the person served rather than superior service. Be aware that littering, smoking, and other behaviors can be used to make you feel guilty, and that a stern look by a stranger can trigger feelings of guilt. Recognize dieting as an area loaded with guilt — if you give in to counterproductive behavior, learn from it rather than feeling guilty. Some service workers use blatant hand extending and negative comments to produce guilt and get bigger tips.

### Step 11: Understand the Psychological Payoffs for Choosing Guilt

Understand that guilt is an avoidance technique for working on yourself in the present — it shifts responsibility for what you are or are not now to what you were or were not in the past. Recognize that guilt helps you avoid the hard work of changing yourself now and the attendant risks that go with change. Notice the tendency to believe that if you feel guilty enough, you will eventually be exonerated for having been naughty. See that guilt can be a means of returning to the safety of childhood, where others made decisions for you. Recognize that guilt is a method for transferring responsibility for your behavior from yourself to others. Understand that guilt can help you win the approval of others even when they don't approve of your behavior. Recognize that guilt can be a way to win pity from others, indicating low self-esteem.

### Step 12: Use the Strategy — View the Past as Unchangeable

Begin to view the past as something that can never be changed, regardless of how you feel about it. Emblazon this sentence on your consciousness: "My feeling guilty will not change the past, nor will it make me a better person." Use this thinking to help differentiate guilt from learning as a result of your past. The chapter tells of a client who had an extramarital affair and felt guilty, but continued the affair—demonstrating that his guilt was easier than examining his marriage closely.

### Step 13: Use the Strategy — Ask What You Are Avoiding

Ask yourself what you are avoiding in the present with guilt about the past. Go to work on that particular thing, which will eliminate the need for guilt. The extramarital affair client illustrated this: he had two choices—recognize that guilt was easier than examining his marriage, or accept his behavior and live up to his own value system.

### Step 14: Use the Strategy — Accept Your Choices Despite Others' Disapproval

Begin to accept certain things about yourself that you've chosen but which others may dislike. See that it is natural for parents, employer, neighbors, or spouse to take a stand against some of your behavior. Remember that it is necessary that you approve of yourself; the approval of others is pleasant but not essential. Once you no longer need approval, the guilt for behavior which does not bring approval will disappear.

### Step 15: Use the Strategy — Keep a Guilt Journal

Keep a Guilt Journal and write down instances of guilt that arise. Note when the guilt occurs, why it occurs, and with whom it occurs. Document what you are avoiding in the present with this focus on the past. Use the journal to gain helpful insights into your particular guilt patterns.

### Step 16: Use the Strategy — Reconsider Your Value System

Reconsider your value system — determine which values you actually believe in and which you only pretend to accept. List values you pretend to hold. Resolve to live up to a code of ethics that is self-determined, not one imposed by others. As the chapter states: "List all of these phony values and resolve to live up to a code of ethics that is self-determined, not one that has been imposed by others."

### Step 17: Use the Strategy — Make a Guilt Points List

List significant past actions you regret. Assign yourself guilt points for each of them on a scale of one to ten. Add up your score. Observe that it makes no difference in the present whether the score is one hundred or one million — the present-moment is still the same, and all of your guilt is merely wasteful activity.

### Step 18: Use the Strategy — Assess Real Consequences Rather Than Emotional Feelings

Assess the real consequences of your behavior. Rather than looking for an emotional feeling to determine yes's and no's in your life, determine whether the results of your actions are pleasing and productive for you.

### Step 19: Use the Strategy — Teach Others They Cannot Manipulate You With Guilt

Teach those in your life who attempt to manipulate you with guilt that you are perfectly capable of handling their disappointment in you. Learn new responses that do not accept guilt (e.g., "Okay, Mom, if you want to risk your back on a few chairs because you can't wait a few minutes, I guess there is little I can do to dissuade you"). Understand that with repeated non-reinforcement, their behavior will change once they see they cannot force you to choose guilt. Once you defuse the guilt, the emotional control over you and the possibility of manipulation are eliminated.

### Step 20: Use the Strategy — Do Something That Will Result in Guilt

Do something which you know is bound to result in feelings of guilt. As you check into a hotel and a bellboy is assigned to show you to a room you can find alone, announce that you'll do it alone. If rejected, tell your unwanted companion that he is wasting his time and energy since you will not be leaving a tip for a service you don't want. Take a week to be alone if you've always wanted to do so, despite protestations from other family members. Use these behaviors to tackle the widespread guilt that many sectors of the environment are adept at helping you choose.

### Step 21: Use the Strategy — Role-Working Exercise to Defuse Parental Guilt

Engage in role-playing exercises to practice outmaneuvering guilt-producing statements. When responding to guilt-producing statements, begin responses with references to the other person as responsible for their own feelings (e.g., say "You feel" rather than "I feel") to reduce the potential for guilt. Practice refusing to yield to obvious guilt producers. Learn new responses that do not accept the guilt (e.g., "Is there anything you'd like to say to me before you leave?" in response to "I'm having a tachycardia attack right now. I think I'm going to die. You're killing me"). The chapter includes a dialogue between daughter and mother where the daughter's responses begin with "You feel" rather than "I feel," reducing the potential for guilt.

### Step 22: Understand Worry — Definition and Distinction From Planning

Understand that worry is defined as being immobilized in the present as a result of things that are going or not going to happen in the future. Do NOT confuse worrying with planning for the future — if the present-moment activity contributes to a more effective future, this is NOT worry. Recognize that worry is only present when you are immobilized in the present about a future happening. "If you are planning, and the present-moment activity will contribute to a more effective future, then this is not worry. It is worry only when you are in any way immobilized in the present about a future happening."

### Step 23: Recognize That Worry Does Not Equal Caring

Recognize the cultural equating of worrying with caring ("Of course I'm worried, it's only natural when you care about someone"). Understand that worry is not the same as love, which predicates a relationship in which each person has the right to be what they choose without any necessary conditions imposed by the other. Stop proving your love by worrying at the appropriate time. The chapter cites the Civil War example: "In 1975, some 115 years later, all of those worriers are dead and all their combined worrying did not change a moment of what is now history."

### Step 24: Use the Strategy — Ask What You Are Avoiding With Worry

When you catch yourself worrying, ask yourself: "What am I avoiding now by using up this moment with worry?" Begin to address whatever it is you're avoiding. Remember that the best antidote to worry is action. The chapter tells of a client who spent three hours playing tennis, touch football, and Ping-Pong while another man continued to worry about the stock market. Neither man's behavior had any effect on the stock market.

### Step 25: Use the Strategy — Recognize the Preposterousness of Worry

Ask yourself repeatedly: "Is there anything that will ever change as a result of my worrying about it?" Repeatedly confront yourself with this question to see the futility of worry.

### Step 26: Use the Strategy — Designate "Worry-Time"

Designate ten minutes in the morning and afternoon as your worry periods. Use these periods to consider every potential concern you can fit into the time slot. Using your ability to control your own thoughts, postpone any further worry until your next designated "worry-time." You will see the waste of using any time in this fashion and will eventually reduce your worry significantly.

### Step 27: Use the Strategy — Make a Worry List

Make a worry list of everything you worried about yesterday, last week, and even last year. See if any of your worry did anything productive for you. Assess how many of the things you worried about ever materialized at all. Recognize that worry is doubly wasteful: it does nothing to alter the future, and the projected negative outcome sometimes turns out to be minor or even positive when it arrives. The chapter includes a complete Worry Sheet listing 27 common worries (children, health, dying, job, economy, heart attack, security, spouse's happiness, doing the right thing, healthy child, prices, accidents, what others think, weight, money, car breaking down, bills, parents dying, getting into heaven, weather, getting old, flying, daughter's virginity, talking in front of groups, spouse not calling, going into the city, having nothing to worry about).

### Step 28: Use the Strategy — "Just Worry!" Demonstration

When tempted to worry, stop and turn to someone and say, "Watch me—I'm about to worry." You may find it difficult to demonstrate worry since worry is not a tangible, demonstrable behavior.

### Step 29: Use the Strategy — The Worry-Eradicating Question

Ask yourself: "What's the worst thing that could happen to me (or them) and what is the likelihood of it occurring?" This can help you see the futility of worry by honestly evaluating both the severity and probability of your feared outcome.

### Step 30: Use the Strategy — Act in Direct Conflict With Your Worry Areas

Deliberately choose to act in a manner that is directly in conflict with your usual areas of worry. If you compulsively save for the future, worried about having enough money, begin to use your money today. Adopt the mindset: "Being of sound mind, I spent all my money while I was alive." The chapter uses the image of the rich uncle's will: "Being of sound mind, I spent all my money while I was alive."

### Step 31: Use the Strategy — Face Your Fears With Productive Behavior

Begin to face the fears you possess with productive thought and behavior. Identify a specific fear or worry (e.g., fear of wild dogs). Prepare yourself with a sense of confidence (e.g., carry a rock for protection). Refuse to show fear or back down when confronted with the feared situation. Addressing a fear or worry directly is one productive way to reduce it from your life. The chapter tells of a woman who carried a rock to address her fear of wild dogs on an island off the coast of Connecticut. She refused to slow down when the dogs growled and came running toward her. As the dogs charged forward and encountered someone who refused to back down, they gave up and ran away.

### Step 32: Live in the Present Moment — The Core Principle

Learn to live now and not waste your current moments in immobilizing thoughts about the past or future. Recognize that there is no other moment to live but the present, and all futile guilt and worry are done in the present moment. Apply this understanding to both guilt (past) and worry (future) as they both take time from the present moment. Lewis Carroll's Alice Through the Looking Glass makes the point: "The rule is, jam tomorrow, and jam yesterday…but never jam today." "It must come sometimes to 'jam-today,'" Alice objected. The challenge: "Any jam today? Since it must come sometime, how about now?"

## Best Practices

- ✅ Distinguish between learning from the past (productive, present-focused) and guilt (immobilizing, past-focused)
- ✅ Keep a Guilt Journal to track patterns of guilt triggers and what you are avoiding in the present
- ✅ Ask "What am I avoiding now?" whenever you catch yourself feeling guilty or worried
- ✅ Designate specific "worry-time" periods to contain your worry rather than letting it consume your day
- ✅ Teach others they cannot manipulate you with guilt by calmly responding without accepting the guilt
- ✅ Act in direct conflict with your worry areas to test whether the feared outcomes are manageable
- ✅ Face fears with productive behavior rather than avoidance
- ✅ Live in the present moment — there is no other moment to live but now
- ❌ Do not confuse worrying with planning — planning contributes to the future; worry immobilizes the present
- ❌ Do not believe that feeling guilty enough will exonerate you or undo the past
- ❌ Do not accept that worry equals caring — worry is not the same as love
- ❌ Do not allow others to use "If you loved me" or self-sacrifice stories to manipulate your behavior
- ❌ Do not keep score of grievances or try to maintain an informal record of fairness in relationships
- ❌ Do not let institutional messages (church, school, family) impose guilt that does not serve your values

## Keep In Mind

- Guilt and worry are similar in their function: both immobilize you in the present moment
- Guilt cannot undo anything that happened in the past
- The past can never be changed, regardless of how you feel about it
- Worry is doubly wasteful: it does nothing to alter the future, and the projected negative outcome can turn out to be minor or even positive
- "My feeling guilty will not change the past, nor will it make me a better person."
- "If you are planning, and the present-moment activity will contribute to a more effective future, then this is not worry."
- Guilt is not a natural behavior — it is a learned emotional response that can only be used if the victim teaches the exploiter that they are vulnerable
- An effective approach to both guilt and worry is action in the present moment
- There is no other moment to live but now
- "Any jam today? Since it must come sometime, how about now?"

## Security & Safety Notes

This chapter addresses emotional and psychological well-being rather than digital or financial security. The strategies described are self-help exercises intended for personal growth. If you are experiencing severe guilt, anxiety, or worry that significantly impairs your daily functioning, consider seeking support from a qualified mental health professional. The chapter's advice to "do something that will result in guilt" as an exposure exercise should be applied thoughtfully and at a pace that is safe for you.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You confuse learning from past mistakes with guilt and continue to immobilize yourself with harsh self-judgment.
  **Solution:** Ask whether you are unable to take action now as a result of past behavior (guilt) or actively planning to avoid repeating it (learning). If it is guilt, use the Guilt Journal to identify what you are avoiding in the present and take action on it instead.

- **Problem:** You accept guilt manipulation from parents, partners, or children because you fear being seen as selfish or ungrateful.
  **Solution:** Respond using "You feel" language rather than "I feel" language, making the other person responsible for their own feelings. Practice new responses until the guilt producers in your life learn they cannot control you this way.

- **Problem:** You worry about the future and mistake this anxiety for caring or responsible planning.
  **Solution:** Ask "Is there anything that will ever change as a result of my worrying about it?" If the present-moment activity does not contribute to a more effective future, it is worry — not planning. Use designated "worry-time" periods to contain it, and shift to productive action as the approach to addressing concerns.

- **Problem:** You demand fairness and keep an informal record in relationships, which breeds ongoing resentment and prevents genuine connection.
  **Solution:** Recognize that perfect balance is not required in personal relationships. Focus on whether your record-keeping is making you happier or more miserable, and choose to live by your own internal standards rather than external comparisons.
