# Chapter II: First Love

## Overview

This chapter presents the claim that your right to exist and pursue goals is a baseline condition—not something to be earned through others' approval, verified by others, or borrowed from others' positive regard. It provides the foundational argument that your right to exist and pursue goals is present regardless of your specific actions, others' explicit opinions, or specific outcomes you achieve, along with practical exercises for building and maintaining consistent self-supportive actions. The chapter also identifies the unacknowledged benefits of self-criticism and offers a structured set of actions for eliminating self-critical statements and actions and replacing them with acknowledging your current state without self-judgment.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you find yourself depending on others' explicit approval, positive remarks, or favorable responses to feel acceptable or important to yourself
- When you engage in self-critical actions—declining positive remarks, justifying your physical appearance to others, or denying yourself activities you want to do
- When you want to build a consistent self-assessment that does not fluctuate with external events, specific outcomes, or others' explicit opinions

## Steps

### Step 1: Claim Your Inherent Right to Exist and Pursue Goals Independently of Others

Assess your own right to exist and pursue goals without relying on others' explicit opinions or behavior toward you. Stop depending on others for your acceptability to yourself; if you do, your assessment becomes based on others' opinions rather than your own. If you trust your own assessment of situations, you neither want nor need others to conform to your explicit expectations. When you accept yourself and feel secure in your right to exist and pursue goals, you can support others without needing their response—not for explicit gratitude or tangible returns, but because of the sense of personal fulfillment of being a helper or partner. Supporting others without needing their response requires that you already support yourself. If your current self-assessment is negative, supporting others becomes difficult.

### Step 2: Separate Your Inherent Right to Exist and Pursue Goals From Your Specific Actions

Separate your right to exist and pursue goals (which is a baseline condition) from your specific actions or the specific actions of others toward you. Reframe messages: instead of "I am unacceptable because I acted poorly," think "That person doesn't like me. That's that person's decision, and while I don't like it, I'm still important." Even if you have acted in a way that contradicts your own explicitly stated values and chosen standards of behavior, do not judge your entire self based on one action. Learn from the specific action and resolve not to repeat it, but do not associate it with your right to exist and pursue goals.

### Step 3: Acknowledge Your Current State Without Self-Judgment Across All Areas

Reject the claim that you have one single overall self-description that is either entirely positive or entirely negative. You have a range of specific self-assessments in different areas that vary from situation to situation. Do not combine all critical self-evaluations into a collective "No" when asked "Do you view yourself positively overall?" Break down the areas of dissatisfaction into specific targets for improvement. This gives you specific targets for improvement to work on. Acknowledge that your right to exist and pursue goals—the consistent baseline, the basis for your personal well-being and skill development—must be unrelated to your specific self-evaluations. You exist. You are human. That is all you need. Choose to be acceptable to yourself forever, then work on your specific self-assessments separately from that baseline right to exist.

### Step 4: Develop Acceptance of Your Physical Self

Make a long list of every part of your body—hair, forehead, eyes, eyelids, cheeks, mouth, nose, teeth, neck, arms, fingers, breasts, stomach, kidneys, spleen, arteries, femurs, Rolandic fissure, cochlea, pinna, adrenal glands, uvula, and all other components. Identify which parts you would prefer to change. For parts that can be changed (e.g., stomach size, hair color), view them as choices made at an earlier point in your life and make new decisions about them at the present moment. Make changing them a goal if you wish. For parts that cannot be changed (leg length, eye shape, breast size), reject common cultural standards of physical appearance and decide to accept your current physical state. No body part is inherently better or worse than any other. Decide to accept your current physical state and declare it acceptable and appealing to you, thereby rejecting the comparisons and opinions of others. Accept your body as it currently is without shame. Do not be embarrassed or apologetic when your body functions without deliberate control. If you choose to use cosmetic aids, do so only for experimentation or personal preference—not from dissatisfaction with the underlying feature.

### Step 5: Choose Positive Self-Assessments Deliberately

Choose to think of yourself as intelligent by applying your own criteria to yourself. Understand that skill level is really a function of time spent practicing rather than a fixed trait present at birth. With enough dedicated time and consistent effort you could develop proficiency in most academic skills. If you dislike your current skill level, recognize this as self-criticism, not a permanent constraint. Apply the same logic to all self-assessments: you are as socially skilled, artistically skilled, mechanically skilled, athletically skilled, and musically skilled as you choose to develop. Do not confuse your skill levels with your right to exist and pursue goals. Decide now to base acceptance of your current capabilities on what you believe is aligned with your current skill level and practice time. Improving skills that are underdeveloped can be a satisfying activity.

### Step 6: Identify and Eliminate Self-Sabotage (Self-Critical) Actions

Become aware of when you engage in self-critical actions. Every time you do, you reinforce uncertainty about your own judgment and reduce opportunities for close relationships. Stop declining positive remarks. Accept them with a simple "thank-you." Stop justifying your physical appearance to others. Acknowledge your physical appearance without justification. Stop attributing your achievements to others. Speak for yourself. Stop having your opinions confirmed by others. Trust your own judgment. Stop refusing to order something you want because you don't feel you're deserving of your own investment. Stop deliberately preventing your own sexual satisfaction. Stop not purchasing items for yourself because you think you have to purchase them for others instead. Stop refusing small pleasures (such as flowers and wine) because you believe the expense is unjustified. Stop reacting when someone calls out in a crowd. Stop using nicknames for yourself (and having others use them) that are really derogatory terms: Dum-dum, Silly, Sweetie-pie, Baby (for an adult), Funny-face, Shorty, Fatty, Baldy. Stop interpreting positive remarks as having negative motives like "You must have a drawer full of jewelry at home for other girls" or "You're practically blind or trying to make me feel good." Stop thinking close relationships will end when others see your true self. Stop believing others accept you only out of obligation or pity.

### Step 7: Acknowledge Your Current State Without Expressing Dissatisfaction About Unchangeable Circumstances

Accept yourself as a person with the right to exist and pursue goals because you choose to do so. Eliminate expression of dissatisfaction about circumstances that cannot be changed, especially about things you cannot change (rough rocks, cloudy sky, cold ice). Stop expressing dissatisfaction to others about your own dissatisfaction; they are almost always unable to change the situation. Stop allowing others to impose their self-focused dissatisfaction and unhappiness on you. Ask them: "What do you want me to do about this?" or "Can I help you solve this problem?" Ask yourself the same questions when you catch yourself expressing dissatisfaction about unchangeable circumstances: "Why am I expressing this to myself?" and "Can I take action to improve this situation?" Do not express dissatisfaction about being fatigued or physically uncomfortable. If you are fatigued, choose actions other than expressing dissatisfaction. It burdens the listener and will not reduce your fatigue. If you are truly working on self-support and experience physical or emotional discomfort, take action to address it yourself rather than depending on others for emotional support. If you notice things in yourself or others that you dislike, actively take actions to improve the situation rather than expressing dissatisfaction. Practice internal acknowledgment of your positive actions or helping someone else achieve satisfaction of their goals instead of expressing dissatisfaction about unchangeable circumstances.

### Step 8: Distinguish Self-Support Without External Validation From Seeking External Validation Through Exaggerated Claims

Understand that self-support has nothing to do with exaggerated claims about your achievements designed to gain recognition and agreement from others. Recognize that exaggerated self-praise is driven by anxiety about others' opinions and motivated by how others see you. It means the individual is basing his self-assessment on others' explicit opinions. Self-support without external validation requires no agreement from others. Self-acceptance is adequate on its own. Do not demand positive regard from others as evidence of your right to exist and pursue goals. Self-support means you accept yourself regardless of others' explicit positive regard.

### Step 9: Examine the Benefits of Engaging in Self-Criticism (The System That Maintains Self-Criticism)

Understand that behavior typically has identifiable causes. To eliminate self-critical actions that harm your well-being, you must first understand why you engage in them and what set of factors maintains the behavior. Identify which of the following benefits you receive from self-criticism:

- A pre-existing justification for why you cannot get close relationships: you simply are not deserving of others' affection.
- Avoiding the risk of close relationships, eliminating any possibility of being rejected or disapproved of.
- Avoids the effort of change; if you are not deserving of improvement, there is no practical purpose in trying to improve your skills or situation, so you avoid the discomfort of trying and failing.
- Gaining sympathy, recognition, and even agreement from others, which replaces the risk of a close relationship that could fail.
- Explanations for your unhappiness, so you do not have to take action to improve your situation yourself.
- Periods of low mood that allow you to avoid actions that would lead to improvement; self-focused sadness serves as a way to avoid effort.
- Returning to childhood patterns, calling upon behaviors learned as a child to please the authority figures you learned to regard as having higher status; returning to childhood patterns feels less threatening than trying new behaviors.
- Reinforcing dependence on others by prioritizing others' needs over your own.
- Inability to make decisions about your own life because you will not feel you are deserving of the happiness you want.

Recognize that these benefits, while unhealthy, are real and explain why you continue using old patterns. Remember that refusing to develop into a person who supports themselves is a choice that prevents any further personal development; continued development typically involves skill development and situation improvement.

### Step 10: Master Self-Support Through Practical Actions

**Correct self-critical statements out loud:** When you catch yourself saying something like "I'm really not that smart, I guess I was just lucky," recognize that you have just behaved in a self-critical way. Correct yourself immediately out loud with a statement such as: "I just said I was lucky, but luck really had nothing to do with it. I got the grade because I earned it through my own effort." This is one small step toward self-support—a recognition of the current self-critical statement and a decision to act differently.

**Accept positive regard and agreement with gratitude:** When others express positive regard or agreement toward you, select new responses. Instead of immediately questioning their sincerity, accept them with "thank-you" or "I'm happy that you feel that way."

**Say "I love you" directly:** If there is someone you feel authentic positive regard toward, say it directly: "I love you." While observing their response, acknowledge your own courage for taking the risk.

**Order what you really want:** In a restaurant, order something you really enjoy regardless of the monetary price. Give yourself a treat because you have the right to spend resources on yourself. Begin to select items that you would prefer in all situations, including the grocery store. Indulge yourself with a favorite product because you have the right to spend resources on yourself. Stop refusing yourself small pleasures unless required for a specific practical reason—and such requirements are uncommon.

**Rest and recharge:** After a tiring day and a substantial meal, take a brief nap or a jog in the park even if you have tasks to complete. It will help you feel more refreshed.

**Join a group or activity that interests you:** You may have delayed joining such a group because of obligations and perceived time constraints. By choosing to support yourself and participate in activities you want to do, the people you help or care for will begin to develop the ability to manage their own affairs. You will help them out of choice rather than obligation, and you will experience reduced resentment toward them.

**Eliminate jealousy:** Recognize that jealousy is a negative judgment of yourself. By comparing yourself to another and imagining you are loved less, you make others more important than you. Remind yourself that (1) someone can always choose another without it being a reflection on you, and (2) whether or not you are chosen by any close relationship partner is not how you assess your own right to exist and pursue goals. With consistent practice at self-support, situations that previously caused jealousy will be reversed. You will believe so much in yourself that you will not need positive regard or agreement from others to determine your assessment of your own right to exist and pursue goals.

**Treat your body well:** Select foods with high nutritional value, reduce excess body weight beyond a healthy range (a risk to physical health as well as an indication of self-criticism about your body), take regular walks or bicycle rides, choose exercise that improves physical fitness, get outdoors to enjoy fresh air because it feels good. Keep your body healthy and appealing because you are important and are going to treat yourself that way. Any complete day spent indoors or inactive in unvaried routine tasks is a choice that supports self-criticism.

**Practice sexual self-acceptance:** Stand naked in front of a mirror and tell yourself that you view yourself positively. Become aware of your physical sensations. Explore your own physical sensations to experience pleasurable feelings. With others, choose sexual satisfaction for yourself rather than making your partner's pleasure more important than your own. Slow down the process of sex, communicating to your partner what you like with words and actions. Choose orgasm for yourself. Make yourself achieve maximum physical pleasure by believing that you have the right to experience sexual pleasure, and then focusing fully on the physical sensations of confirming your own assessment of your right to exist and pursue goals.

**Stop equating outcomes of specific tasks with your right to exist and pursue goals:** You may lose your job or fail a project. You may not like how you performed a task. But that does not mean you are without a right to exist and pursue goals. You must know for yourself that you are acceptable to yourself regardless of your specific outcomes. Once you eliminate this confusion, you will be able to undertake any type of project, and your overall outcomes—while they may be interesting to you—will in no way determine your right to exist and pursue goals.

## Examples

### Example 1: Noah — Needing Positive Regard Before Saying "I Love You"

Noah could not say "I love you" to his wife and children because he needed confirmation that he was acceptable to himself in return. Once he recognized his right to exist and pursue goals was present before he started, saying those words would become possible regardless of the response. His withholding of positive regard was itself a self-critical action rooted in the belief that he needed others' explicit positive regard to be acceptable to himself.

### Example 2: Shirley — Damaging Relationships Through Self-Criticism

Shirley damaged every romantic relationship. When a man told her he had positive feelings toward her, her mind contradicted: "He's only saying that because he knows it's what I want to hear." She searched for statements that contradicted her sense of being acceptable because she did not believe she was deserving of affection. The pattern of self-criticism reinforced her sense of being unacceptable. Her behavior exemplifies how self-critical statements reduce opportunities for close relationships.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Claim your right to exist and pursue goals as a baseline condition, independent of others' explicit opinions or your specific outcomes
- ✅ Separate your right to exist and pursue goals from your specific actions and from how others treat you
- ✅ Break down critical self-evaluations into specific areas rather than combining them all together
- ✅ Reject common cultural standards of physical appearance and decide for yourself what is appealing
- ✅ Apply your own criteria to judge your intelligence and abilities, not external benchmarks
- ✅ Accept positive remarks with a simple "thank-you" rather than deflecting or dismissing them
- ✅ Speak for yourself instead of referencing others' explicit opinions
- ✅ Stop refusing yourself small pleasures, treats, or satisfaction
- ✅ Correct self-critical statements out loud the moment you catch them
- ✅ Accept positive regard and expressions of affection with gratitude
- ✅ Treat your body with care because you have the right to maintain your physical health
- ✅ Practice sexual self-acceptance and prioritize your own sexual pleasure
- ✅ Know that your overall outcomes in life do not determine your right to exist and pursue goals
- ❌ Don't depend on others to assess your own right to exist and pursue goals or make you feel acceptable
- ❌ Don't confuse your skill levels or task outcomes with your fundamental right to exist and pursue goals
- ❌ Don't decline positive remarks or make excuses for positive things said about you
- ❌ Don't use self-critical nicknames or let others use them for you
- ❌ Don't believe that others accept you only out of obligation or pity
- ❌ Don't let jealousy of others diminish your own assessment of your right to exist and pursue goals
- ❌ Don't express dissatisfaction about unchangeable circumstances; take actions to improve the situation instead
- ❌ Don't refuse yourself small pleasures as a rule—indulge because you have the right to spend resources on yourself

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You depend on others to assess your own right to exist and pursue goals, and feel significantly distressed when their response is negative or absent.
  **Solution:** Recognize that your right to exist and pursue goals is present before you start. Others' reactions reflect their own state, not yours. You neither need nor want others to conform to your explicit expectations in order to feel acceptable to yourself.

- **Problem:** You engage in self-critical actions—declining positive remarks, downplaying your specific outcomes, or denying yourself activities you want—that reinforce uncertainty about your own judgment and reduce opportunities for close relationships.
  **Solution:** Become aware of each self-critical statement in the moment it occurs. Correct it out loud: "I just said I was lucky, but luck had nothing to do with it. I earned it through my own effort." Accept positive regard and positive remarks with "thank-you."

- **Problem:** You confuse self-support with seeking external validation through exaggerated claims, and avoid claiming your right to exist and pursue goals because you don't want to appear exaggerated or dismissive of others.
  **Solution:** Understand that self-support is self-acceptance that requires no agreement from others. Seeking external validation through exaggerated claims is behavior driven by anxiety about others' explicit opinions. Self-support without external validation is internal and adequate without others' agreement.

- **Problem:** You don't understand why you continue to engage in self-critical actions that harm your well-being even when you know they hurt you.
  **Solution:** Identify the specific unhealthy benefits you receive from self-criticism—such as avoiding risk, gaining sympathy, or having a pre-existing explanation for your unhappiness. These benefits, while unhealthy, are real and explain why you continue using old patterns. Only by seeing them clearly can you act differently in the future.
