# Chapter I: Taking Charge of Yourself

## Overview

This chapter teaches that you are the sole decider of your emotions, behavior, and life direction. Happiness is a choice made in each present moment through the thoughts you allow into your head, not a condition imposed upon you by circumstances or other people. The chapter provides actionable tools—including a syllogism exercise, a stick-shift analogy for learning new thinking patterns, and an immobilization checklist—to help you eliminate self-defeating emotions and take full responsibility for your feelings.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you feel that outside events or other people are making you unhappy and want to reclaim control over your own emotional experience
- When you recognize self-defeating patterns—such as anxiety, anger, or passivity—that keep you from functioning at the level you would like
- When you want to stop living according to others' expectations and begin living each present moment intentionally and happily

## Steps

### Step 1: Use Your Own Death as a Motivator to Take Charge of Your Life

When contemplating a decision about whether to take charge of yourself and make your own choice, ask yourself: "How long am I going to be dead?" With that eternal perspective, make your own choice and leave the worrying, fears, questions of whether you can afford it, and guilt to those who are going to be alive forever. If you don't begin taking these steps, you will live your entire life the way others say you must. Live your life in a way that is pleasing to you, since it is your life to do with as you want.

### Step 2: Redefine Intelligence Based on Happiness, Not Problem-Solving

Stop measuring intelligence by your ability to solve complex problems, read, write, compute, or resolve abstract equations quickly. Stop letting formal education and bookish excellence be the true measures of self-fulfillment. Measure intelligence by an effective, happy life lived each day and each present moment of every day. If you are happy and live each moment for everything it's worth, you are an intelligent person. If you know that given your inability to resolve a particular concern you can still choose happiness for yourself, or at a minimum refuse to choose unhappiness, you are intelligent.

### Step 3: Recognize That You Control Your Emotions Through Your Thoughts

Accept the radical notion that you can control your own emotions; that anger, fear, hate, as well as love, ecstasy, and joy are things you choose to feel, not things that happen to you. Do not accept emotions passively; choose them actively. When sorrowful events occur, do not just naturally feel sorrow and hope that happy events will come along; choose how to feel. See a given emotion as a choice rather than as a condition of life.

### Step 4: Use the Syllogism Exercise to Prove You Can Control Your Feelings

Learn the structure of a logical syllogism (major premise, minor premise, conclusion). Use the following syllogism as proof that you can control your feelings:

- MAJOR PREMISE: I can control my thoughts.
- MINOR PREMISE: My feelings come from my thoughts.
- CONCLUSION: I can control my feelings.

Accept the major premise: You have the power to think whatever you choose to allow into your head. If something just "pops" into your head, you still have the power to make it go away, and therefore you still control your mental world. Answer the question: "If you don't control your thoughts, who does?" If others control what you think, then they should go for therapy and you will instantly get better.

Accept the minor premise: You cannot have a feeling (emotion) without first having experienced a thought. Take away your brain and your ability to "feel" is wiped out. A feeling is a physical reaction to a thought.

Accept the conclusion: If you control your thoughts, and your feelings come from your thoughts, then you are capable of controlling your own feelings. You control your feelings by working on the thoughts that preceded them. Simply put, you believe that things or people make you unhappy, but this is not accurate. You make yourself unhappy because of the thoughts that you have about the people or things in your life.

Becoming a free and healthy person involves learning to think differently. Once you can change your thoughts, your new feelings will begin to emerge, and you will have taken the first step on the road to your personal freedom.

### Step 5: Rewrite Self-Defeating Statements to Take Responsibility for Your Feelings

Identify the self-defeating statements from List 1 that you have used:

- "You hurt my feelings."
- "You make me feel bad."
- "I can't help the way I feel."
- "I just feel angry, don't ask me to explain it."
- "He makes me sick."
- "Heights scare me."
- "You're embarrassing me."
- "She really turns me on."
- "You made a fool of me in public."

Rewrite each statement to reflect that you are in charge of how you feel and that your feelings come from the thoughts you have about anything:

- "I hurt my feelings because of the things I told myself about your reaction to me."
- "I made myself feel bad."
- "I can help the way I feel, but I've chosen to be upset."
- "I've decided to be angry, because I can usually manipulate others with my anger, since they think I control them."
- "I make myself sick."
- "I scare myself at high places."
- "I'm embarrassing myself."
- "I turn myself on whenever I'm near her."
- "I made myself feel foolish by taking your opinions of me more seriously than my own, and believing that others would do the same."

Examine the message each old statement sends; it is that you are not responsible for how you feel. Examine why the statements in List 2 did not evolve into clichés. The answer lies in our culture, which teaches the thinking of List 1 and discourages the logic of List 2. Ask yourself if there is a sufficient payoff in being unhappy, down, or hurt. Then begin to examine, in depth, the kind of thoughts that are leading you to these debilitating feelings.

### Step 6: Learn Not to Be Unhappy Through Awareness and Repetition (The Stick Shift Analogy)

Accept that it is not easy to think in new ways; you are accustomed to a certain set of thoughts and the debilitating thoughts that follow. Understand that happiness is easy, but learning not to be unhappy can be difficult. Recognize that taking charge of yourself begins with awareness. Catch yourself when you say things like, "He hurt my feelings." Remind yourself what you're doing at the moment you're doing it. Understand that new thinking requires awareness of the old thinking.

Realize that you have become habituated in mental patterns that identify the causes of your feelings as outside of yourself. You have put in thousands of hours of reinforcement for such thinking, and you'll need to balance the scale with thousands of hours of new thinking, thinking that assumes responsibility for your own feelings. Apply the same process used to learn physical tasks (like driving a stick shift) to the emotional world: awareness, then after thousands of trials, mistakes, new efforts, the day comes when the new behavior becomes second nature. Repeat endlessly that your mind really is your own and that you are capable of controlling your own feelings.

Taking charge of yourself involves more than simply trying on new thoughts for size. It requires a determination to be happy and to challenge and destroy each and every thought that creates a self-immobilizing unhappiness in you. Practice, practice, practice, never yielding to the temptation to quit or slack off.

### Step 7: Choose Happiness Even When It Seems Impossible (The Punishment Thought Experiment)

Imagine that each time you become unhappy, you are subjected to some treatment you find unpleasant: locked in a room alone for long periods of time, forced into a crowded elevator where you must stand for days, deprived of all food, forced to eat a dish you find particularly distasteful, or physically tortured by others rather than mentally tortured by yourself.

Imagine that you were subjected to any one of these punishments until you made your unhappy feelings go away. Ask yourself: How long do you think you would continue to hold on to them? Recognize that you would take control rather quickly. Accept that the issue is not whether you can take control of your feelings, but whether you will. Ask yourself: What must you endure before you'll make such a choice?

### Step 8: Choose Self-Fulfilling Behavior Over Self-Defeating Behavior in Everyday Life

When stuck in traffic, do not become angry, swear at other drivers, berate your passengers, or take your feelings out on anyone and anything in range. Do not justify your behavior by saying that traffic always sends you into a snit or that you simply have no control in a traffic jam. Instead, decide to think something else and use your mind in a self-enhancing kind of way. Talk to yourself in new ways and become accustomed to new behavior which might include whistling, singing, turning on a tape recorder to write verbal letters, even timing yourself with thirty-second postponements of your anger. Decide not to be uncomfortable. Choose to substitute, in slow progressive steps, healthy new feelings and habits for old self-defeating emotions.

When bored at dull parties or committee meetings, make your mind work in exciting ways by changing the subject with a key observation, or writing the first chapter of your novel, or working on new plans which will help you avoid these settings in the future. Using your mind actively means assessing the people and events which give you the greatest difficulty and then deciding on new mental efforts to make them work for you. In a restaurant, if you typically get upset over poor service, think first of why you should not choose to be upset because someone or something is not going the way you would like it to go. You're too worthy to be upset by someone else, especially someone who is so unimportant in your life. Devise strategies to change the setting, or leave, or whatever. But don't just get perturbed. Use your brain to work for you, and eventually you'll have the terrific habit of not being upset when things go wrong.

### Step 9: Choose Health Over Illness for Physical Sufferings Without Organic Dysfunction

Recognize that you can choose to eliminate some physical sufferings which are not rooted in a known organic dysfunction, including headaches, backaches, ulcers, hypertension, rashes, skin eruptions, cramps, fleeting pains and the like. Understand that you may have chosen physical maladies as a means of being noticed and as a means of receiving sympathy and pity. If you want the headache, you have actually chosen it. Learn not to want this for yourself and practice making the headache shift from one location to another in your head. Learn that you control the headache by making it move around. Decide not to choose headaches (or other chosen ailments) completely.

### Step 10: Recognize and Eliminate Immobilization in Your Life

Use the word "immobilization" as the indicator of negative emotions in your life. Understand that immobilization can range from total inaction to mild indecision and hesitancy. Ask yourself: Does your anger keep you from saying, feeling, or doing something? If so, then you are immobilized. Ask yourself: Does your shyness prevent you from meeting people you want to know? If so, you are immobilized and missing out on experiences that are rightfully yours. Ask yourself: Is your hate and jealousy helping you to grow an ulcer or to raise your blood pressure? Does it keep you from working effectively on the job? Are you unable to sleep or make love because of a negative present-moment feeling? These are all signs of immobilization.

Understand the definition: Immobilization is a state, however mild or serious, in which you are not functioning at the level that you would like to. If feelings lead to such a state, you need to look no further for a reason to get rid of them. Use the following checklist to identify when you are immobilized:

- You can't talk lovingly to your spouse and children though you want to.
- You can't work on a project that interests you.
- You don't make love and would like to.
- You sit in the house all day and brood.
- You don't play golf, tennis, or other enjoyable activities, because of a leftover gnawing feeling.
- You can't introduce yourself to someone who appeals to you.
- You avoid talking to someone when you realize that a simple gesture would improve your relationship.
- You can't sleep because something is bothering you.
- Your anger keeps you from thinking clearly.
- You say something abusive to someone that you love.
- Your face is twitching, or you are so nervous that you don't function the way you would prefer.

Understand that virtually all negative emotions result in some degree of self-immobility, and this alone is a solid reason for eliminating them entirely from your life. Recognize the exception: If an angry voice is simply a device for emphasis and it works (e.g., yelling at a young child to emphasize that you do not want him to play in the street), then you've adopted a healthy strategy. However, if you yell at others not to make a point, but because you are internally upset, then you've immobilized yourself, and it's time to begin working at new choices.

### Step 11: Live in the Present Moment

Combat immobilization by learning to live in the present moment. Get in touch with your "now." Recognize that there really is no other moment you can ever live. Now is all there is, and the future is just another present moment to live when it arrives. You cannot live it until it does appear. Do not sacrifice the present for the future. Avoid the culture that teaches: "Save for the future! Consider the consequences. Don't be a hedonist. Think of tomorrow. Plan for your retirement."

Avoid the disease of present-moment avoidance, which assumes many forms:

- Do not go into the woods to soak up nature and let your mind wander focusing on all of the things you should be doing back home (kids, groceries, house, bills) or all of the things you'll have to do when you get out of the woods.
- Do not go on vacation basking in the sun in anticipation of what your friends back home will say when you return with a magnificent tan.
- Do not let your mind run on past or future events while experiencing the present moment with your spouse.
- Do not read while your mind is on a mental excursion, avoiding the material even while your eyes focus on each word.

Drink in all of every moment and tune out the past which is over and the future which will arrive in time. Seize the present moment as the only one you have. Remember that wishing, hoping, and regretting are the most common and dangerous tactics for evading the present. Avoid idealizing the future, thinking that at some miraculous moment in the future life will change, everything will fall into place, and you will find happiness. When the future arrives, it will be the present, and you must use it to prepare for the next future. Do not let the vicious circle of idealizing the future, being disappointed when it arrives, and then idealizing again become your lifestyle. Interrupt it with some strategic present-moment fulfillment.

Follow Henry James's advice from The Ambassadors: "Live all you can; it's a mistake not to. It doesn't so much matter what you do in particular, so long as you have your life. If you haven't had that what have you had?…What one loses one loses, make no mistake about that…. The right time is any time that one is still so lucky as to have…. Live!" Develop an appreciation for the present moment. Seize every second of your life and savor it. Value your present moments. Using them up in any self-defeating ways means you've lost them forever.

### Step 12: Choose Growth Motivation Over Imperfection (Deficiency) Motivation

Understand the two types of motivation: imperfection or deficiency motivation, and growth motivation. Choose to be motivated out of a desire to grow rather than a need to repair your deficiencies. Recognize that if you recognize that you can always grow, improve, become more and greater, that is enough. When you decide to be immobilized or to experience hurtful emotions, you've made a nongrowth decision.

Growth motivation means using your life energy for greater happiness, rather than having to improve yourself because you've sinned or because you are in some way incomplete. Choose personal mastery in every present moment of your life. Mastery means you are the decider of your fate; you are not a coper, or a striver, or someone who adjusts to the world. Rather you choose what your world will be for you.

Follow George Bernard Shaw's principle: "People are always blaming their circumstances for what they are. I don't believe in circumstances. The people who get on in this world are the people who get up and look for the circumstances they want, and if they can't find them, make them." Apply the same rigid application to unlearning self-defeating thinking that you would give to the learning of any difficult enterprise. If you really want to be neurosis-free, self-fulfilled and in control of your own choices, and achieve present-moment happiness, you will need to repeat endlessly that your mind really is your own and that you are capable of controlling your own feelings.

### Step 13: Recognize That You Are Responsible for How You Feel (The Culture of Victimhood)

Recognize that you have grown up in a culture which has taught you that you are not responsible for your feelings. Identify the host of sayings you have used over and over to defend yourself against the fact that you do control your feelings. Examine the message each saying sends: that you are not responsible for how you feel. Understand that you feel what you think, and you can learn to think differently about anything—if you decide to do so.

Ask yourself if there is a sufficient payoff in being unhappy, down, or hurt. Then begin to examine, in depth, the kind of thoughts that are leading you to these debilitating feelings. Reject the cultural messages that imply you are a helpless prisoner who must have someone or something else do things for you. Only you can improve your lot or make yourself happy. Take control of your own mind, and then practice feeling and behaving in the ways that you choose.

### Step 14: Distinguish Between Negative Emotions That Have a Payoff and Those That Are Purely Immobilizing

Recognize that you might believe that anger, hostility, shyness, and other similar feelings are worth having at times. Use immobilization as your guide: if the feeling immobilizes you in any way, it is negative and should be eliminated. Recognize that if an angry voice is simply a device for emphasis and it works (e.g., yelling at a young child to emphasize that you do not want him to play in the street), then you've adopted a healthy strategy. However, if you yell at others not to make a point, but because you are internally upset, then you've immobilized yourself, and it's time to begin working at new choices that will help you reach your goal without experiencing feelings that are hurtful to you.

## Examples

### Example 1: Tolstoy's Ivan Ilych — Living a Life Controlled by Others

Tolstoy's Ivan Ilych awaited death and contemplated a past thoroughly dominated by others, a life in which he had given up control of himself in order to fit into a system. He asked, "What if my whole life has been wrong?" As you look back on your life, much the way Ivan Ilych did, you'll find that you seldom experience regret for anything that you've done. It is what you haven't done that will torment you. The message, therefore, is clear. Do it!

### Example 2: The Stick-Shift Learning Process — Learning Not to Be Unhappy

Think back to learning to drive a stick shift automobile. You were faced with what seemed to be an insurmountable problem. Three pedals but only two feet to make them work. You first became aware of the complexity of the task. Let the clutch out slow, ooops too fast, jerky business, gas pedal down at the same rate as you release the clutch, right foot for the brake, but the clutch must go in, or you jerk again. A million mental signals: always thinking, using your brain. What do I do? Awareness, and then after thousands of trials, mistakes, new efforts, the day comes when you step into your car and drive away. No stalling, no jerking and no thinking. Driving a stick shift has become second nature, and how did you do it? With great difficulty. Lots of present-moment thinking, reminding, working. The process is identical in the emotional world. For example, you've been taught that going to the dentist is a nasty experience associated with pain. You could make the whole experience work for rather than against you by choosing to make it a pleasant, exciting procedure. You could, if you really decided to use your brain, make the sound of the drill signal a beautiful sexual experience and each time the brrrrrr sound appeared, you could train your mind to envision the most orgiastic moment of your life. You could think something different about what you used to call pain, and choose to feel something new and pleasurable.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Use the eternal perspective of death as a motivator to make choices that are truly yours
- ✅ Measure intelligence by happiness and present-moment living, not by credentials or problem-solving ability
- ✅ Practice the syllogism exercise daily to reinforce that your feelings come from your thoughts
- ✅ Catch and rewrite self-defeating statements in the moment they occur
- ✅ Build awareness of your old thinking patterns before trying to replace them
- ✅ Practice new thoughts repeatedly, just as you would practice any new skill
- ✅ Use immobilization as your guide for identifying emotions worth eliminating
- ✅ Live fully in each present moment rather than sacrificing today for tomorrow
- ✅ Choose growth motivation over the need to repair perceived deficiencies
- ✅ Accept full responsibility for your feelings rather than blaming others or circumstances
- ❌ Don't blame other people, events, or circumstances for how you feel
- ❌ Don't assume your emotions happen to you without your consent
- ❌ Don't judge intelligence solely by academic or intellectual achievements
- ❌ Don't use self-defeating statements that give away responsibility for your feelings
- ❌ Don't expect new thinking to become automatic after a single attempt
- ❌ Don't give up when new thoughts feel unnatural or difficult at first
- ❌ Don't sacrifice the present moment to worry about the future or ruminate on the past
- ❌ Don't idealize the future as the time when you will finally be happy
- ❌ Don't use deficiency motivation—improving yourself out of a sense of incompleteness—as your primary driver
- ❌ Don't mistake angry venting for a healthy strategy if it stems from internal upset rather than purposeful emphasis

## Keep In Mind

- Someone's death does not make you unhappy; you cannot be unhappy until you learn of the death, so it is not the death but what you tell yourself about the event.
- Hurricanes aren't depressing by themselves; depression is uniquely human.
- If someone says to you, "Think of a pink antelope," you can turn it green, or make it an aardvark, or simply think of something else if you so choose. You alone control what enters your head as a thought.
- The disease of present-moment avoidance assumes many forms. Learn to recognize it in your own life.
- Wishing, hoping, and regretting are the most common and dangerous tactics for evading the present.
- "As you look back on your life, much the way Tolstoy's Ivan Ilych did, you'll find that you seldom experience regret for anything that you've done. It is what you haven't done that will torment you."

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You believe that other people or external events are making you unhappy, and feel powerless to change how you feel.
  **Solution:** Apply the syllogism exercise: your feelings come from your thoughts, and you control your thoughts. Choose a new thought about the situation, and your feelings will follow.

- **Problem:** You catch yourself using self-defeating statements like "You hurt my feelings" or "I can't help the way I feel," which hand over control of your emotions to others.
  **Solution:** Immediately rewrite each statement to reflect that you are in charge: "I hurt my feelings because of the things I told myself about your reaction to me" and "I can help the way I feel, but I've chosen to be upset."

- **Problem:** You have been trying new thoughts for a short time but haven't yet seen lasting change, and are tempted to conclude that the approach doesn't work.
  **Solution:** Understand that learning not to be unhappy requires thousands of repetitions, just like learning any difficult skill. Practice, practice, practice, and do not yield to the temptation to quit or slack off.

- **Problem:** You find yourself immobilized by a negative emotion—unable to act, think clearly, or engage with life—and don't recognize it as a signal to change.
  **Solution:** Use the immobilization checklist to identify the specific ways the feeling is limiting you. Virtually all negative emotions result in some degree of self-immobility, and this alone is sufficient reason to eliminate them.

- **Problem:** You use the present moment to worry about the future or ruminate on the past, and never actually experience today.
  **Solution:** Recognize that now is the only moment you can ever live. When you catch your mind wandering, bring it back intentionally. Remember: wishing, hoping, and regretting are the tactics people use to avoid the present.
