# Chapter I: Taking Responsibility of Yourself

## Overview

This chapter teaches that you are the sole decider of your emotions, behavior, and life direction. Contentment is a decision made in each current moment through the thoughts you choose to entertain, not a state imposed upon you by circumstances or other people. The chapter provides actionable tools—including a syllogism exercise, a manual-transmission analogy for learning new thought patterns, and a paralysis checklist—to help you eliminate self-limiting emotions and take full responsibility for your feelings.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you feel that outside events or other people are making you unhappy and want to regain influence over your own emotional experience
- When you recognize self-limiting cycles—such as anxiety, anger, or passivity—that keep you from functioning at a higher level
- When you want to stop living according to others' demands and begin living each current moment with intention and contentment

## Steps

### Step 1: Use Your Own Death as a Motivator to Take Responsibility of Your Life

When contemplating a decision about whether to take responsibility of yourself and make your own choice, ask yourself: "How long am I going to be dead?" With that eternal perspective, make your own choice and leave the worrying, fears, questions of whether you can afford it, and guilt to those who will never die. If you don't begin taking these steps, you will spend your whole existence the way others say you must. Live your life in a way that is pleasing to you, since it is your life to direct as you choose.

### Step 2: Redefine Cognitive Capacity Based on Contentment, Not Problem-Solving

Stop measuring intelligence by your ability to solve complex problems, read, write, compute, or resolve abstract equations quickly. Stop letting formal education and bookish excellence be the true measures of individual satisfaction. Measure intelligence by an effective, content life lived each day and each current moment of every day. If you are content and live each moment for everything it's worth, you demonstrate cognitive maturity. If you know that given your inability to resolve a particular concern you can still choose contentment for yourself, or at a minimum refuse to choose unhappiness, you demonstrate cognitive maturity.

### Step 3: Recognize That You Influence Your Emotions Through Your Thoughts

Accept the notion that you can influence your own emotions; that anger, fear, hate, as well as love, ecstasy, and joy are decisions you make to feel, not things that happen to you. Do not allow emotions to arise without examining them; choose them actively. When sorrowful events occur, do not just naturally feel sorrow and hope that happy events will come along; choose how to feel. See a given emotion as a choice rather than as a fixed aspect of existence.

### Step 4: Use the Syllogism Exercise to Prove You Can Control Your Feelings

Learn the form of a logical syllogism (major premise, minor premise, conclusion). Use the following syllogism as proof that you can control your feelings:

- MAJOR PREMISE: I can control my thoughts.
- MINOR PREMISE: My feelings come from my thoughts.
- CONCLUSION: I can control my feelings.

Accept the major premise: You have the ability to think whatever you choose to allow into your head. If something just "pops" into your head, you still have the ability to make it go away, and therefore you still retain the ability over your mental world. Answer the question: "If you don't control your thoughts, who does?" If others control what you think, then they should seek therapy and you will instantly get better.

Accept the minor premise: You cannot have a feeling (emotion) without first having experienced a thought. Remove the brain and its ability to "feel" is eliminated. A feeling is a physical reaction to a thought.

Accept the conclusion: If you control your thoughts, and your feelings come from your thoughts, then you are capable of influencing your own feelings. You influence your feelings by working on the thoughts that preceded them. Simply put, you believe that things or people make you unhappy, but this is not accurate. You contribute to your own unhappiness because of the thoughts that you have about the people or things in your life.

Becoming a free and healthy person involves learning to think differently. Once you can change your thoughts, your new emotional responses will begin to emerge, and you will have taken the first step on the road to your personal freedom.

### Step 5: Rewrite Self-Limiting Statements to Take Responsibility for Your Feelings

Identify the self-limiting statements from the first list that you have used:

- "You hurt my feelings."
- "You make me feel bad."
- "I can't help the way I feel."
- "I just feel angry, don't ask me to explain it."
- "He makes me sick."
- "Heights scare me."
- "You're embarrassing me."
- "She really turns me on."
- "You made a fool of me in public."

Rewrite each statement in the list to reflect that you are responsible for how you feel and that your feelings come from the thoughts you have about anything:

- "I hurt my feelings because of the things I told myself about your reaction to me."
- "I made myself feel bad."
- "I can help the way I feel, but I've chosen to be upset."
- "I've decided to be angry, because I can usually manipulate others with my anger, since they think I control them."
- "I make myself sick."
- "I scare myself at high places."
- "I'm embarrassing myself."
- "I turn myself on whenever I'm near her."
- "I made myself feel foolish by taking your opinions of me more seriously than my own, and believing that others would do the same."

Examine the message each old statement sends; it is that you are not responsible for how you feel. Examine why the statements in the second list did not evolve into clichés. The answer lies in our culture, which teaches the thinking of the first list and discourages the logic of the second list. Ask yourself if there is a measurable benefit in being unhappy, down, or hurt. Then begin to examine, in depth, the kind of thoughts that are leading you to these debilitating feelings.

### Step 6: Learn to Prevent Unhappiness Through Awareness and Repetition (The Manual Transmission Analogy)

Accept that it is not easy to think in new ways; you are accustomed to a certain set of thoughts and the debilitating thoughts that follow. Understand that happiness is easy, but learning not to be unhappy can be difficult. Recognize that taking responsibility of yourself begins with awareness. Notice when you say things like, "He hurt my feelings." Remind yourself what you're doing at the moment you're doing it. Understand that new thought patterns require awareness of the old thought patterns.

Realize that you have become accustomed to thought patterns that identify the causes of your feelings as external to your thinking. You have put in extended periods of practice for such thinking, and you'll need to counteract the habit with repeated practice, thinking that assumes responsibility for your own feelings. Apply the same process used to learn physical tasks (like driving a manual transmission) to the emotional world: awareness, then after repeated practice, mistakes, new efforts, the day comes when the new behavior becomes automatic. Repeat endlessly that your mind really is your own and that you are capable of influencing your own feelings.

Taking responsibility of yourself involves more than simply testing new thoughts without effort. It requires a determination to be content and to challenge and destroy each and every thought that creates a paralyzing unhappiness in you. Practice, practice, practice, never yielding to the temptation to quit or slack off.

### Step 7: Choose Contentment Even When It Seems Impossible (The Punishment Thought Experiment)

Imagine that each time you become unhappy, you are subjected to some unpleasant consequence: confined to isolation for extended periods of time, required to remain standing for extended periods, denied food, required to consume a food you find unpleasant, or subjected to physical pain rather than mental distress by yourself.

Imagine that you were subjected to any one of these punishments until you made your unhappy feelings go away. Ask yourself: How long do you think you would continue to hold on to them? Recognize that you would choose a different feeling. Accept that the issue is not whether you can take influence over your feelings, but whether you will. Ask yourself: What must you endure before you'll make such a choice?

### Step 8: Choose Productive Behavior Over Self-Limiting Behavior in Everyday Life

When stuck in traffic, do not become angry, swear at other drivers, berate your passengers, or take your feelings out on anyone and anything in range. Do not justify your behavior by saying that traffic always sends you into a snit or that you simply have no control in a traffic jam. Instead, decide to think something else and use your mind in a constructive manner. Talk to yourself in new ways and become accustomed to new behavior which might include whistling, singing, turning on a tape recorder to write verbal letters, even timing yourself with thirty-second postponements of your anger. Decide not to become distressed. Choose to substitute, in gradual incremental stages, healthy new feelings and habits for old self-limiting emotions.

When bored at dull parties or committee meetings, make your mind work in new ways by changing the subject with a relevant insight, or writing the initial portion of your novel, or working on new plans which will help you avoid these settings in the future. Using your mind actively means assessing the people and events which give you the greatest difficulty and then deciding on new thinking strategies to make them work for you. In a restaurant, if you typically become distressed over poor service, think first of why you should not choose to be upset because someone or something is not going the way you would like it to go. You can choose not to be upset by someone else, especially someone who is so unimportant in your life. Devise strategies to change the setting, or leave, or whatever. But don't just become perturbed. Use your brain to work for you, and eventually you'll have the effective habit of not being upset when things go wrong.

### Step 9: Choose Wellness Over Discomfort for Physical Sufferings Without Identified Physical Cause

Recognize that you can choose to eliminate certain physical discomforts which are not rooted in an identified physical cause, including headaches, backaches, ulcers, hypertension, rashes, skin eruptions, cramps, and similar physical discomforts. Understand that you may have chosen physical maladies as a means of gaining attention and as a means of receiving concern and compassion. If you want the headache, you have actually chosen it. Learn not to want this for yourself and practice making the headache shift from one location to another in your head. Learn that you control the headache by making it move around. Decide not to choose headaches (or similar chosen physical discomforts) completely.

### Step 10: Recognize and Reduce Immobilization in Your Life

Use the word "immobilization" as the indicator of negative emotions in your life. Understand that immobilization can range from complete inaction to mild uncertainty. Ask yourself: Does your anger keep you from saying, feeling, or doing something? If so, then you are paralyzed. Ask yourself: Does your shyness prevent you from meeting people you want to know? If so, you are paralyzed and avoiding experiences you want to have. Ask yourself: Is your hate and jealousy helping you to grow an ulcer or to raise your blood pressure? Does it keep you from working effectively on the job? Are you unable to sleep or make love because of a negative current-moment feeling? These are all signs of immobilization.

Understand the definition: Immobilization is a state, however mild or serious, in which you are not functioning at the level you want to function at. If feelings lead to such a state, you need to look no further for a reason to get rid of them. Use the following checklist to identify when you are paralyzed:

- You can't talk lovingly to your spouse and children though you want to.
- You can't work on a project that interests you.
- You don't make love and would like to.
- You sit in the house all day and brood.
- You don't play golf, tennis, or other enjoyable activities, because of a leftover gnawing feeling.
- You can't introduce yourself to someone who appeals to you.
- You avoid talking to someone when you realize that a simple gesture would improve your relationship.
- You can't sleep because something is bothering you.
- Your anger keeps you from thinking clearly.
- You say something abusive to someone that you love.
- Your face is twitching, or you are so nervous that you don't function the way you would prefer.

Understand that nearly all negative feelings result in some degree of self-paralysis, and this alone is a clear reason for eliminating them entirely from your life. Recognize the exception: If an angry voice is simply a device for emphasis and it works (e.g., yelling at a young child to emphasize that you do not want him to play in the street), then you've adopted an effective approach. However, if you yell at others not to make a point, but because you are distressed from within, then you've prevented yourself from acting, and it's time to begin working at new choices.

### Step 11: Focus on the Current Moment

Combat immobilization by learning to focus on the current moment. Get in touch with your "now." Recognize that there really is no other moment you can ever live. Now is all there is, and the future is just another current moment to live when it arrives. You cannot live it until it does appear. Do not sacrifice the present for the future. Avoid the culture that teaches: "Save for the future! Consider the consequences. Don't be a hedonist. Think of tomorrow. Plan for your retirement."

Avoid the pattern of current-moment avoidance, which assumes many forms:

- Do not go into the woods to absorb nature and let your mind wander focusing on various things you should be doing back home (family responsibilities, shopping, home maintenance, expenses) or all of the things you'll have to do when you get out of the woods.
- Do not go on vacation basking in the sun in anticipation of what your friends back home will say when you return with a dark tan.
- Do not let your mind run on past or future events while experiencing the current moment with your spouse.
- Do not read while your mind is on a mental excursion, avoiding the material even while your eyes focus on each word.

Absorb each current moment and tune out the past which is over and the future which will arrive in time. Seize the current moment as the only one you have. Remember that wishing, hoping, and regretting are the most common and harmful strategies for evading the present. Avoid idealizing the future, thinking that at some miraculous moment in the future life will change, everything will fall into place, and you will find contentment. When the future arrives, it will be the present, and you must use it to prepare for the next future. Do not let the cycle of idealizing the future, being disappointed when it arrives, and then idealizing again become your lifestyle. Interrupt it with some intentional current-moment satisfaction.

Follow Henry James's advice from The Ambassadors: "Live all you can; it's a mistake not to. It doesn't so much matter what you do in particular, so long as you have your life. If you haven't had that what have you had?…What one loses one loses, make no mistake about that…. The right time is any time that one is still so lucky as to have…. Live!" Develop an appreciation for the current moment. Seize every second of your life and savor it. Value your current moments. Using them up in any self-limiting ways means you've lost them forever.

### Step 12: Choose Development Motivation Over Deficiency (Deficiency) Motivation

Understand the two forms of motivation: deficiency motivation, and development motivation. Choose to be motivated out of a desire to develop rather than a need to address perceived shortcomings. Recognize that if you recognize that you can always grow, improve, become more and greater, that is enough. When you decide to be unable to act or to experience painful feelings, you've made a non-development decision.

Development motivation means using your life energy for greater contentment, rather than having to improve yourself because you've sinned or because you are in some way incomplete. Choose self-direction in every current moment of your life. Self-direction means you are the decider of your fate; you are not a coper, or a striver, or someone who adjusts to the world. Rather you choose what your daily environment will be for you.

Follow George Bernard Shaw's viewpoint: "People are always blaming their circumstances for what they are. I don't believe in circumstances. The people who get on in this world are the people who get up and look for the circumstances they want, and if they can't find them, make them." Apply the same consistent effort to unlearning self-limiting thinking that you would give to the learning of any challenging task. If you really want to be free from persistent anxiety, satisfied with yourself and in control of your own choices, and achieve current-moment contentment, you will need to repeat endlessly that your mind really is your own and that you are capable of influencing your own feelings.

### Step 13: Recognize That You Are Responsible for How You Feel (The Victimhood Mindset)

Recognize that you have grown up in a culture which has taught you that you are not responsible for your feelings. Identify the many phrases you have used repeatedly to defend yourself against the fact that you do influence your feelings. Examine the message each saying sends: that you are not responsible for how you feel. Understand that you feel what you think, and you can learn to think differently about anything—if you decide to do so.

Ask yourself if there is a measurable benefit in being unhappy, down, or hurt. Then begin to examine, in depth, the kind of thoughts that are leading you to these debilitating feelings. Reject the cultural messages that imply you are a helpless prisoner who must have someone or something else do things for you. Only you can improve your situation or make yourself happy. Take influence over your own mind, and then practice feeling and behaving in the ways that you choose.

### Step 14: Distinguish Between Harmful Feelings That Have a Benefit and Those That Are Purely Paralyzing

Recognize that you might believe that anger, hostility, shyness, and other similar feelings are useful in specific situations. Use immobilization as your guide: if the feeling paralyzes you in any way, it is harmful and should be eliminated. Recognize that if an angry voice is simply a device for emphasis and it works (e.g., yelling at a young child to emphasize that you do not want him to play in the street), then you've adopted an effective approach. However, if you yell at others not to make a point, but because you are distressed from within, then you've prevented yourself from acting, and it's time to begin working at new choices that will help you reach your goal without experiencing feelings that are hurtful to you.

## Examples

### Example 1: The Character Ivan Ilych in Tolstoy's Work — Living a Life Controlled by Others

The character Ivan Ilych in Tolstoy's work awaited death and contemplated a past thoroughly controlled by others, a life in which he had given up control of himself in order to fit into a social system. He asked, "What if my whole life has been wrong?" As you look back on your life, much the way the character Ivan Ilych in Tolstoy's work did, you'll find that you rarely feel regret for anything that you've done. It is what you haven't done that will distress you. The message, therefore, is direct. Do it!

### Example 2: The Manual Transmission Learning Process — Learning to Prevent Unhappiness

Think back to learning to drive a manual transmission automobile. You were faced with what seemed to be a difficult challenge. Three pedals but only two feet to make them work. You first became aware of the complexity of the task. Let the clutch out slow, ooops too fast, jerky business, gas pedal down at the same rate as you release the clutch, right foot for the brake, but the clutch must go in, or you jerk again. Many mental cues: always thinking, using your brain. What do I do? Awareness, and then after repeated practice, mistakes, new efforts, the day comes when you step into your car and drive away. No stalling, no jerking and no thinking. Driving a manual transmission has become automatic, and how did you do it? With great difficulty. Lots of current-moment thinking, reminding, working. The process is identical in the emotional world. For example, you've been taught that going to the dentist is a negative experience associated with pain. You could make the whole experience work for rather than against you by choosing to make it a pleasant, exciting procedure. You could, if you really decided to use your brain, associate the sound of the drill with a highly pleasurable moment and each time the brrrrrr sound appeared, you could train your mind to envision a highly pleasurable moment of your life. You could think something different about what you previously called pain, and choose to feel something new and pleasurable.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Use the eternal perspective of death as a motivator to make choices that are truly yours
- ✅ Measure cognitive capacity by contentment and current-moment living, not by credentials or problem-solving ability
- ✅ Practice the syllogism exercise daily to reinforce that your feelings come from your thoughts
- ✅ Catch and rewrite self-limiting statements in the moment they occur
- ✅ Build awareness of your old thought patterns before trying to replace them
- ✅ Practice new thoughts repeatedly, just as you would practice any new skill
- ✅ Use immobilization as your guide for identifying emotions worth eliminating
- ✅ Live fully in each current moment rather than sacrificing today for tomorrow
- ✅ Choose development motivation over the need to address perceived shortcomings
- ✅ Accept full responsibility for your feelings rather than blaming others or circumstances
- ❌ Don't blame other people, events, or circumstances for how you feel
- ❌ Don't assume your emotions happen to you without your consent
- ❌ Don't judge intelligence solely by academic or intellectual achievements
- ❌ Don't use self-limiting statements that give away responsibility for your feelings
- ❌ Don't expect new thinking to become automatic after a single attempt
- ❌ Don't give up when new thoughts feel unnatural or difficult at first
- ❌ Don't sacrifice the current moment to worry about the future or ruminate on the past
- ❌ Don't idealize the future as the time when you will finally be content
- ❌ Don't use deficiency motivation—improving yourself out of a sense of incompleteness—as your primary driver
- ❌ Don't mistake angry venting for an effective approach if it stems from internal distress rather than purposeful emphasis

## Keep In Mind

- Someone's death does not directly cause unhappiness; you cannot feel unhappy until you become aware of the death, so it is not the death but what you tell yourself about the event.
- Hurricanes do not directly cause depression; depression is uniquely human.
- If someone says to you, "Think of a pink antelope," you can turn it green, or make it an aardvark, or simply think of something else if you so choose. You alone influence what enters your head as a thought.
- The pattern of current-moment avoidance assumes many forms. Learn to recognize it in your own life.
- Wishing, hoping, and regretting are the common and harmful strategies people use to evade the current moment.
- "As you look back on your life, much the way the character Ivan Ilych in Tolstoy's work did, you'll find that you rarely feel regret for anything that you've done. It is what you haven't done that will distress you."

## Common Challenges

- **Problem:** You believe that other people or external events are making you unhappy, and feel unable to change how you feel.
  **Solution:** Apply the syllogism exercise: your feelings come from your thoughts, and you influence your thoughts. Choose a new thought about the situation, and your feelings will follow.

- **Problem:** You catch yourself using self-limiting statements like "You hurt my feelings" or "I can't help the way I feel," which hand over influence of your emotions to others.
  **Solution:** Immediately rewrite each statement to reflect that you are responsible: "I hurt my feelings because of the things I told myself about your reaction to me" and "I can help the way I feel, but I've chosen to be upset."

- **Problem:** You have been trying new thoughts for a short time but haven't yet seen sustained improvement, and are tempted to conclude that the approach seems ineffective.
  **Solution:** Understand that learning to prevent unhappiness requires repeated practice, just like learning any challenging skill. Practice, practice, practice, and do not yield to the temptation to quit or slack off.

- **Problem:** You find yourself paralyzed by a negative feeling—unable to act, think clearly, or engage with life—and don't recognize it as an indicator to change.
  **Solution:** Use the immobilization checklist to identify the specific ways the feeling is limiting you. Nearly all negative feelings result in some degree of self-paralysis, and this alone is an adequate reason to eliminate them.

- **Problem:** You use the current moment to worry about the future or ruminate on the past, and rarely fully experience the current day.
  **Solution:** Recognize that now is the only moment you can ever live. When you catch your mind wandering, bring it back intentionally. Remember: wishing, hoping, and regretting are the strategies people use to avoid the current moment.
