# Chapter VII: Breaking Free From Social Convention

## Overview

This chapter identifies social conventions — the accumulated shoulds, musts, and oughts imposed by family, culture, religion, and society — as a self-defeating pattern (an "erroneous zone") that prevents individuals from making self-directed choices based on their own judgment rather than others' expectations and from taking responsibility for their own feelings, and that these conventions are perpetuated by the very people who adopt them without examining them. The author shows that operating from an external locus of control, engaging in "musterbation" (rigid should-thinking), and obeying rules and etiquette without examination limit personal autonomy and keep people adhering to standards set by others. Through detailed lessons and exercises, the chapter provides concrete strategies for shifting to an internal locus of control, eliminating blame and hero worship, escaping the right-versus-wrong framework, and living by self-determined standards regardless of others' opinions.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you feel unhappy or frustrated, or feel immobilized (unable to take effective action) and notice yourself blaming others, external circumstances, or situations outside your control for your feelings
- When you find yourself following social etiquette conventions, formal rules, and cultural traditions, or other people's expectations even when they feel meaningless or constraining, or continuing these patterns for weeks, months, or years without reassessing whether they align with your current values
- When you are caught in arguments focused on determining who is right (right-vs.-wrong disputes), feel unable to choose, or sense that your life is being run by shoulds borrowed from others rather than your own choices

### Step 3: Escape the Right vs. Wrong Trap

Stop thinking in terms of right versus wrong. Instead of attempting to convince someone how wrong they are, have discussions in which you have no prescribed expectations (borrowed "shoulds") for how the other person must behave. As long as the other person is allowed to be different from you, you will eliminate the repeated arguing in which you persistently pursue being right. Recognize that judgments of right and wrong represent borrowed shoulds, and these shoulds get in your way, particularly when they conflict with another person's need to have their own perspective. Think of any decision as bringing about something different, or more effective, or legal, but the moment it becomes a question of right vs. wrong, recognize that you are trapped into the "I've always got to be right and when things or people are not right, then I'm going to be unhappy" trap. Stop dividing the world neatly into extremes such as black/white, yes/no, good/bad and right/wrong. Clifford, who was in a marriage in which he argued regularly about many topics, was told: "Instead of attempting to convince your wife how wrong she is, why not simply have discussions in which you have no shoulds for her. As long as she is allowed to be different from you, you'll eliminate the repeated arguing in which you persistently pursue being right." Clifford was able to set aside this need, and also to put some communication and love back in his marriage.

### Step 4: Overcome Indecisiveness as a Spin-Off of Right vs. Wrong Thinking

When making a decision, set aside judgments of rightness and wrongness, because assuming an outcome is right implies a guarantee of a specific result. If you are trying to decide on a college to attend, shift the process to: "There is no single right college. If I select A then these are the more likely consequences, while B will probably bring about these." Neither is inherently right or wrong; one is simply different from the other, and you will have no guarantees regardless of the choice. View all potential outcomes as being neither right nor wrong, good nor bad, or even better or worse. Simply different. If you buy one dress which you like, this is how you are going to look, which is only different from (not better than) wearing that other dress. Once you give up those inaccurate and self-destructive categories of right and wrong, find decision-making a simple matter of weighing which consequences you'd prefer at a given present decision point (the specific moment when you are choosing among options). If you begin to regret the decision, rather than deciding that regret is a waste of your time (because it keeps you living in the past), simply resolve to make a different decision in your next present moment, one that will bring consequences that the earlier decision failed to bring. But never attempt to put it into a right or wrong categorization. Recognize that nothing is more important than anything else. The child collecting seashells is not doing something more right or wrong than the President of General Motors making a major corporate decision. They are different. Nothing more!

### Step 6: Evaluate and Reform Etiquette Rules

Distinguish between good manners (behavior that shows genuine consideration for other people's comfort and dignity) and etiquette guidelines (arbitrary social conventions created without practical purpose). Recognize that many etiquette guidelines are meaningless rules that constrain behavior without benefiting anyone. There is no single objectively correct or proper way for you; there is only what you decide is right for you — as long as you don't make it hard for others to get along. Choose how you'll introduce people, what you'll tip, what you'll wear, how you'll speak, where you'll sit, how you'll eat, and other similar choices strictly on the basis of what you genuinely prefer based on your own values (your authentic self-directed choice). Any time you fall into the trap of "What should I wear," or "How should I do it," recognize that you're giving up your autonomy and self-direction—the part of yourself that should be guiding your choices. Be self-directed rather than other-directed in the everyday running of your life. Being true to yourself means relying less on others for approval. "Think of all the little meaningless rules you've been encouraged to adopt simply because an Emily Post, Amy Vanderbilt, or Abigail van Buren has so written. Eat your corn on the cob this way, always wait for the hostess to start before eating, introduce the man to the woman, sit on that side of the church at a wedding, tip this, wear that, use these words. Don't consult yourself; look it up in the book."

### Step 10: Recognize and Avoid the Common Payoffs of Musterbation

You can pat yourself on the back for being obedient. Do not do this. Recognize that your obedience to the external should allows you to assign the responsibility for your standing still to the should rather than to yourself. As long as the should is the rationale for what you are (or aren't), you can avoid the risks involved in trusting yourself to change. Thus your shoulds can prevent you from developing. Stop using your musts to maneuver others. By telling someone how a task should be done, you can make them do it the way you want it to be done. ### Step 12: Become Your Own Judge and Live According to Your Own Standards

Become your own judge of your conduct. Learn to rely on yourself to make present-moment decisions (decisions made in the current moment without being bound by past habits or future anxieties). Cease leafing through a lifetime of policies and traditions for an answer. Sing your own song of happiness in any way that you choose, oblivious to how it is supposed to be. Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote in Literary Ethics in 1838, "Men grind and grind in the mill of a truism, and nothing comes out but what was put in. But the moment they desert the tradition for a spontaneous thought (an unscripted idea that arises from your own mind rather than from memorized tradition), then poetry, wit, hope, virtue, learning anecdote, all flock to their aid." The book comments: "What a beautiful thought. Stay with tradition and you will always be the same, but toss it aside, and the world is yours to use as creatively as you choose."

## Examples

### Example 1: Barbara and the Obesity Problem

Barbara, a client who struggled with her weight, blamed her mother (who forced food on her as a child), her husband (who neglected her), her children (who were inconsiderate), and even her own metabolism and astrological forces (such as planetary positions or horoscopes). She tried Weight Watchers, pills, diet doctors, and astrology — all externally directed. After counseling, Barbara began to recognize that her unhappiness was the result of her own choices. Her first decision was to change her eating habits through discipline. Instead of blaming her husband and children for treating her badly and driving her to food, she began to see that she had been playing the martyr for years, virtually begging them to exploit her. Once Barbara began demanding to be treated well, she found that her family was only too ready to treat her that way, and instead of seeking solace in food she found fulfillment in relationships based on mutual respect and love. She is now not only thinner, but happier as well. She knows that it isn't her husband or her children, her mother or astrological forces (such as planetary positions or horoscopes) that are making her happy. She knows it is herself, for now she controls her own mind.

### Example 4: Marjorie (a composite example used by the author to illustrate the power of internalized shoulds) and the Premarital Sex Should

Marjorie holds a should that all premarital sex is a taboo. She is thirty-four years old, and so far she has never had a sexual experience because of this learned should. But Marjorie feels dissatisfied. She would like to have a sexual relationship, and she is very much dissatisfied with herself in this area. Moreover, if Marjorie does not marry, her should (in this case should-not) would then keep her from participating in sex for a lifetime. When she is confronted with this outcome she shudders at the thought, and yet her should-not is still there. Marjorie has additional effects from her should. She can't even stay overnight in the same house with her boyfriend out of concern about others' judgment. Thus she is frequently being inconvenienced because of her shoulds by having to come home to her mother at night. Hanging onto them keeps her from the difficult step of exploring this. But her response is typically, "I shouldn't do it." Clearly, her shoulds work against her own happiness.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Take full responsibility for your feelings (acknowledge that your emotions and choices are yours to control, not the product of external forces) — use "I" language rather than blaming external forces
- ✅ Distinguish between good manners (behavior that shows genuine consideration for other people's comfort and dignity) and etiquette rules (arbitrary social conventions created without practical purpose)
- ✅ Break a rule when it no longer serves a clear purpose for you or others
- ✅ Quietly live by your own standards without staging revolts or demanding others change
- ✅ Stop dividing decisions into right vs. wrong — see all outcomes as simply different, with different consequences
- ✅ Eliminate "should," "must," and "ought" from your vocabulary when they are borrowed from others
- ✅ Keep an internal/external journal to track when you assign responsibility externally and practice shifting inward
- ✅ Make your own "Rules of Conduct" based on what makes sense to you
- ✅ Start new traditions (create personally meaningful rituals that replace inherited ones you find meaningless) that fit your life rather than inheriting ones that don't serve you
- ✅ Be your own hero — stop elevating others and taking credit and responsibility for yourself
- ❌ Don't assign responsibility for your feelings to parents, partners, circumstances, or astrological forces (such as planetary positions or horoscopes)
- ❌ Don't model your behavior on heroes, celebrities, or anyone you have placed on a pedestal
- ❌ Don't use "should" as a weapon (imposing rigid obligations on yourself or others to induce guilt, shame, or compliance) on yourself or others — it can produce strain and disturbance
- ❌ Don't blindly obey rules, laws, or etiquette just because they exist — assess whether they serve you and others
- ❌ Don't engage in right-vs.-wrong arguments that produce frustration and keep you from connecting with others
- ❌ Don't let indecision paralyze you — if no outcome is inherently "right," choose the one whose consequences you prefer

## Security & Safety Notes

This chapter addresses internal locus of control, personal standards, and social conventions rather than digital or financial security. The advice to quietly resist rules you find purposeless (the "shrugger" approach) requires careful judgment — the strategies described are meant for social and personal conventions, not for laws or rules that protect the safety of others. The chapter itself notes that there is "no benefit in rebelling just for the sake of rebelling."

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You blame others — parents, partners, circumstances — for your unhappiness, never recognizing your own role in maintaining it.
  **Solution:** Keep an internal/external journal. Every time you catch yourself assigning blame externally, rewrite the statement in "I" language. Ask yourself what you are actually doing (or not doing) that contributes to your feelings, and take one small action to change it.

- **Problem:** You are paralyzed by indecision because you believe one option is "right" and another is "wrong," and you fear making the wrong choice.
  **Solution:** Eliminate the categories of right and wrong entirely. List the likely consequences of each option and choose the one whose outcomes you prefer — recognizing that neither is inherently better, only different. Trust yourself to adjust course later if needed.

- **Problem:** You are trapped in "musterbation" — rigidly following shoulds borrowed from others — and feel constant strain, resentment, or self-criticism when you cannot or do not meet them.
  **Solution:** Make a list of all the rules you live by that don't actually make sense to you. For each one, ask: "Do I truly believe this, or did I inherit it?" Replace borrowed shoulds with a self-authored "Rules of Conduct" and start one new tradition that genuinely fits your values.

- **Problem:** You engage in arguments focused on determining who is right (right-vs.-wrong disputes), believing that being right will fix your relationships or validate your position.
  **Solution:** Shift from winning to understanding. Allow the other person to be different without trying to convince them they are wrong. Ask yourself whether being right is more important than being happy, connected, and at peace. Often, letting go of the need to be right is what actually resolves the conflict.
