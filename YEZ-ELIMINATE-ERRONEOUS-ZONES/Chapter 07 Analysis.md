# Chapter VII: Breaking the Barrier of Convention

## Overview

This chapter identifies social conventions — the accumulated shoulds, musts, and oughts imposed by family, culture, religion, and society — as an erroneous zone that prevents individuals from living in a self-directed way and from taking responsibility for their own feelings. The author shows that operating from an external locus of control, engaging in "musterbation" (rigid should-thinking), and obeying rules and etiquette without examination are forms of self-enslavement that keep people trapped in lives designed by others. Through detailed lessons and exercises, the chapter provides concrete strategies for shifting to an internal locus of control, eliminating blame and hero worship, escaping the right-versus-wrong framework, and quietly living by self-determined standards regardless of others' opinions.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you feel unhappy, frustrated, or feeling stuck and notice yourself blaming others, external circumstances, or situations outside your control for your feelings
- When you find yourself following etiquette, rules, traditions, or other people's expectations even when they feel meaningless or unnecessarily constraining
- When you are caught in arguments about who is right, feel paralyzed by indecision, or sense that your life is being run by shoulds borrowed from others rather than your own choices

## Steps

### Step 1: Shift from External to Internal Locus of Control

Examine whether you assign responsibility for your feelings to someone or something external to yourself. If asked why you feel bad, notice whether you respond with answers like "My parents mistreat me," "She hurt my feelings," "My friends don't like me," "My luck is down," or "Things just aren't going well." If asked why you are happy, notice whether you respond with answers like "My friends treat me well," "My luck has changed," "Nobody is bugging me," or "She came through for me." Shift to internally oriented answers such as: "I tell myself the wrong things," "I put too much emphasis on what others say," "I worry about what someone else thinks," "I'm not strong enough now to avoid being unhappy," and "I don't have the skills to keep me from being miserable." When you are feeling good, use "I" references such as: "I worked hard at being happy," "I made things work for me," "I'm telling myself the right things," "I'm in charge of me, and this is where I choose to be." Take responsibility for all that you experience emotionally. Recognize that most shoulds and traditions are imposed by external sources, and if you hold many shoulds and cannot break conventions prescribed by others, you are operating from an external locus of control. Exercise internal control over your own thinking, feeling, and behavior. The chapter illustrates this with Barbara, a client with excess weight, who blamed her mother (who forced food on her as a child), her husband (who neglected her), her children (who were inconsiderate), and even her own metabolism and the stars. She tried Weight Watchers, pills, diet doctors, and astrology — all externally directed. After a period of counseling, Barbara began to recognize that her unhappiness was the result of her own choices. Her first decision was to change her eating habits through discipline: "Next time she felt hungry she resolved to reward herself with thoughts of her own internal streng..."

### Step 2: Eliminate Blame and Hero Worship

Recognize that blame is unproductive. Regardless of fault you find with another, and regardless of how much you blame them, it will not change you. Understand that blame keeps the focus off you when you are looking for external reasons to explain your unhappiness or frustration. Realize that even if blame has an effect, it will not be on you. You may succeed in making another feel guilty for something by blaming them, but you won't succeed in changing whatever it is about you that is making you unhappy. Avoid looking to others to determine your values. If someone does it, then I should do it too. Do not model your own behavior on the standards of heroes. Recognize that your heroes are people. They are human. They do the same things that you do. Stop elevating others to positions above yourself and giving them the responsibility for your good feelings. ### Step 3: Escape the Right vs. Wrong Trap

Stop thinking in erroneous right vs. wrong ways. Instead of attempting to convince someone how wrong they are, have discussions in which you have no shoulds for the other person. As long as the other person is allowed to be different from you, you will eliminate the repeated arguing in which you persistently pursue being right. Recognize that rights and wrongs represent shoulds of one kind or another, and the shoulds get in your way, particularly when they conflict with another person's need to have their own as well. Think of any decision as bringing about something different, or more effective, or legal, but the moment it becomes a question of right vs. wrong, recognize that you are trapped into the "I've always got to be right and when things or people are not right, then I'm going to be unhappy" trap. Stop dividing the world neatly into extremes such as black/white, yes/no, good/bad and right/wrong. Clifford, who was in a marriage in which he argued regularly about many topics, was told: "Instead of attempting to convince your wife how wrong she is, why not simply have discussions in which you have no shoulds for her. As long as she is allowed to be different from you, you'll eliminate the repeated arguing in which you persistently pursue being right." Clifford was able to set aside this strong need, and also to put some communication and love back in his marriage.

### Step 4: Overcome Indecisiveness as a Spin-Off of Right vs. Wrong Thinking

When making a decision, take away the rightness and wrongness of every decision, because right implies a guarantee. If you are trying to decide on the right college to attend, shift the process to: "There is no such thing as the right college. If I select A then these are the more likely consequences, while B will probably bring about these." Neither is right, one is simply different than the other, and you'll have no guarantees regardless of the choice. View all potential outcomes as being neither right nor wrong, good nor bad, or even better or worse. Simply different. If you buy one dress which you like, this is how you are going to look, which is only different from (not better than) wearing that other dress. Once you give up those inaccurate and self-destructive rights and wrongs, find decision-making a simple matter of weighing which consequences you'd prefer at a given present-moment. If you begin to choose regret over the decision, rather than deciding that regret is a waste of your time (because it keeps you living in the past), simply resolve to make a different decision in your next present moment, one that will bring consequences that the earlier decision failed to bring. But never attempt to put it into a right or wrong categorization. Recognize that nothing is more important than anything else. The child collecting seashells is not doing something more right or wrong than the President of General Motors making a major corporate decision. They are different. Nothing more!

### Step 5: Stop Musterbating — Eliminate Shoulds, Musts, and Oughts

Recognize that you are "musterbating" whenever you find yourself behaving in ways that you feel you must, even though you might prefer some other form of behavior. Understand that shoulds can produce a feeling of strain, which is all the greater the more a person tries to actualize their shoulds in their behavior. Recognize that because of externalizations, the shoulds always contribute to disturbance in human relations in one way or another. Ask yourself: Do you feel you should be kind to your colleagues, supportive of your spouse, helpful to your children and always work hard? And if at any given moment you fail in one of these shoulds, do you berate yourself and hence take on that strain and disturbance? Determine whether these shoulds belong to others and you have merely borrowed them. If so, you are musterbating. Realize that there are many should-nots as there are shoulds. Understand that you are allowed to be unconventional if you choose to. No one is keeping score on you, or going to punish you for not being something that someone else said you must be. Recognize that you can never be something you do not want to be consistently. It is not possible. Therefore, any borrowed should can produce strain in you, since you won't be able to fulfill your erroneous expectation. Realize that the strain does not result from your unconventional, nonsupportive, indiscreet or whatever behavior, but from the imposition of the should. "Perhaps these are not your shoulds. If, in fact, they belong to others and you have merely borrowed them, then you are musterbating. There are many should-nots as there are shoulds. These include: you should not be rude, angry, foolish, silly, juvenile, lewd, gloomy, offensive and many others. But you don't have to musterbate. Ever. It's all right to be lacking composure, or to not understand. You're allowed to be unconventional if you choose to. No one is keeping score on you, or going to punish you for not being something...

### Step 6: Evaluate and Reform Etiquette Rules

Distinguish between good manners (which simply entail consideration for other people) and etiquette guidelines (which are meaningless rules that were composed arbitrarily at one time). Recognize that many etiquette guidelines are meaningless rules. There is no single proper way for you; there is only what you decide is right for you — as long as you don't make it hard for others to get along. Choose how you'll introduce people, what you'll tip, what you'll wear, how you'll speak, where you'll sit, how you'll eat, and so on, strictly on the basis of what you want. Any time you fall into the trap of "What should I wear," or "How should I do it," recognize that you're giving up a chunk of yourself. Be self-directed rather than other-directed in the everyday running of your life. Being true to yourself means relying less on others for approval. "Think of all the little meaningless rules you've been encouraged to adopt simply because an Emily Post, Amy Vanderbilt, or Abigail van Buren has so written. Eat your corn on the cob this way, always wait for the hostess to start before eating, introduce the man to the woman, sit on that side of the church at a wedding, tip this, wear that, use these words. Don't consult yourself; look it up in the book."

### Step 7: Resist Blind Obedience to Rules and Laws

Determine for yourself which rules work, and are needed to maintain social order, and which can be broken without harm to yourself or others. Understand that there is no benefit in rebelling just for the sake of rebelling, but there are real benefits in being your own person and living your life according to your own standards. When a rule is unwise or no longer serves a purpose, and you are unable to act according to your own judgment because you must follow purposeless rules, reconsider the rules and your behavior. If you must comply with all rules at all times, recognize that you are likely to experience ongoing emotional dissatisfaction. Recognize that laws are there to serve you, not to make you a servant. Do not obey laws without examining them. Once you recognize that laws are there to serve you, not to make you a servant, you can begin to eliminate that musterbation behavior. The chapter illustrates with examples: the Nazis executed six million Jews because it was the "Law." A Suffolk County spokesman explained why people who had been inadvertently overcharged on their real estate taxes could not be reimbursed: "The law says that past tax bills cannot be reevaluated once they are paid. It's the law, I can't help it. My job is to enforce it, not to interpret it." At a swimming pool, young people were lounging near the edge when the pool was empty because the rule said it was for adults between 6:00 and 8:00 P.M. "This was the rule, and despite the fact that no adults wanted to use it, the rule was still being enforced. No flexibility, no ability to alter the rule when circumstances so warrant, simply blind obedience to a rule that had no logical reason at that moment." On Guam, enlisted men sat in the rain with one of their own ranks guarding a section of empty red benches reserved for officers at the midnight movie which officers never attended. When asked why, the answer was: "I don't make the rules, I just enforce them."

### Step 8: Resist Enculturation and Traditions When They Affect You Negatively — Become a "Shrugger"

Make decisions for yourself and carry them out without creating unnecessary conflict. Avoid public protests or demonstrations where they will do no good. Recognize that unwise rules, traditions, and policies are unlikely to disappear, but you don't have to be a part of them. Simply shrug as others go through their unquestioned behaviors. If they want to behave that way, fine for them but that's not for you. To make a public confrontation is often the surest way to incur negative consequences and create more obstacles for yourself. You'll find many everyday situations where it is easier to circumvent the rules quietly than to start a protest movement. Decide to be the person you want to be, or the one others want you to be. You must decide. Learn to allow others their choice even when they choose to obey rules even if it hurts them. No anger, only your own convictions. You may be seen as insubordinate, which is the price you'll pay for thinking for yourself. You may be seen as different, labeled selfish or rebellious, disapproval from others, and sometimes be ostracized. Do not be guided by others' rules which have you ending up in a position of disadvantage. Do not stage a revolt or make a public confrontation. Simply slip away and allow everyone else to participate in the demeaning ritual. Pass up your opportunity to participate. ### Step 10: Recognize and Avoid the Common Payoffs of Musterbation

Recognize that you can take solace in being a "good boy" or a "good girl" by following all of your shoulds. You can pat yourself on the back for being obedient. This dividend is a step backward in which you go back to an earlier stage of development when you were rewarded with approval whenever you behaved, which meant relying on someone else to establish your rules of conduct. Do not do this. Recognize that your obedience to the external should allows you to assign the responsibility for your standing still to the should rather than to yourself. As long as the should is the rationale for what you are (or aren't), you can avoid the risks involved in trusting yourself to change. Thus your shoulds prevent you from developing. Stop using your musts to maneuver others. By telling someone how a task should be done, you can make them do it the way you want it to be done. Stop hauling out a should when you lack confidence in yourself. As your self-image wanes, the should becomes your defense. Stop feeling morally superior about your behavior and retaining your resentment when others don't fit into the shoulds that you have for yourself and the rest of the world. Hence, you feel better about yourself in your mind at the expense of others who don't obey the rule-patterns. Stop trying to win approval by conforming. You feel good by fitting-in, which is what you were told throughout that you should be doing. That old approval-seeking need appears here as well. ### Step 11: Use Specific Strategies for Removing Your Shoulds

Begin with insight into your own behavior. Study the neurotic dividends spelled out above. Then ask yourself why are you burdening yourself with so many shoulds. Ask yourself if you really believe in them, or if you've just become accustomed to behaving that way. List all of the rules you abide by, which just don't seem to apply. Those conventional behaviors that you complain about, but can't seem to shed. Then, make your own "Rules of Conduct" that make the greatest amount of sense to you. Write them down, even if you don't believe at this time that you are capable of living them out. Start your own traditions. For example, if you've always decorated your Christmas Tree on Christmas Eve, and you'd rather do it three days before, begin a new Christmas tradition — one that makes sense to you. Have a consultation with your relatives and friends about the many rules of conduct you all follow which you find distasteful. Perhaps you'll be able to bring about some new rules which appear to be more reasonable to everyone. You'll discover that the old rules are still in effect only because no one ever seriously thought to challenge them in the past. Keep an internal/external journal. Write down your "external" references in which you assign responsibility to others for how you are feeling. See if you can shift yourself to the "internal" side with some new acts of courage. Keep track of your success in moving over to the internal side. See how many rules you impose on others. Ask them if they really need those directives, or if they would behave in the identical fashion without them. Perhaps you'll even learn that they could come up with more effective and flexible guidelines. Take a risk on challenging a rule or policy that you'd like to eliminate. But be prepared to deal without hostility with the consequences of your behavior. ### Step 12: Become Your Own Judge and Live According to Your Own Standards

Become your own judge of your conduct. Learn to rely on yourself to make present-moment decisions. Cease leafing through a lifetime of policies and traditions for an answer. Sing your own song of happiness in any way that you choose, oblivious to how it is supposed to be. Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote in Literary Ethics in 1838, "Men grind and grind in the mill of a truism, and nothing comes out but what was put in. But the moment they desert the tradition for a spontaneous thought, then poetry, wit, hope, virtue, learning anecdote, all flock to their aid." The book comments: "What a beautiful thought. Stay with tradition and you ensure that you'll always be the same, but toss it aside, and the world is yours to use as creatively as you choose."

## Examples

### Example 1: Barbara and the Obesity Problem

Barbara, a client with excess weight, blamed her mother (who forced food on her as a child), her husband (who neglected her), her children (who were inconsiderate), and even her own metabolism and the stars. She tried Weight Watchers, pills, diet doctors, and astrology — all externally directed. After a period of counseling, Barbara began to recognize that her unhappiness was the result of her own choices. Her first decision was to change her eating habits through discipline. Instead of blaming her husband and children for treating her badly and driving her to food, she began to see that she had been playing the martyr for years, virtually begging them to exploit her. Once Barbara began demanding to be treated well, she found that her family was only too ready to treat her that way, and instead of seeking solace in food she found fulfillment in relationships based on mutual respect and love. She is now not only thinner, but happier as well. She knows that it isn't her husband or her children, her mother or the stars that are making her happy. She knows it is herself, for now she controls her own mind.

### Example 4: Marjorie and the Premarital Sex Should

Marjorie holds a should that all premarital sex is a taboo. She is thirty-four years old, and so far she has never had a sexual experience because of this learned should. But Marjorie lacks inner peace. She would like to have a sexual relationship, and she is very much dissatisfied with herself in this area. Moreover, it is possible that Marjorie will never marry, and her should (in this case should-not) would then keep her from participating in sex for a lifetime. When she is confronted with this outcome she shudders at the thought, and yet her should-not is still there. Marjorie has additional effects from her should. She can't even stay overnight in the same house with her boyfriend out of concern about others' judgment. Thus she is frequently being inconvenienced because of her shoulds by having to come home to her mother at night. Hanging onto them keeps her from the difficult step of exploring this. But her response is typically, "I shouldn't do it." Clearly, her shoulds work against her own happiness.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Take full responsibility for your feelings — use "I" language rather than blaming external forces
- ✅ Distinguish between good manners (consideration for others) and etiquette rules (arbitrary conventions)
- ✅ Evaluate each rule for yourself and break it when it no longer serves you or others
- ✅ Quietly live by your own standards without staging revolts or demanding others change
- ✅ Stop dividing decisions into right vs. wrong — see all outcomes as simply different, with different consequences
- ✅ Eliminate "should," "must," and "ought" from your vocabulary when they are borrowed from others
- ✅ Keep an internal/external journal to track when you assign responsibility externally and practice shifting inward
- ✅ Make your own "Rules of Conduct" based on what genuinely makes sense to you
- ✅ Start new traditions that fit your life rather than inheriting ones that don't serve you
- ✅ Be your own hero — stop elevating others and taking credit and responsibility for yourself
- ❌ Don't assign responsibility for your feelings to parents, partners, circumstances, or the stars
- ❌ Don't model your behavior on heroes, celebrities, or anyone you have placed on a pedestal
- ❌ Don't use "should" as a weapon on yourself or others — it produces only strain and disturbance
- ❌ Don't blindly obey rules, laws, or etiquette just because they exist — assess whether they serve you and others
- ❌ Don't engage in right-vs.-wrong arguments that produce only frustration and keep you from genuine connection
- ❌ Don't let indecision paralyze you — if no outcome is inherently "right," choose the one whose consequences you prefer

## Security & Safety Notes

This chapter addresses internal locus of control, personal standards, and social conventions rather than digital or financial security. The advice to quietly resist rules you find purposeless (the "shrugger" approach) should be applied with judgment — the strategies described are meant for social and personal conventions, not for laws or rules that protect the safety of others. The chapter itself notes that there is "no benefit in rebelling just for the sake of rebelling."

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You blame others — parents, partners, circumstances — for your unhappiness, never recognizing your own role in maintaining it.
  **Solution:** Keep an internal/external journal. Every time you catch yourself assigning blame externally, rewrite the statement in "I" language. Ask yourself what you are actually doing (or not doing) that contributes to your feelings, and take one small action to change it.

- **Problem:** You are paralyzed by indecision because you believe one option is "right" and another is "wrong," and you fear making the wrong choice.
  **Solution:** Eliminate the categories of right and wrong entirely. List the likely consequences of each option and choose the one whose outcomes you prefer — recognizing that neither is inherently better, only different. Trust yourself to adjust course later if needed.

- **Problem:** You are trapped in "musterbation" — rigidly following shoulds borrowed from others — and feel constant strain, resentment, or self-criticism when you cannot or do not meet them.
  **Solution:** Make a list of all the rules you live by that don't actually make sense to you. For each one, ask: "Do I truly believe this, or did I inherit it?" Replace borrowed shoulds with a self-authored "Rules of Conduct" and start one new tradition that genuinely fits your values.

- **Problem:** You engage in arguments about who is right, believing that being right will fix your relationships or validate your position.
  **Solution:** Shift from winning to understanding. Allow the other person to be different without trying to convince them they are wrong. Ask yourself whether being right is more important than being happy, connected, and at peace. Often, letting go of the need to be right is what actually resolves the conflict.
