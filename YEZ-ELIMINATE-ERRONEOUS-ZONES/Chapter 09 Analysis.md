# Chapter IX: Putting an End to the Critic

## Overview

This chapter provides a framework for eliminating procrastination and the critical behavior that sustains it. It teaches that a non-doer — defined here as a person who refrains from taking direct action — is a critic who sits back and watches others — defined here as observing other people's actions without participating — while reflecting on those people's efforts — defined here as thinking about or evaluating the work that others are doing — and that becoming a doer — defined here as transitioning from a passive observer to an active participant — requires action, accepting uncertainty, and making adjustments. The chapter offers techniques including devoting your full attention to each successive five-minute interval, immediately beginning delayed tasks, and using varied mental exercises to eliminate boredom. It also addresses emotional obstacles such as fear of failure, need for approval, and the use of anxiety to avoid taking action.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you find yourself criticizing others instead of taking action yourself
- When you use boredom, anxiety, or fatigue as reasons to delay tasks you have committed to complete
- When you need specific techniques to begin tackling tasks you have delayed

## Steps

### Step 1: Recognizing That Procrastinators Are Often Critics

1. Recognize in yourself the behavior of observing others act — defined here as watching other people take action without joining in — while you yourself refrain from acting — defined here as choosing not to take action yourself.
2. Resolve to eliminate critical and passive behaviors completely — defined here as entirely, without exception — so that you can take direct action — defined here as immediate, purposeful behavior aimed at completing a task — instead of delaying — defined here as postponing or putting off action — while criticizing others.

"A non-doer is very often a critic, that is, someone who sits back — defined here as remaining in a passive, inactive position — and watches doers, and then speaks abstractly and at length — defined here as discussing ideas in general terms without specific details, and doing so for an extended period — about how the doers are performing — defined here as evaluating the quality or effectiveness of other people's actions. It is easy to be a critic, but being a doer — defined here as actively taking action to accomplish tasks — requires effort, risk and change." Real champions — defined here as highly accomplished individuals who consistently achieve excellence — such as Henry Aarons, Johnny Carsons, Bobby Fishers, Katharine Hepburns, and Joe Louises "have no time for criticizing others. They're too busy doing. They work. They help others who have developed fewer skills — defined here as having acquired less experience or ability in a particular area — rather than serve as their critics."

### Step 2: Eliminating Boredom as a Spin-Off of Procrastination

1. Recognize that boredom — defined here as a feeling of dissatisfaction or lack of interest — is a choice and not produced by your physical surroundings — defined here as the tangible environment around you — or external conditions — defined here as circumstances outside of your direct control.
2. Eliminate boredom by redirecting your mind or energy — defined here as consciously shifting your focus or effort from one thing to another — to a different activity in that same interval — defined here as within the same period of time.
3. Use your mind actively in circumstances that previously bored you.

"Life is never boring but some people choose to be bored. The concept of boredom entails an inability to use up present moments in a personally fulfilling way. Boredom is a choice; something you visit upon yourself." Example: "At a meeting, change the dull tempo with a pertinent question, or make your mind go off in exciting ways such as writing a poem, or memorizing twenty-five numbers backwards, just for the sheer drill of memory training. Decide to never be bored again."

### Step 3: Identifying Your Typical Procrastinating Behaviors

1. Review the list of typical procrastinating behaviors and identify which ones apply to you.

Remaining in a job with no clear path — defined here as an absence of defined opportunities for promotion, skill development, or meaningful change — for advancement or change; remaining in a relationship marked by persistent dissatisfaction — defined here as ongoing unhappiness or discontent — and hoping it may improve — defined here as expecting that the situation will get better without taking action to change it; refusing to work on relationship difficulties; not tackling addictions; delaying arduous or menial tasks; avoiding confrontations; being afraid to change locations geographically; delaying spending time with children; deciding to start your diet tomorrow; using sleep or tiredness as a reason for delaying tasks; getting sick when faced with a troublesome task; using the "I don't have time to do it" ruse; constantly looking forward to a vacation; being a critic and using criticism to hide your own refusal to act; refusing to get a physical checkup; being afraid to make a move toward someone you're fond of; experiencing boredom; planning but never putting in action a regular exercise program; living your entire life for your children and always delaying your own happiness.

### Step 4: Understanding the Reasons You Continue to Procrastinate

1. Review the list of factors that lead to procrastination to understand why you procrastinate.

Procrastination can allow avoidance of tasks that feel uncomfortable or undesirable; you can feel comfortable with your self-deception; you can stay exactly as you are forever; by experiencing boredom you have someone or something else to blame; by being a critic you can feel important at the expense of others; by waiting for things to get better you can blame the world for your unhappiness; you can avoid ever having to fail; wishing for things to happen allows you to return to a time when others handled your responsibilities; you can win sympathy from others; you can excuse low-quality work; by delaying tasks you might be able to get someone else to complete them; procrastination enables you to convince yourself you have abilities you do not possess; by avoiding a task you can escape success — defined here as avoiding positive outcomes or achievements by not attempting the task.

### Step 5: Making a Decision to Live Five Minutes at a Time

1. Devote your full attention — defined here as concentrate completely and without distraction — to each successive five-minute interval — defined here as every consecutive five-minute period, one after another.
2. Instead of thinking of tasks in long-range terms, think about the current five-minute period.
3. Try to use up a five-minute period doing what you want.
4. Refuse to delay activities that would produce personal fulfillment or enjoyment.

### Step 6: Simply Beginning the Task You Have Been Postponing

1. Sit down and get started on something you have been delaying.
2. Begin a letter or a book.
3. Recognize that much of your delaying is unnecessary since you will often find the task enjoyable once you give up the procrastination.
4. Use beginning to help eliminate worry about completing the entire task.

"Begin a letter or a book."

### Step 7: Assessing Your Fear by Asking What Is the Worst That Could Happen

1. Ask yourself: "What is the worst identifiable consequence — defined here as the most negative outcome that can be clearly identified — that could realistically happen to me — defined here as in a practical, evidence-based manner — if I did what I'm delaying right now?"
2. Recognize that the answer is so insignificant that it may jar you into action.
3. Assess your fear and you will have no reason to hold onto it.

### Step 8: Giving Yourself a Designated Time Slot for the Postponed Task

1. Assign yourself a specific fifteen-minute interval — defined here as schedule a dedicated 15-minute block of time — at a consistent time each week — defined here as the same day and time every week — (for example, Wednesday from 10:00 to 10:15 P.M.) which you will devote exclusively to the task you have been delaying.
2. Use the fifteen minutes of devoted effort to begin overcoming procrastination.

"for example, Wednesday from 10:00 to 10:15 P.M." — defined here as a specific day and 15-minute time window

### Step 9: Thinking of Yourself as Too Significant to Live with Postponement Anxiety

1. Regard yourself as too valuable — defined here as consider yourself too important or worthy — to spend your time worrying about tasks you have committed to complete.
2. When you know you are uncomfortable with postponement anxiety, remember that people who respect themselves and their own time — defined here as individuals who value their own well-being and use their time wisely — do not live with postponement anxiety.

### Step 10: Tackling the Fear of Living Effectively by Looking at Your Now

1. Write down the specific details — defined here as document concrete, factual information about your present circumstances — of your current situation.
2. Decide what specific tasks or situations you are avoiding right now — defined here as in the present moment, at this exact time.
3. Begin to address your fear of taking effective action.
4. Recognize that procrastination is replacing immediate, concrete action — defined here as direct, physical behavior taken now rather than planned for later — with worry about a specific future result — defined here as a particular outcome that has not yet occurred.
5. Treat the future task as a present task, so that the worry subsides.

### Step 11: Taking Immediate Action on Addictive or Avoided Behaviors

1. Quit smoking now.
2. Begin your diet immediately.
3. Give up alcohol immediately.
4. Set aside this book — defined here as put the book down and discontinue reading — and do one push-up as your beginning exercise project.
5. Tackle problems with action now.
6. Remind yourself that beginning the task — defined here as starting the work, regardless of how well it is done — is far more important than completing it flawlessly — defined here as finishing the work perfectly without errors.
7. Recognize that your own self-defeating choices are the only thing holding you back because you do not believe you are as strong as you really are.

"Quit smoking…now! Begin your diet…this moment! Give up booze…this second. Put this book down and do one push-up as your beginning exercise project. That's how you tackle problems…with action now! Do it! The only thing holding you back is you, and the neurotic choices you've made because you don't believe you're as strong as you really are. How simple…just do it!"

### Step 12: Using Your Mind Creatively to Eliminate Boredom

1. Start using your mind actively in circumstances that previously bored you.
2. At a meeting, change the dull tempo with a pertinent question.
3. Make your mind go off in exciting ways such as writing a poem, or memorizing twenty-five numbers backwards, just for the sheer drill of memory training.
4. Decide to never be bored again.

"At a meeting, change the dull tempo with a pertinent question, or make your mind go off in exciting ways such as writing a poem, or memorizing twenty-five numbers backwards, just for the sheer drill of memory training. Decide to never be bored again."

### Step 13: Moving from Critic to Doer When Facing Criticism

1. When someone begins to criticize you, ask: "Do you think I need a critic at this moment?" — where "this moment" means right now, in the present.
2. When you find yourself being a critic, ask the individual who works alongside you — defined here as a colleague or coworker in the same organization — if that person — defined here as the specific individual you are addressing — wants to hear your criticism, and if they do, what is their reason for wanting to hear it — defined here as what motivation or purpose they have for listening to your feedback?
3. Use these questions to help shift from criticizing to acting.

### Step 14: Evaluating Your Life as If You Had Six Months to Live

1. Write down an honest assessment — defined here as record a truthful evaluation — of your current life.
2. Ask: Are you doing what you would choose to be doing if you knew you had six months remaining to live — defined here as a hypothetical limited remaining lifespan?
3. If not, begin doing it because, in the context of a full lifetime, that is all you have.
4. Recognize that given the span of human history, any interval of waiting makes no meaningful difference. Your total lifetime, viewed against the span of human history, is a brief interval. Delaying anything makes no sense.

### Step 15: Being Courageous About Undertaking an Activity You Have Been Avoiding

1. Be courageous about undertaking an activity that you have been avoiding.
2. Recognize that a single deliberate act of courage can remove the specific fear you have been avoiding.
3. Stop telling yourself that you must complete the task flawlessly.
4. Remind yourself that beginning the task is far more important than completing it flawlessly.

### Step 16: Deciding Not to Be Tired Until the Moment Before You Get Into Bed

1. Choose not to act on feelings of tiredness — defined here as deliberately ignore or override sensations of fatigue — until you are lying in bed ready to sleep — defined here as in a horizontal position in bed prepared for rest.
2. Do not allow yourself to use tiredness or sickness as a reason to avoid tasks or to delay action.
3. Recognize that when you remove the reason for the illness or exhaustion—that is, avoidance of a task—physical symptoms — defined here as bodily sensations such as pain, fatigue, or tension — such as headaches, fatigue, or tension that stem from task avoidance commonly decrease in intensity — defined here as frequently become less severe or strong.

### Step 17: Eliminating the Words "Hope," "Wish" and "Maybe" from Your Vocabulary

1. Eliminate the words "hope," "wish" and "maybe" from your vocabulary because these words signal a tendency to delay tasks — defined here as indicate or suggest a habit of postponing action.
2. If you see these words creeping in, substitute new sentences.
3. Change "I hope things will work out" to "I will make it happen."
4. Change "I wish things were better" to "I am going to do the following things to ensure that I feel better."
5. Change "Maybe it will be okay" to "I will make it okay."

The three phrases that support procrastination: "I hope things will work out." "I wish things were better." "Maybe it'll be okay." The substitutions: "I will make it happen." "I am going to do the following things to ensure that I feel better." "I will make it okay."

### Step 18: Keeping a Journal of Your Own Complaining or Critical Behavior

1. Keep a journal of your own complaining or critical behavior.
2. By writing these actions down, you will accomplish two things:
   a. You will see how your critical behavior surfaces in your life—the frequency, patterns, events and people that are related to your being a critic.
   b. You will stop yourself from criticizing because it will be such a pain to have to write in the journal.

### Step 19: Having a Conference with All Involved When Procrastination Affects Others

1. If you are delaying a decision that affects other people's lives or schedules — defined here as a choice that impacts additional people beyond yourself — (for example, a move, a relationship difficulty, a new job) — defined here as examples of significant life changes that affect multiple people — have a conference with all involved and ask their opinions.
2. Be courageous about talking of your own fears.
3. See if you are delaying for reasons that are based only on assumptions.
4. By enlisting the aid of a confidant to help you with your procrastination, you will have made it a joint effort.
5. Soon you will dissipate much of the anxiousness that goes along with procrastination by sharing that as well.

"a move, a relationship difficulty, a new job" — defined here as examples of significant life changes that impact multiple people

### Step 20: Writing a Contract with Your Loved Ones to Deliver Postponed Goods

1. Write a contract with your loved ones in which you will deliver the commitments — defined here as follow through on promises or obligations — you have made to others but have postponed.
2. Have each party keep a copy of the contract.
3. Build in penalties for defaulting.
4. Examples of contract items: a ball game, dinner-out, vacation, or theater visit.

"Whether it's a ball game, dinner-out, vacation, or theater visit, you'll find this strategy helpful and personally rewarding, since you'll be participating in events that you also find enjoyable."

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You criticize others to hide your own refusal to act, feeling important at the expense of others while remaining stuck.
  **Solution:** When you catch yourself being a critic, ask "Do you think I need a critic at this moment?" and shift from criticizing to acting.

- **Problem:** You delay starting tasks because they feel overwhelming, using "I don't have time" or tiredness as a reason.
  **Solution:** Give yourself a specific fifteen-minute interval at a consistent time each week (for example, Wednesday from 10:00 to 10:15 P.M.) and use those fifteen minutes to begin. Much of your delaying is unnecessary since you will likely find the task enjoyable once you start.

- **Problem:** You allow worry about a specific future result to immobilize you in the present moment, replacing immediate, concrete action with concern.
  **Solution:** Treat the specific future result as a present task. Write down the specific details of your current situation, decide what you are avoiding, and begin addressing your fear of taking effective action.

- **Problem:** You get sick or tired when faced with a troublesome task, using sickness as a reason to avoid the task.
  **Solution:** Decide not to be tired until the moment before you get into bed. When you remove the reason for the illness—avoidance of a task—physical symptoms such as headaches, fatigue, or tension that stem from task avoidance commonly decrease in intensity.
