# Chapter VI: Exploring the Unknown

## Overview

This chapter confronts the fear of the unknown as a central erroneous zone and shows that the preference for safety is a habit of avoidance that limits how fully a person can live. Through a series of lessons and exercises, the author demonstrates that, according to the book's framework, the keys to breaking free from routine are: (1) spontaneity — the ability to act on impulse without requiring a plan or justification; (2) inner confidence — the deep knowing that you can cope with any situation; and (3) a willingness to abandon the need for reasons and plans. The chapter reframes failure as a myth, eliminates the "do your best" neurosis, and provides a 23-item behavioral checklist and 18 concrete strategies for actively exploring the unknown and choosing growth over safety.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you catch yourself eating the same foods, wearing the same clothes, visiting the same places, and thinking familiar thoughts without questioning these habitual patterns
- When the need for a plan, a reason, or certainty is preventing you from trying something new or taking a risk
- When you are afraid of failure, embarrassment, or the unknown and this fear is keeping you from activities or relationships you would enjoy

## Steps

### Step 1: Understand Your Conditioned Reflex to Avoid New Experiences

Recognize that early training in society tends to encourage caution at the expense of curiosity, safety at the expense of adventure. Acknowledge that these early messages ("Avoid the questionable, stay in areas that you know, never wander into the unknown") have become a habit of avoiding new experiences. Realize that this habit limits your personal satisfaction and present happiness in many ways. According to the book's framework, only the insecure strive for security. The author observes, "You may be a safety expert—an individual who shuns the unknown in favor of always knowing where he is going, and what he can expect when he gets there."

### Step 2: Abandon the Need for a Reason for Everything You Do

Accept that you can choose any action simply because you prefer it, and for no other reason. You don't need a justification for any action. Stop requiring a justification for every action, as this need for reasons keeps you from opening up and growing. When someone asks you why, remember that you don't have to provide a justification that will satisfy them — you can act just because you prefer to. As the chapter notes: "As a child you could play with a grasshopper for an hour, for no reason but that you liked it...But as an adult, you have to come up with a good reason for things." Emerson observed in his personal Journal of April 11, 1834: "Four snakes gliding up and down a hollow for no purpose that I could see. Not to eat. Not for love…But only gliding."

### Step 3: Eliminate the "If It's Unusual I Must Stay Away From It" Mentality

When you see deaf people using sign language, watch with curiosity but attempt to converse with them. When you encounter people who speak a foreign language, find a way to communicate instead of wandering off. Do not avoid the uncertainty of communicating in a language other than your own. Stop avoiding activities and people merely because they are unknown or considered socially discouraged. The author places homosexuals (people attracted to the same sex), transvestites (people who dress in clothing traditionally associated with a different gender), the handicapped (people with physical or mental disabilities), the retarded (people with intellectual disabilities), and nudists (people who practice social nudity) in the category of the socially unfamiliar — that is, the category of people whose behavior or appearance falls outside what most people encounter in daily life — noting that people are unsure how to interact and therefore avoid them entirely.

### Step 4: Practice Spontaneity

Examine your spontaneity — can you try something unfamiliar or do you cling tightly to familiar habits? Define spontaneity as being able to try something on impulse just because it is something you would enjoy. Be willing to be judged as reckless — what does the judgment of others matter when you're having an enjoyable experience discovering the unknown? Ask yourself: "Have you really lived thousands of days or have you lived one day thousands of times?" Can you spontaneously take avenues that don't always lead to a sure thing? A colleague of the author — a graduate-level instructor — asks experienced teachers in the classroom: "Have you really been teaching for thirty years or have you been teaching one year, thirty times?"

### Step 5: Eliminate Rigidity and Prejudice

Recognize that rigidity contributes to prejudice, which means to prejudge. Understand that prejudice is based less on hate or even dislike and more on the fact that it feels easier and safer to stay with the familiar. Eliminate your prejudgments and allow yourself to engage with new people and ideas. Realize that if you don't trust anyone, you can't understand — it really means you don't trust yourself in unfamiliar situations. "Being spontaneous means eliminating your prejudgments and allowing yourself to engage with new people and ideas."

### Step 6: Avoid the "Always Having a Plan" Trap

Realize that there is no such thing as planned spontaneity — it is a contradiction in terms. Don't become attached to your plan — this rigid attachment is the real problem. Make a new decision each day rather than consulting an agenda to see where you ought to be. Have sufficient confidence in yourself to be able to alter the plan. Don't let the plan overshadow your judgment. Henry, a client in his mid-twenties, suffered from planning obsession. He always ate the same breakfast, planned clothing days in advance, had dresser drawers arranged perfectly by color and size, and imposed rigid rules on his family. After counseling, he began to try spontaneous living, saw his plans as manipulators of others and convenient escapes from wandering into the risky unknown. After six months, he actually applied for a job in a firm that would require him to relocate regularly for work.

### Step 7: Test and Develop Internal Security

Perform this exercise: Imagine that without warning you are transported to a remote, unfamiliar location with no money or possessions. Ask yourself: "Could you survive this situation? Could you make friends, obtain food and shelter, or would you simply feel helpless about how unfortunate you were to have this sudden misfortune?" Relying on external security leaves you vulnerable. But if you have inner confidence and are not afraid of the unknown, then you would survive. Redefine security as the knowledge that you can cope with difficult situations, including having no external support. Don't be trapped by dependence on external support, since it robs you of your ability to live and grow and live fully. Take a look at people without external support, people who don't have it all mapped out — people who have learned to rely on their own resourcefulness are perhaps better off than those who depend entirely on external structures and guarantees. James Kavanaugh's poem "Some Day" captures this spirit: "Someday I'll walk away / And be free / And leave the sterile ones / Their secure sterility. I'll leave without a forwarding address / And walk across some barren wilderness / To drop the world there. Then wander free of care / Like an unemployed Atlas."

### Step 8: Redefine Failure

Understand that failure as a permanent state does not exist — it is simply someone else's subjective opinion of how a certain specific act should have been completed. Once you believe that no single correct method exists for most actions, then failing becomes impossible. Not to succeed in a particular endeavor is not to fail as a person — it is simply not being successful with that particular trial at that particular present moment. Apply natural behavior logic to yourself: Natural behavior logic is the principle that natural behaviors — behaviors arising from instinct rather than conscious self-evaluation — are performed without self-evaluation, judgment of success or failure, or comparison to external standards. Spiders construct webs as a matter of instinct, building the web they are capable of building without declaring it a "successful web" or an "unsuccessful web." Cats hunt mice; if they aren't successful in one attempt, they simply go after another. They don't lie there and whine or experience severe anxiety because they failed. Natural behavior simply is! Rid yourself of the fear of failure by applying this same natural behavior logic to your own behavior. "Consider a dog barking for fifteen minutes, and someone saying, 'He really isn't very good at barking, I'd give him a C.' How absurd! It is impossible for an animal to fail because there is no provision for evaluating natural behavior — natural behavior operates outside the framework of human judgment and grading."

### Step 9: Eliminate the "Do Your Best" Neurosis

Change the maxim from "Do your best" to simply "Do." Don't paralyze yourself with perfectionistic do-your-best nonsense. For most activities, having to do your best, or even to do well, is an obstacle to doing. Don't let perfectionism keep you on the sidelines avoiding activities you might enjoy. If you have children, don't create paralysis and bitterness by insisting they do their best. Instead, talk with them about the activities they enjoy most, and give encouragement to try hard in those areas. In other activities, emphasize that doing is so much more important than succeeding. Teach them to play in the volleyball game rather than sit on the sidelines saying "I'm no good." Teach the lessons of self-esteem and pride and pleasure in the activities the individual values, not competitiveness or trying to do well. Louann, an eighteen-year-old straight-A student, was focused on achievement and had developed a physical tic when talking about socializing. She began to place importance on her feelings and set strict standards to learning new social behaviors. She received her first "C" in a college freshman English class, which Louann's mother was concerned about. The author recommended Louann celebrate it openly and take Louann out for dinner in celebration.

### Step 10: Don't Equate Your Self-Worth with Achievement

Refuse to use achievement rewards as substitutes for trying something new and unknown. Don't reside in safe areas of inquiry because "I know I can get an A." Risk a lower grade by embarking on a new discipline rather than staying safe. Don't take the safe job where you know you'll be successful instead of trying something new and risking failure. Think of Thomas Edison — if he'd used failure as an indicator of his own self-esteem, he would have given up on himself after his first trial at inventing a commercially viable light bulb. See failure as instructive, as an incentive to work and exploration. "Nothing fails like success because you do not learn anything from it. The only thing we ever learn from is failure. Success often reinforces unexamined beliefs."

### Step 11: Use the "Fear of the Unknown" Behavior Checklist

Assess your own behavior using this checklist and challenge yourself on why you want to live each day identically to the one before — examining whether sameness is a choice or an unexamined habit:

1. Eating the same kinds of foods for years — avoid exotic, new taste treats
2. Wearing the same kinds of clothes forever — never trying out a new style
3. Reading the same newspapers and magazines which support the same editorial position routinely and never admitting a contrary viewpoint
4. Seeing the same movies (with different titles) for years — refusing to view anything that might support a different philosophical or political belief
5. Living in the same neighborhood, city or state, simply because your parents happened to have chosen that location
6. Refusing to listen to ideas which you do not share — immediately insisting the speaker is crazy or uninformed
7. Being afraid to try a new activity because you can't do it well — saying "I don't think I'd be very good, I'll just watch"
8. Compulsive achievement in school or on the job — grades more important than anything else, using achievement rewards as substitutes for trying something new
9. Avoiding anyone whom you label deviant — using derogatory tags as protection from fear of the unfamiliar
10. Staying with the same job even though you dislike it, out of apprehension about entering the uncertainty of new work
11. Staying in a marriage that obviously is not working, out of fear of the unknown single life
12. Taking vacations in the same place, at the same hotel, during the same season each year
13. Using performance rather than enjoyment as criteria for everything you do
14. Measuring things in monetary terms — if it costs more, it is worth more
15. Striving for the important title, fancy automobiles, right labels on clothes and other status symbols
16. An inability to alter a plan when an interesting alternative arises
17. Being preoccupied with time, allowing clocks to govern your life — sleeping, eating and making love by the clock
18. Dismissing certain kinds of activities which you've never tried (meditation, yoga, astrology, backgammon, bocci, Mah-jongg, isometrics)
19. Viewing sex without imagination — always doing the same thing in the same position
20. Hiding behind the same clique of friends and never branching out with different people
21. At a party, staying with your spouse or date for the entire evening not because you want to but because you are safe
22. Hanging back because of a fear of what might happen if you ventured into a conversation with strange people on strange topics
23. Condemning yourself if you don't succeed in your endeavors

### Step 12: Recognize the Psychological Support System That Keeps You From Exploring

Understand that by staying the same you never have to think on your feet — if you have a good plan, you simply consult your script rather than your wits. Recognize that staying with the familiar has its own built-in reward system — the fear of the unfamiliar is strong, and as long as you stay with the familiar you are keeping that fear at bay. Don't justify staying with the familiar by saying you're "postponing gratification" or engaging in mature-behavior (claiming that responsible, mature conduct requires staying the course and avoiding novelty) — this is really out of panic and misgiving. Don't feel important by having done it right and equating your self-worth with good performance — the right thing (the socially approved or conventionally correct action in a given situation) is only someone else's editorial opinion. "Think of Columbus. Everyone warned him he would fall over the edge. It is easier to be one of the many treading familiar ground rather than an explorer risking everything."

### Step 13: Implement Specific Strategies to Come to Grips With the Unknown

Make selective efforts — that is, intentional, targeted efforts — to try new things even if you are tempted to stay with the familiar — at a restaurant, order a new dish. Why? Because it would be different and you might enjoy it. Invite a collection of people — a small, deliberately chosen group — to your home who represent widely divergent points of view — views that differ significantly from your own and from those held by the people you usually socialize with — go with the unknown rather than your typical clique — the small, insular circle of friends and acquaintances you normally spend time with — of acquaintances. Give up having to have a reason for everything you do. Begin to take some risks that will get you out of your routine: take an unplanned vacation with no reservations or maps, where you just trust yourself to handle whatever might come along; interview for a new job; talk with someone that you've been avoiding because you were afraid of not knowing what might happen; take a new route to work; have dinner at midnight. Entertain yourself with a fantasy in which you allow yourself to have anything you want — no holds barred, unlimited money for a two-week period. You'll find that most of your mental meanderings — the random desires and daydreams that pass through your mind — are indeed attainable for you. Take a risk that might involve some personal upheaval — a disruption to your routine or comfort zone — but which would be intensely rewarding — deeply satisfying and fulfilling — to you. Whenever you find yourself avoiding the unknown, ask yourself: "What's the worst thing that could happen to me?" You'll probably find that the fears of the unknown — the anxiety about what unfamiliar situations might bring — are out of proportion — disproportionately large relative to the actual risk — to the reality of the consequences. Try to do something silly — meaning lighthearted and undignified — like going barefoot in the park, or skinny-dipping — open your own personal horizons to new experiences that you've previously avoided because they were silly or inane. Remind yourself that the fear of failure is very often — in a large proportion of cases — the fear of someone else's disapproval or ridicule — the judgment or mockery of other people — let them have their own opinions, which have nothing to do with you. Make an attempt to do some things that you've always avoided with the sentence "I'm just not good at that" — spend an afternoon painting a picture and have a hell of a time. If the end product is less than masterful — not of professional or expert quality — you haven't failed, you've had a half day of pleasure. Remember that the opposites of growth are sameness — remaining static and unchanging — and death — the complete cessation of growth and vitality. Remember that the opposites of growth are sameness and death.

### Step 14: Choose Growth Over Security

Remember that, according to the book's framework, security is a myth — as long as you are a person on earth, you can never have security. Recognize that security means knowing what is going to happen, no excitement, no risks, no challenge, no growth. According to the book's framework, choose the great uncharted unknown — the path of uncertainty, exploration, and growth that lies entirely outside your comfort zone — over security, like the road less traveled by (a reference to Robert Frost's poem "The Road Not Taken," symbolizing the deliberate choice of growth over familiarity). Understand that your erroneous zone — your fear of the unknown that keeps you stuck in familiar but unfulfilling patterns — is waiting to be replaced by new exciting activities — activities that challenge you to grow, explore, and step outside your comfort zone — that will bring pleasure to your life. Realize that you don't have to know where you're going — as long as you're on your way. "Think of a road with a fork in it. In one direction lies security, in the other the great uncharted unknown. Which road would you take?" Robert Frost answered in The Road Not Taken: "Two roads diverged in a wood, and I— / I took the one less traveled by, / And that has made all the difference."

## Examples

### Example 1: Henry and the "Having-a-Plan" Neurosis

Henry, a client in his mid-twenties, always ate the same breakfast, planned clothing days in advance, had dresser drawers arranged perfectly by color and size, and imposed rigid rules on his family. He suffered from planning obsession. After counseling, he began to try spontaneous living, saw his plans as manipulators of others and convenient escapes from wandering into the risky unknown. ### Example 2: The Author's Colleague Who Lived in Europe

A colleague of the author who taught graduate courses stayed with the same university, consulting work, and comfortable lifestyle for an extended period — approximately five years. In 1974 he decided to live in Europe for six months because it was something he had always wanted to do. He taught two courses for an overseas graduate program and learned firsthand that he could handle the doubtful. After three weeks in Germany, because of his inner confidence, he had as many opportunities to conduct workshops, work with clients, and give lectures as he had had in New York. Even in a remote village in Turkey, where he lived for two months, he was busier than he had been in New York.

### Example 3: Louann and the "C" in English

Louann, an eighteen-year-old straight-A student, was focused on achievement and had developed a physical tic when talking about socializing. She began to place importance on her feelings and set strict standards to learning new social behaviors. She received her first "C" in a college freshman English class, which Louann's mother was concerned about. The author recommended Louann celebrate it openly and take Louann out for dinner in celebration — illustrating that doing, not achieving, is what matters.

### Example 4: The Reader With the Hidden-Camera Editorial

"In one recent study, a reader, whose political position was well known, was asked to read an editorial which began with a position identical to his own. In the middle of the editorial, the viewpoint shifted, and a hidden camera revealed that the reader's eyes similarly shifted to another part of the page." This illustrates how people refuse to engage with ideas that differ from their own — a hallmark of the fear of the unknown.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Accept that you can choose any action simply because you prefer it — no external justification required
- ✅ Practice spontaneity regularly: try a new food, take a new route, have dinner at midnight
- ✅ Redefine failure as simply a result that is different from what you hoped for — not a judgment on your person
- ✅ Replace "Do your best" with simply "Do" — action matters more than perfection
- ✅ Separate your self-worth from achievement — you are worthwhile regardless of outcomes
- ✅ Develop inner confidence by imagining yourself handling extreme uncertainty — situations in which all familiar supports and references are absent and you must rely entirely on your own resourcefulness
- ✅ Engage with people, ideas, and activities that feel unfamiliar rather than avoiding them
- ✅ Make a Declaration of Independence from the people and beliefs that keep you small and afraid
- ❌ Don't let your attachment to a plan override your judgment — be willing to alter it when a better opportunity arises
- ❌ Don't measure your life by performance, status symbols, or how it compares to others
- ❌ Don't stay in a job, marriage, or neighborhood solely out of fear of the unknown
- ❌ Don't avoid trying something new because you fear you won't be good at it
- ❌ Don't confuse postponing gratification with hiding from the unknown out of panic
- ❌ Don't let perfectionism keep you on the sidelines of your own life

## Security & Safety Notes

This chapter addresses emotional and psychological patterns rather than digital or financial security. The inner confidence exercise (imagining being stripped naked — meaning stripped of all possessions, social roles, status symbols, and external supports — and dropped in an unfamiliar location) is a thought experiment designed to build confidence in your resourcefulness — it is not an instruction to take unplanned risks in physically unsafe situations. Apply these principles thoughtfully and at a pace that matches your current life circumstances, responsibilities, and personal readiness for change.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You confuse spontaneity with recklessness and resist change entirely, living the same routine day after day.
  **Solution:** Start small — try a new food, take a new route, have a conversation with someone outside your usual circle. According to the book's framework, spontaneity is a muscle; use the 23-item behavioral checklist to identify areas where you have become rigid and choose one item to challenge per week, working systematically through the list.

- **Problem:** You use "doing your best" and perfectionism to avoid trying anything you might not excel at, which keeps you on the sidelines of your own life.
  **Solution:** Replace "Do your best" with simply "Do." Focus on the experience of participating rather than the outcome. The author's recommendation to Louann — celebrating a "C" with dinner — captures the spirit: doing something imperfectly is better than not doing it at all.

- **Problem:** You equate your self-worth with achievement and stay in safe, familiar areas where you know you will succeed, rather than risking failure in new domains.
  **Solution:** Recognize that failure is not a judgment on your person — it is simply a result that is different from what you hoped for. Think of Thomas Edison, who saw every failed experiment as instructive rather than as evidence of inadequacy. Risk a lower grade or an uncertain outcome in exchange for genuine growth.

- **Problem:** You justify staying with the familiar by calling it "mature" or "postponing gratification" when it is actually fear of the unknown in disguise.
  **Solution:** Ask yourself honestly: "What's the worst thing that could happen to me?" and "What am I avoiding?" According to the book's framework, in most cases the feared consequences are disproportionate to the actual reality of what could happen, meaning the mind amplifies potential risks far beyond their actual likelihood or severity, and what you are avoiding is the discomfort of being bad at something new.
