# Chapter VI: Exploring the Unknown

## Overview

This chapter confronts the fear of the unknown as a central erroneous zone and shows that the conditioned preference for safety, predictability, and security is a psychological barrier that robs life of its fullest expression. Through a series of lessons and exercises, the author demonstrates that spontaneity, internal security, and a willingness to abandon the need for reasons and plans are the keys to breaking free from a life lived on repeat. The chapter reframes failure as a myth, eliminates the "do your best" neurosis, and provides a 23-item behavioral checklist and 18 concrete strategies for actively exploring the unknown and choosing growth over safety.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you catch yourself eating the same foods, wearing the same clothes, visiting the same places, and thinking the same thoughts on autopilot
- When the need for a plan, a reason, or guaranteed security is preventing you from trying something new or taking a risk
- When you are afraid of failure, embarrassment, or the unknown and this fear is keeping you from activities or relationships you would genuinely enjoy

## Steps

### Step 1: Understand Your Conditioned Reflex to Avoid New Experiences

Recognize that early training in society tends to encourage caution at the expense of curiosity, safety at the expense of adventure. Acknowledge that these early messages ("Avoid the questionable, stay in areas that you know, never wander into the unknown") have become a psychological barrier. Realize that this barrier prevents your own personal fulfillment and present-moment happiness in countless ways. "Only the insecure strive for security. You may be a safety expert—an individual who shuns the unknown in favor of always knowing where he is going, and what he can expect when he gets there."

### Step 2: Abandon the Need for a Reason for Everything You Do

Accept that you can do anything you want just because you want to, and for no other reason. You don't need a reason for anything that you do. Stop looking for a reason for everything you do, as this passion for reasons keeps you from opening up and growing. When someone asks you why, remember that you don't have to come up with a reasonable answer that will satisfy them — you can do what you decide just because you want to. As the chapter notes: "As a child you could play with a grasshopper for an hour, for no reason but that you liked it...But as an adult, you have to come up with a good reason for things." Emerson observed in his Journal of April 11, 1834: "Four snakes gliding up and down a hollow for no purpose that I could see. Not to eat. Not for love…But only gliding."

### Step 3: Eliminate the "If It's Unusual I Must Stay Away From It" Mentality

When you see deaf people using sign language, watch with curiosity but attempt to converse with them. When you encounter people who speak a foreign language, work it out and attempt somehow to communicate instead of wandering off. Do not avoid the vast unknown of communicating in other than your own spoken language. Stop avoiding activities and people merely because they are unknown or considered taboo. "Thus homosexuals, transvestites, the handicapped, the retarded, nudists and the like, are in the category of the obscure. You are not quite certain how to behave, and therefore you avoid the entire business."

### Step 4: Practice Spontaneity

Take a hard look at your spontaneity — can you open up to something new or do you rigidly hang onto your accustomed behavior? Define spontaneity as being able to try anything at the spur of the second just because it is something you'd enjoy. Be willing to be condemned as irresponsible or incautious — what does the judgment of others matter when you're having such a marvelous time discovering the unknown? Ask yourself: "Have you really lived 10,000 or more days or have you lived one day, 10,000 or more times?" Can you spontaneously take the avenues that don't always lead to a sure thing? A colleague of the author who teaches graduate courses asks old-timers in the classroom: "Have you really been teaching for thirty years or have you been teaching one year, thirty times?"

### Step 5: Eliminate Rigidity and Prejudice

Recognize that rigidity is the basis of all prejudice, which means to prejudge. Understand that prejudice is based less on hate or even dislike and more on the fact that it's easier and safer to stay with the known. Eliminate your prejudgments and allow yourself to meet and deal with new people and ideas. Realize that if you don't trust anyone you can't get a "handle on," it really means you don't trust yourself on unfamiliar grounds. "Being spontaneous means eliminating your prejudgments and allowing yourself to meet and deal with new people and ideas."

### Step 6: Avoid the "Always Having a Plan" Trap

Realize that there is no such thing as planned spontaneity — it is a contradiction in terms. Don't fall in love with your plan — this is the real neurosis. Make a new decision each day rather than consulting an agenda to see where you ought to be. Have a strong enough belief in yourself to be able to alter the plan. Don't let the plan become bigger than you. Henry, a client in his middle twenties, suffered from "having-a-plan-neurosis." He always ate the same breakfast, planned clothing days in advance, had dresser drawers arranged perfectly by color and size, and imposed rigid rules on his family. After counseling, he began to try spontaneous living, saw his plans as manipulators of others and convenient escapes from wandering into the risky unknown. After several months, he actually applied for a job in a firm that would require him to move frequently.

### Step 7: Test and Develop Internal Security

Perform this exercise: "Suppose that right now, this second, as you are reading this book, someone swooped down on you, stripped you naked, and carted you off in a helicopter. No warning, no money, nothing but yourself. Supposing you were flown to the middle of Red China and dropped in a field." Ask yourself: "Would you survive or collapse? Could you make friends, get food, shelter and the like, or would you simply lie there and moan about how unfortunate you were to have this catastrophe visited on you?" If you need external security, you'd perish. But if you have internal security and are not afraid of the unknown, then you would survive. Redefine security as the knowledge that you can handle anything, including having no external security. Don't be trapped by external security, since it robs you of your ability to live and grow and fulfill yourself. Take a look at people without external security, people who don't have it all mapped out — perhaps they are way ahead of the game. James Kavanaugh's poem "Some Day" captures this spirit: "Someday I'll walk away / And be free / And leave the sterile ones / Their secure sterility. I'll leave without a forwarding address / And walk across some barren wilderness / To drop the world there. Then wander free of care / Like an unemployed Atlas."

### Step 8: Redefine Failure

Understand that failure does not exist — it is simply someone else's opinion of how a certain act should have been completed. Once you believe that no act must be performed in any specific other-directed way, then failing becomes impossible. Not to succeed in a particular endeavor is not to fail as a person — it is simply not being successful with that particular trial at that particular present moment. Apply natural behavior logic to yourself: Spiders construct webs, not successful or unsuccessful webs. Cats hunt mice; if they aren't successful in one attempt, they simply go after another. They don't lie there and whine or have a nervous breakdown because they failed. Natural behavior simply is! Rid yourself of the fear of failure by applying this same logic to your own behavior. "Consider a dog barking for fifteen minutes, and someone saying, 'He really isn't very good at barking, I'd give him a C.' How absurd! It is impossible for an animal to fail because there is no provision for evaluating natural behavior."

### Step 9: Eliminate the "Do Your Best" Neurosis

Change the maxim from "Do your best" to simply "Do." Don't paralyze yourself with perfectionistic do-your-best nonsense. In the vast majority of activities, having to do your best, or even to do well, is an obstacle to doing. Don't let perfectionism keep you on the sidelines avoiding potentially pleasurable activities. If you have children, don't breed paralysis and resentment by insisting they do their best. Instead, talk with them about the things they seem to enjoy the most, and give encouragement to try hard in those areas. In other activities, emphasize that doing is so much more important than succeeding. Teach them to play in the volleyball game rather than sit on the sidelines saying "I'm no good." Teach the lessons of self-esteem and pride and pleasure in the activities deemed important by the individual, not competitiveness or trying to do well. Louann, an eighteen-year-old straight-A student, was imbued with the achievement standard and had developed a nervous twitch when talking about socializing. She began to place importance on her feelings and applied rigorous standards to learning new social behaviors. She received her first "C" in a college freshman English class, which her mother was concerned about. The author recommended she make a big production out of it and take her out for dinner in celebration.

### Step 10: Don't Equate Your Self-Worth with Achievement

Refuse to use achievement rewards as substitutes for trying something new and unknown. Don't reside in safe areas of inquiry because "I know I can get an A." Risk a lower grade by embarking on a new discipline rather than staying safe. Don't take the safe job where you know you'll be successful instead of entering a new race and taking a chance on failing. Think of Thomas Edison — if he'd used failure as an indicator of his own self-esteem, he would have given up on himself after his first trial. See failure as instructive, as an incentive to work and exploration. "Nothing fails like success because you do not learn anything from it. The only thing we ever learn from is failure. Success only confirms our superstitions."

### Step 11: Use the "Fear of the Unknown" Behavior Checklist

Assess your own behavior using this checklist and challenge why you want to live each day the same as the one before:

1. Eating the same kinds of foods for a lifetime — avoid exotic, new taste treats
2. Wearing the same kinds of clothes forever — never trying out a new style
3. Reading the same newspapers and magazines which support the same editorial position day after day and never admitting a contrary viewpoint
4. Seeing the same movies (with different titles) for a lifetime — refusing to view anything that might support a different philosophical or political belief
5. Living in the same neighborhood, city or state, simply because your parents happened to have chosen that location
6. Refusing to listen to ideas which you do not share — immediately insisting the speaker is crazy or uninformed
7. Being afraid to try a new activity because you can't do it well — saying "I don't think I'd be very good, I'll just watch"
8. Compulsive achievement in school or on the job — grades more important than anything else, using achievement rewards as substitutes for trying something new
9. Avoiding anyone whom you label deviant — using derogatory tags as protection from fear of the unfamiliar
10. Staying with the same job even though you dislike it, out of apprehension about entering the vast unknown of new work
11. Staying in a marriage that obviously is not working, out of fear of the unknown single life
12. Taking vacations in the same place, at the same hotel, during the same season each year
13. Using performance rather than enjoyment as criteria for everything you do
14. Measuring things in monetary terms — if it costs more, it is worth more
15. Striving for the important title, fancy automobiles, right labels on clothes and other status symbols
16. An inability to alter a plan when an interesting alternative arises
17. Being preoccupied with time, allowing clocks to run your life — sleeping, eating and making love by the clock
18. Dismissing certain kinds of activities which you've never tried (meditation, yoga, astrology, backgammon, bocci, Mah-jongg, isometrics)
19. Viewing sex without imagination — always doing the same thing in the same position
20. Hiding behind the same clique of friends and never branching out with different people
21. At a party, staying with your spouse or date for the entire evening not because you want to but because you are safe
22. Hanging back because of a fear of what might happen if you ventured into a conversation with strange people on strange topics
23. Condemning yourself if you don't succeed in all your endeavors

### Step 12: Recognize the Psychological Support System That Keeps You From Exploring

Understand that by staying the same you never have to think on your feet — if you have a good plan, you simply consult your script rather than your wits. Recognize that staving off the unknown has its own built-in reward system — the fear of the obscure is strong, and as long as you stay with the familiar you are keeping that fear at bay. Don't justify staying with the familiar by saying you're "postponing gratification" or being "mature-behavior" — this is really out of panic and misgiving. Don't feel important by having done it right and equating your self-worth with good performance — the "right thing" is only someone else's editorial opinion. "Think of Columbus. Everyone warned him he would fall over the edge. It is easier to be one of the many treading familiar ground rather than an explorer risking everything."

### Step 13: Implement Specific Strategies to Come to Grips With the Unknown

Make selective efforts to try new things even if you are tempted to stay with the familiar — at a restaurant, order a new dish. Why? Because it would be different and you might enjoy it. Invite a collection of people to your home who represent widely divergent points of view — go with the unknown rather than your typical clique of acquaintances. Give up having to have a reason for everything you do. Begin to take some risks that will get you out of your routine: take an unplanned vacation with no reservations or maps, where you just trust yourself to handle whatever might come along; interview for a new job; talk with someone that you've been avoiding because you were afraid of not knowing what might happen; take a new route to work; have dinner at midnight. Entertain yourself with a fantasy in which you allow yourself to have anything you want — no holds barred, unlimited money for a two-week period. You'll find that almost all of your mental meanderings are indeed attainable for you. Take a risk that might involve some personal upheaval but which would be intensely rewarding to you. Whenever you find yourself avoiding the unknown, ask yourself: "What's the worst thing that could happen to me?" You'll probably see that the fears of the unknown are out of proportion to the reality of the consequences. Try to do something silly like going barefoot in the park, or skinny-dipping — open your own personal horizons to new experiences that you've previously avoided because they were silly or inane. Remind yourself that the fear of failure is very often the fear of someone else's disapproval or ridicule — let them have their own opinions, which have nothing to do with you. Make an attempt to do some things that you've always avoided with the sentence "I'm just not good at that" — spend an afternoon painting a picture and have a hell of a time. If the end product is less than masterful, you haven't failed, you've had a half day of pleasure. Remember that the opposites of growth are sameness and death — resolve to live each day in a new way, being spontaneous and alive. Have a talk with those people in your life who you feel are most responsible for your fear of the unknown — state in no uncertain terms that you intend to do new things, and check out their reactions. State your Declaration of Independence from their control. Instead of "Do your best in everything" as a credo, try "Select the things that are important to you, and work hard at them, and in the rest of your life, just do." It's O.K. not to do your best! Don't let your convictions keep you stagnant — believing something out of past experience and hanging onto that belief is an avoidance of reality. Assess your behavior not on what you believe, but in terms of what is and what you experience in the present. Remember: "Nothing human is alien to you. You can be anything you choose." Imprint this on your mind and remind yourself of it when you fall into your typically safe evasive behavior. Become aware of avoiding the unknown as you are doing it — at that moment begin a dialogue with yourself. Tell yourself it's okay if you don't know where you are going at every moment of your life. Deliberately fail at something — ask yourself if you're really less of a person for losing a tennis game or painting a bad picture, or are you still a worthwhile individual who just enjoyed some pleasant activity. Have a conversation with a member of a group that you've avoided in the past — you'll soon discover that your prejudices, when challenged by you, are keeping you stagnant and uninteresting.

### Step 14: Choose Growth Over Security

Remember that security is a myth — as long as you are a person on earth, you can never have security. Recognize that security means knowing what is going to happen, no excitement, no risks, no challenge, no growth. Choose the great uncharted unknown over security, like the road less traveled by. Understand that your erroneous zone of fear of the unknown is waiting to be replaced by new exciting activities that will bring pleasure to your life. Realize that you don't have to know where you're going — as long as you're on your way. "Think of a road with a fork in it. In one direction lies security, in the other the great uncharted unknown. Which road would you take?" Robert Frost answered in The Road Not Taken: "Two roads diverged in a wood, and I— / I took the one less traveled by, / And that has made all the difference."

## Examples

### Example 1: Henry and the "Having-a-Plan" Neurosis

Henry, a client in his middle twenties, always ate the same breakfast, planned clothing days in advance, had dresser drawers arranged perfectly by color and size, and imposed rigid rules on his family. He suffered from "having-a-plan-neurosis." After counseling, he began to try spontaneous living, saw his plans as manipulators of others and convenient escapes from wandering into the risky unknown. After several months, he actually applied for a job in a firm that would require him to move frequently.

### Example 2: The Author's Colleague Who Lived in Europe

A colleague of the author who taught graduate courses stayed with the same university, consulting work, and comfortable lifestyle for years. In 1974 he decided to live in Europe for six months because it was something he had always wanted to do. He taught two courses for an overseas graduate program and learned firsthand that he could handle the doubtful. After three weeks in Germany, because of his inner security, he had as many opportunities to conduct workshops, work with clients, and give lectures as he had had in New York. Even in a remote village in Turkey, where he lived for two months, he was busier than he had been in New York.

### Example 3: Louann and the "C" in English

Louann, an eighteen-year-old straight-A student, was imbued with the achievement standard and had developed a nervous twitch when talking about socializing. She began to place importance on her feelings and applied rigorous standards to learning new social behaviors. She received her first "C" in a college freshman English class, which her mother was concerned about. The author recommended she make a big production out of it and take her out for dinner in celebration — illustrating that doing, not achieving, is what matters.

### Example 4: The Reader With the Hidden-Camera Editorial

"In one recent study, a reader, whose political position was well known, was asked to read an editorial which began with a position identical to his own. In the middle of the editorial, the viewpoint shifted, and a hidden camera revealed that the reader's eyes similarly shifted to another part of the page." This illustrates how people refuse to engage with ideas that differ from their own — a hallmark of the fear of the unknown.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Accept that you can do anything you want simply because you want to — no external justification required
- ✅ Practice spontaneity regularly: try a new food, take a new route, have dinner at midnight
- ✅ Redefine failure as simply a result that is different from what you hoped for — not a judgment on your person
- ✅ Replace "Do your best" with simply "Do" — action matters more than perfection
- ✅ Separate your self-worth from achievement — you are worthwhile regardless of outcomes
- ✅ Develop internal security by imagining yourself handling extreme uncertainty with resourcefulness
- ✅ Engage with people, ideas, and activities that feel unfamiliar rather than avoiding them
- ✅ Make a Declaration of Independence from the people and beliefs that keep you small and afraid
- ❌ Don't let your plan become bigger than you — be willing to alter it when a better opportunity arises
- ❌ Don't measure your life by performance, status symbols, or how it compares to others
- ❌ Don't stay in a job, marriage, or neighborhood solely out of fear of the unknown
- ❌ Don't avoid trying something new because you fear you won't be good at it
- ❌ Don't confuse postponing gratification with hiding from the unknown out of panic
- ❌ Don't let perfectionism keep you on the sidelines of your own life

## Security & Safety Notes

This chapter addresses emotional and psychological patterns rather than digital or financial security. The internal security exercise (imagining being stripped naked and dropped in a foreign country) is a thought experiment designed to build confidence in your resourcefulness — it is not an instruction to take unplanned risks in physically unsafe situations. Apply these principles thoughtfully and at a pace appropriate to your circumstances.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You confuse spontaneity with recklessness and resist change entirely, living the same day on repeat.
  **Solution:** Start small — try a new food, take a new route, have a conversation with someone outside your usual circle. Spontaneity is a muscle; use the 23-item behavioral checklist to identify areas where you have become rigid and choose one item to challenge this week.

- **Problem:** You use "doing your best" and perfectionism to avoid trying anything you might not excel at, which keeps you on the sidelines of your own life.
  **Solution:** Replace "Do your best" with simply "Do." Focus on the experience of participating rather than the outcome. The author's recommendation to Louann — celebrating a "C" with dinner — captures the spirit: doing something imperfectly is better than not doing it at all.

- **Problem:** You equate your self-worth with achievement and stay in safe, familiar areas where you know you will succeed, rather than risking failure in new domains.
  **Solution:** Recognize that failure is not a judgment on your person — it is simply a result that is different from what you hoped for. Think of Thomas Edison, who saw every failed experiment as instructive rather than as evidence of inadequacy. Risk a lower grade or a uncertain outcome in exchange for genuine growth.

- **Problem:** You justify staying with the familiar by calling it "mature" or "postponing gratification" when it is actually fear of the unknown in disguise.
  **Solution:** Ask yourself honestly: "What's the worst thing that could happen to me?" and "What am I avoiding?" Most often the feared consequences are disproportionate to the reality, and what you are avoiding is the discomfort of being bad at something new.
