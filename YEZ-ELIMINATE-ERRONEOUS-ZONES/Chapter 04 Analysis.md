# Chapter IV: Breaking Free from the Past

## Overview

This chapter teaches that self-defeating "I'm" labels—such as "I'm shy," "I'm not good at math," or "I'm clumsy"—are learned avoidance patterns, not fixed traits, and are the primary mechanism through which the past controls your present. It provides a structured process for identifying, challenging, and eliminating these labels by understanding their origins, recognizing the neurotic dividends they pay, and replacing them with present-moment, choice-based language. Through 19 focused lessons, the chapter gives you the tools to stop being a finished product of your history and start being the sum of the choices you make today.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you catch yourself using self-limiting "I'm" labels—"I'm bad at math," "I'm shy," "I'm not musical"—and accepting them as unchangeable facts about yourself
- When you are immobilized by patterns you can't break, even though you know they are holding you back from the life you want
- When you want to stop letting childhood messages, old choices, or other people's opinions determine who you are today

## Steps

### Step 1: Identify and List Your Self-Descriptors ("I'm" Labels)

Ask yourself: "Who are you?" and "How do you describe yourself?" — write down every label you use, including both positive and negative ones. Compile a complete list of your self-descriptors (I'm nervous, I'm shy, I'm lazy, I'm not musical, I'm clumsy, I'm forgetful, etc.). Focus specifically on the negative, self-canceling "I'm" labels — the ones that get in your way. Examine whether you are genuinely satisfied with any of these labels or whether they limit you. Decide which labels you are willing to change.

### Step 2: Recognize the Four Neurotic Sentences That Keep You Chained to Your Past

Identify every self-defeating "I'm" you use as rooted in one or more of the following four neurotic sentences:

- "That's me."
- "I've always been that way."
- "I can't help it."
- "That's my nature."

Understand that each time you use one of these sentences, you are saying, "And I intend to continue being the way I've always been." Check yourself on the extent to which you are chained to your past by examining how often you use these four sentences.

### Step 3: Understand the Two Origins of Your "I'm" Labels

Categorize each of your "I'm" labels into one of two origins:

- **From others:** Labels pinned on you as a child by parents, teachers, grandparents, family friends, etc.
- **From self-created avoidance:** Labels you adopted to avoid uncomfortable, difficult, or distasteful activities.

For labels from others: Recognize that they are leftovers from childhood — accepted without challenge. For self-created labels: Recognize that they are adaptive but delusional excuses used to avoid something. Understand that in either case, you are stating, "I am a finished product in this area, and I am never going to be any different."

### Step 4: Have a Conversation With Those Responsible for Your "I'm" Labels

Identify the people in your life most responsible for many of your "I'm" labels (parents, long-time family friends, old teachers, grandparents, etc.). Have a conversation with them in which you ask: "How do you think I got to be the way I am? Have I always been that way?" Tell them you are determined to change, and ask them whether they believe you are capable of being different. Be prepared for their interpretations — they may feel you "can't be any different, since you've always been that way."

### Step 5: Learn the Ten Typical "I'm" Categories and Their Neurotic Dividends

Review the ten categories below and identify which ones apply to you. For each applicable category, name the specific "neurotic dividend" (payoff) you receive by keeping the label — this is what you avoid by holding onto it. Recognize that each neurotic dividend is a form of avoidance. Decide which labels you want to eliminate.

**The Ten Categories:**

1. **I'm poor at math, spelling, reading, languages, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Guarantees you won't put in the effort to change. The academic "I'm" keeps you from doing the hard work of mastering difficult or boring subject matter. As long as you label yourself inept, you have a built-in reason to avoid it.

2. **I'm lousy at some skill areas such as cooking, sports, crocheting, drawing, acting, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Assures you won't have to do any of these things in the future and justifies poor past performance. Reinforces the absurd notion that you shouldn't do anything if you don't do it really well. Avoidance is better than doing unless you're the world champion.

3. **I'm shy, reserved, temperamental, nervous, afraid, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Calls on genetics as an excuse. Lets you blame your parents and use them as the reason for your current "I'm." You choose this behavior to avoid being assertive in troublesome situations. It's a leftover from childhood when others had you believe you were incapable of thinking for yourself. You excuse all self-forfeiting behavior as out of your control.

4. **I'm clumsy, uncoordinated, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Enables you to avoid potential ridicule for lack of physical skill. The lack of skill comes from believing the label and avoiding physical activity, not from some inherent defect. "You get good at what you practice, not what you shun."

5. **I'm unattractive, ugly, big-boned, plain, too tall, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Keeps you from taking risks with the opposite sex and justifies poor self-image and lack of love. Gives you a ready-made excuse for not putting yourself on the line in a love relationship. "There's only one problem: we see exactly what we choose to—even in mirrors."

6. **I'm unorganized, meticulous, sloppy, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Convenient for manipulating others and justifying why things must be done a certain way. "I've always done it that way." By relying on tradition, you don't have to entertain the risky notion of doing it differently, and you can ensure everyone else does it your way. This "I'm" calls upon "policy" as a substitute for thinking.

7. **I'm forgetful, careless, irresponsible, apathetic, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Especially useful to vindicate yourself for ineffective behavior. The "I'm" keeps you from ever working on your memory or carelessness, and you excuse yourself with "That's me." As long as you haul out this "I'm," you never have to work at changing.

8. **I'm Italian, German, Jewish, Irish, Black, Chinese, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Works well when you run out of other reasons to explain behaviors that don't work for you but are too difficult to tackle. You trot out your ethnic "I'm" as a justification for stereotypical behavior. Example: A maître d' responds to being asked why he overreacts, "What do you expect from me? I'm Italian. I can't help it."

9. **I'm bossy, pushy, authoritarian, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Allows you to continue hostile acts rather than work at developing self-discipline. You varnish the behavior with "I can't help it, I've always been that way."

10. **I'm old, middle-aged, tired, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Uses age as a reason for not participating in risky or threatening activities (sports, dating after divorce or death of a spouse, traveling, etc.). Eliminates all attendant risks of trying something new. The implication is that you are absolutely finished in that area, and since you will always get older, you are finished growing.

### Step 6: Understand the "I'm Circle" — The Vicious Cycle of Self-Labeling

Understand the cycle: A thought or belief about yourself (point 1) → leads to a feeling (point 2) → leads to the behavior you expect of yourself (point 3) → which reinforces the original thought (point 4), completing the circle. Recognize that your "I'm" is what you call upon to exonerate yourself and explain why you persist in a self-defeating pattern — instead of intervening between points 3 and 4. Challenge any aspect of your life in which you've chosen to be a "finished product." Break the circle by choosing differently at point 3 — act in a way that contradicts the label, rather than confirming it. Reframe your sentence: Change "I'm shy" to "Up until now, I've behaved in a shy manner."

### Step 7: Do Not Give Others Responsibility for Your Present-Day "I'm"

Recognize that blaming your parents, teachers, or other significant adults for your present-day "I'm" gives them a measure of control over your life today. Understand that blaming others elevates them above you and ingeniously creates an alibi for staying in your ineffective condition. Recognize that this provides a warranty against any risk-taking — if it's the "culture's fault," you can't do anything about it. Accept full responsibility: Every "I'm" you treasure could be relabeled, "I've chosen to be."

### Step 8: Eliminate the Word "I'm" and Substitute With Past-Tense Language

Eliminate the word "I'm" wherever you can in reference to your self-defeating labels. Substitute "I'm" sentences with:

- "Until today I've chosen to be that way."
- "I used to label myself…"

Understand that "There is no such thing as human nature" — it is a phrase designed to pigeonhole people and create excuses. You are the sum product of your choices.

### Step 9: Announce Your Commitment to Eliminating "I'm" Labels to Those Close to You

Decide which "I'm" labels are most important to eliminate. Announce to those close to you that you are going to work at eliminating these specific "I'm" labels. Ask them to remind you whenever you haul out one of your old "I'm" labels. Let their reminders serve as an accountability mechanism.

### Step 10: Set Behavioral Goals to Act Differently Than You've Ever Done Before

For each "I'm" you want to eliminate, set a concrete behavioral goal that forces you to act in direct contradiction of the label. Make the goal small and specific for that moment. Carry it out regardless of how you feel. For example: If you consider yourself shy, introduce yourself to one person you might otherwise have avoided.

### Step 11: Enlist a Trusted Confidant to Signal You When You Fall Back Into Your "I'm"

Identify a trusted confidant who will help you combat the powers of the past. Ask this person to signal you silently with a pre-arranged signal (e.g., a tug to his ear) each time he notices you falling back into your "I'm." Use the signal as a real-time reminder to correct yourself in that moment.

### Step 12: Keep a Journal on Your Self-Destructive "I'm" Behavior

Keep a journal dedicated to tracking your self-destructive "I'm" behavior. For one week, record in a notebook the exact time, date, and occasion when you used any of the self-destructive "I'm" labels. Also record what action you took and how you were feeling about yourself while you were behaving that way. Use the list of ten typical "I'm" categories provided in this chapter as a guide for your journal-keeping. Work at diminishing the entries over time.

### Step 13: Correct the Four Neurotic Sentences Out Loud When You Catch Yourself Using Them

Watch out for the four neurotic sentences in your own speech. Whenever you catch yourself using one, correct yourself out loud in the following ways:

- Change **"That's me."** → **"That was me."**
- Change **"I can't help it."** → **"I can change that if I work on it."**
- Change **"I've always been that way."** → **"I'm going to be different."**
- Change **"That's my nature."** → **"That's what I used to believe was my nature."**

Say the corrected version aloud, in the moment, each time you catch yourself.

### Step 14: Work on Eliminating One "I'm" Per Day

Select one "I'm" label to eliminate for just that single day. Devote the entire day to working specifically at being aware of this tendency. Actively alter one or two behaviors associated with that "I'm" on that day. Repeat with a different "I'm" the following day, working on them one at a time until each is eliminated.

### Step 15: Interrupt Your Own "I'm Circle" Between Point 3 and Point 4

Visualize your own "I'm circle" — the cycle where a thought leads to a feeling, which leads to behavior, which reinforces the original thought. Learn to catch yourself between the feeling (point 2) and the behavior (point 3) — or more precisely, between the behavior impulse (point 3) and its reinforcing completion (point 4). At that moment of interruption, choose to toss out the ancient excuse and act in a way that breaks the pattern. Resolve at that moment to not let the "I'm" complete the cycle.

### Step 16: Try Something Totally New That You Have Always Avoided

Identify an activity you have never done before and that you have always avoided in the past due to an "I'm" label. Set aside an entire afternoon (minimum three hours) for full immersion in that new activity. Engage deeply in the activity for the full three hours. After your immersion, ask yourself: Can you still use the same "I'm" label that you applied that morning? Use the experience as evidence to challenge and potentially discard that label.

### Step 17: Reframe Your Self-Description Using Present-Moment, Choice-Based Language

Go back to the opening questions: "Who are you?" and "How do you describe yourself?" Think about new labels that are in no way connected to choices others have made for you or those you have made in the past. Reframe every old "I'm" label using present-moment language:

- Instead of "I'm [negative trait]," say "Up until now, I've behaved in a [negative] manner."

Recognize that "You are what you choose today, not what you've chosen before." Treat yourself as a sum product of your choices, not of your past.

### Step 18: Adopt the "I'm an 'I'm' Exorcist" Identity

If you must keep an "I'm" label, choose this one: "I'm an 'I'm' exorcist — and I like it." Make the exorcising of erroneous "I'm" labels a conscious part of your identity. Treat every limiting "I'm" as a demon to be exorcised. Embrace the identity of someone who actively dismantles their own self-imposed limitations.

### Step 19: Understand That All "I'm" Labels Are Learned Avoidance Patterns — and Can Be Unlearned

Accept the foundational truth: All of your "I'm" labels are learned avoidance patterns, not fixed traits. Remind yourself: "You can learn to be almost anything if you make the choice to do so." Let go of the idea that your current limitations are permanent or inherent. Make the active choice to learn something new, since "learning is the only thing which the mind can never exhaust, never alienate, never be tortured by, never fear or distrust, and never dream of regretting." Use learning itself as the antidote to sadness and stagnation.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You accept "I'm" labels as fixed, unchangeable truths about yourself—"I'm bad at math," "I'm shy," "I'm not athletic"—and don't question their origins.
  **Solution:** Categorize each label by its origin. Labels from others are childhood leftovers accepted without challenge. Self-created labels are adaptive but delusional excuses. In both cases, you are saying "I am a finished product." You are not. Ask: "Who decided this?" and realize it was a choice, not a fact.

- **Problem:** You use the four neurotic sentences ("That's me," "I've always been that way," "I can't help it," "That's my nature") to justify staying the same, and feel powerless to change.
  **Solution:** Correct each sentence out loud the moment you catch it: "That's me" → "That was me"; "I've always been that way" → "I'm going to be different"; "I can't help it" → "I can change that if I work on it"; "That's my nature" → "That's what I used to believe was my nature." Each correction is a declaration that you are choosing differently today.

- **Problem:** You blame others (parents, teachers, culture) for your present-day "I'm" labels, which gives them control over your life and provides an alibi for not changing.
  **Solution:** Accept full responsibility: Every "I'm" you treasure could be relabeled, "I've chosen to be." Blaming others elevates them above you and creates a warranty against risk-taking. You cannot change what you refuse to own.

- **Problem:** You are immobilized by a self-defeating pattern and don't know how to break the cycle—the thought produces the feeling, which produces the behavior, which reinforces the thought.
  **Solution:** Interrupt the "I'm circle" between point 3 (behavior impulse) and point 4 (reinforcing completion). At that moment of interruption, choose to act in a way that contradicts the label. Set a concrete, small behavioral goal that forces you to act differently. One new action is enough to begin breaking the pattern.
