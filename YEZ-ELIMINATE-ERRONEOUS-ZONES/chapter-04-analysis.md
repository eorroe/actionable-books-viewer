## Chapter IV: Breaking Free from the Past

### Lesson 1: Identify and List Your Self-Descriptors ("I'm" Labels)
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Ask yourself: "Who are you?" and "How do you describe yourself?" — write down every label you use, including both positive and negative ones.
  2. Compile a complete list of your self-descriptors (I'm nervous, I'm shy, I'm lazy, I'm not musical, I'm clumsy, I'm forgetful, etc.).
  3. Focus specifically on the negative, self-canceling "I'm" labels — the ones that get in your way.
  4. Examine whether you are genuinely satisfied with any of these labels or whether they limit you.
  5. Decide which labels you are willing to change.

- **Examples from book:** The book provides a sample list: "I'm Fat, I'm a Lousy Cook, I'm Shy, Not Musical, a Poor Speller, Lazy, Not Athletic, Tired Easily, Timid, Sloppy, Sickly, Afraid, Stubborn, Gauche, Clumsy, Immature, Accident Prone, Anxious, Meticulous, Short Fused, Forgetful, Careless, Hostile, Not Mechanical, Vindictive, Solemn, Poor at Mathematics, Irresponsible, Apathetic, A Loner, Nervous, Bored, Frigid."

### Lesson 2: Recognize the Four Neurotic Sentences That Keep You Chained to Your Past
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Identify every self-defeating "I'm" you use as rooted in one or more of the following four neurotic sentences:
     - "That's me."
     - "I've always been that way."
     - "I can't help it."
     - "That's my nature."
  2. Understand that each time you use one of these sentences, you are saying, "And I intend to continue being the way I've always been."
  3. Check yourself on the extent to which you are chained to your past by examining how often you use these four sentences.

- **Examples from book:** A grandmother portioning out exact plate sizes for every family member responds, "Oh, I've always been that way. That's just the way I am." A person asked why he always gets upset when the subject of accidents arises responds, "Oh, that's just me, I've always been that way. I really can't help it, it's just my nature." — using all four neurotic sentences in one response.

### Lesson 3: Understand the Two Origins of Your "I'm" Labels
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Categorize each of your "I'm" labels into one of two origins:
     - **From others:** Labels pinned on you as a child by parents, teachers, grandparents, family friends, etc.
     - **From self-created avoidance:** Labels you adopted to avoid uncomfortable, difficult, or distasteful activities.
  2. For labels from others: Recognize that they are leftovers from childhood — accepted without challenge.
  3. For self-created labels: Recognize that they are adaptive but delusional excuses used to avoid something.
  4. Understand that in either case, you are stating, "I am a finished product in this area, and I am never going to be any different."

- **Examples from book:**
  - **From others:** Little Hope in second grade is told by her art teacher she "really isn't all that good" at art. She begins to avoid it, develops the label "I'm not good at art," and as an adult says, "Oh, I'm not good at it. I've always been that way." Other childhood origins: "He's kind of clumsy; his brother is good at athletics, but he's the studious one," "You're just like me, I was never good at spelling either," "Billy was always the shy one," "She's just like her father; he couldn't carry a tune with a wheelbarrow."
  - **From self-created avoidance:** Horace, 46, wants to attend college but uses "I'm too old, I'm not smart enough, and I'm not really interested" to skirt the doing process. A colleague avoids handyman tasks by telling his wife, "Now you know, dear, I'm just not mechanical."

### Lesson 4: Have a Conversation With Those Responsible for Your "I'm" Labels
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Identify the people in your life most responsible for many of your "I'm" labels (parents, long-time family friends, old teachers, grandparents, etc.).
  2. Have a conversation with them in which you ask: "How do you think I got to be the way I am? Have I always been that way?"
  3. Tell them you are determined to change, and ask them whether they believe you are capable of being different.
  4. Be prepared for their interpretations — they may feel you "can't be any different, since you've always been that way."

- **Examples from book:** The book instructs the reader to have this conversation and notes: "You'll be surprised at their own interpretations and how they feel you can't be any different, since 'You've always been that way.'"

### Lesson 5: Learn the Ten Typical "I'm" Categories and Their Neurotic Dividends
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Review the ten categories below and identify which ones apply to you.
  2. For each applicable category, name the specific "neurotic dividend" (payoff) you receive by keeping the label — this is what you avoid by holding onto it.
  3. Recognize that each neurotic dividend is a form of avoidance.
  4. Decide which labels you want to eliminate.

- **The Ten Categories:**
  1. **I'm poor at math, spelling, reading, languages, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Guarantees you won't put in the effort to change. The academic "I'm" keeps you from doing the hard work of mastering difficult or boring subject matter. As long as you label yourself inept, you have a built-in reason to avoid it.
  2. **I'm lousy at some skill areas such as cooking, sports, crocheting, drawing, acting, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Assures you won't have to do any of these things in the future and justifies poor past performance. Reinforces the absurd notion that you shouldn't do anything if you don't do it really well. Avoidance is better than doing unless you're the world champion.
  3. **I'm shy, reserved, temperamental, nervous, afraid, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Calls on genetics as an excuse. Lets you blame your parents and use them as the reason for your current "I'm." You choose this behavior to avoid being assertive in troublesome situations. It's a leftover from childhood when others had you believe you were incapable of thinking for yourself. You excuse all self-forfeiting behavior as out of your control.
  4. **I'm clumsy, uncoordinated, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Enables you to avoid potential ridicule for lack of physical skill. The lack of skill comes from believing the label and avoiding physical activity, not from some inherent defect. "You get good at what you practice, not what you shun."
  5. **I'm unattractive, ugly, big-boned, plain, too tall, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Keeps you from taking risks with the opposite sex and justifies poor self-image and lack of love. Gives you a ready-made excuse for not putting yourself on the line in a love relationship. "There's only one problem: we see exactly what we choose to—even in mirrors."
  6. **I'm unorganized, meticulous, sloppy, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Convenient for manipulating others and justifying why things must be done a certain way. "I've always done it that way." By relying on tradition, you don't have to entertain the risky notion of doing it differently, and you can ensure everyone else does it your way. This "I'm" calls upon "policy" as a substitute for thinking.
  7. **I'm forgetful, careless, irresponsible, apathetic, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Especially useful to vindicate yourself for ineffective behavior. The "I'm" keeps you from ever working on your memory or carelessness, and you excuse yourself with "That's me." As long as you haul out this "I'm," you never have to work at changing.
  8. **I'm Italian, German, Jewish, Irish, Black, Chinese, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Works well when you run out of other reasons to explain behaviors that don't work for you but are too difficult to tackle. You trot out your ethnic "I'm" as a justification for stereotypical behavior. Example: A maître d' responds to being asked why he overreacts, "What do you expect from me? I'm Italian. I can't help it."
  9. **I'm bossy, pushy, authoritarian, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Allows you to continue hostile acts rather than work at developing self-discipline. You varnish the behavior with "I can't help it, I've always been that way."
  10. **I'm old, middle-aged, tired, etc.** — Neurotic dividend: Uses age as a reason for not participating in risky or threatening activities (sports, dating after divorce or death of a spouse, traveling, etc.). Eliminates all attendant risks of trying something new. The implication is that you are absolutely finished in that area, and since you will always get older, you are finished growing.

### Lesson 6: Understand the "I'm Circle" — The Vicious Cycle of Self-Labeling
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Understand the cycle: A thought or belief about yourself (point 1) → leads to a feeling (point 2) → leads to the behavior you expect of yourself (point 3) → which reinforces the original thought (point 4), completing the circle.
  2. Recognize that your "I'm" is what you call upon to exonerate yourself and explain why you persist in a self-defeating pattern — instead of intervening between points 3 and 4.
  3. Challenge any aspect of your life in which you've chosen to be a "finished product."
  4. Break the circle by choosing differently at point 3 — act in a way that contradicts the label, rather than confirming it.
  5. Reframe your sentence: Change "I'm shy" to "Up until now, I've behaved in a shy manner."

- **Examples from book:**
  - A young man who believes he is shy goes to a party, behaves as if he is shy, and his behavior further supports his self-image — a vicious circle. Instead of intervening, he exonerates his behavior with an "I'm" and avoids the risk-taking necessary to escape.
  - A student who believes he's poor in math tackles algebra homework, simply stops instead of putting in extra time or consulting a tutor. When asked why he flunked algebra, he says, "I've always been lousy in mathematics." The "I'm" exonerates him and explains why he persists in a self-defeating pattern.

### Lesson 7: Do Not Give Others Responsibility for Your Present-Day "I'm"
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize that blaming your parents, teachers, or other significant adults for your present-day "I'm" gives them a measure of control over your life today.
  2. Understand that blaming others elevates them above you and ingeniously creates an alibi for staying in your ineffective condition.
  3. Recognize that this provides a warranty against any risk-taking — if it's the "culture's fault," you can't do anything about it.
  4. Accept full responsibility: Every "I'm" you treasure could be relabeled, "I've chosen to be."

- **Examples from book:** "By giving them responsibility for your present-day I'm you've given them a measure of control over your life today, elevated them to a higher position than you and ingeniously created an alibi for staying in your ineffective condition. A neat little payoff indeed, and one that provides you with a warranty against any risk-taking. If it's the 'culture's' fault that you have this I'm, you can't do anything about it."

### Lesson 8: Eliminate the Word "I'm" and Substitute With Past-Tense Language
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Eliminate the word "I'm" wherever you can in reference to your self-defeating labels.
  2. Substitute "I'm" sentences with:
     - "Until today I've chosen to be that way."
     - "I used to label myself…"
  3. Understand that "There is no such thing as human nature" — it is a phrase designed to pigeonhole people and create excuses. You are the sum product of your choices.

- **Examples from book:** The book gives these exact substitutions: "Until today I've chose to be that way" and "I used to label myself…" (as replacements for self-defeating "I'm" statements).

### Lesson 9: Announce Your Commitment to Eliminating "I'm" Labels to Those Close to You
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Decide which "I'm" labels are most important to eliminate.
  2. Announce to those close to you that you are going to work at eliminating these specific "I'm" labels.
  3. Ask them to remind you whenever you haul out one of your old "I'm" labels.
  4. Let their reminders serve as an accountability mechanism.

---

### Lesson 10: Set Behavioral Goals to Act Differently Than You've Ever Done Before
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. For each "I'm" you want to eliminate, set a concrete behavioral goal that forces you to act in direct contradiction of the label.
  2. Make the goal small and specific for that moment.
  3. Carry it out regardless of how you feel.
  4. Example: If you consider yourself shy, introduce yourself to one person you might otherwise have avoided.

- **Examples from book:** "If you consider yourself shy, introduce yourself to one person who you might otherwise have avoided."

---

### Lesson 11: Enlist a Trusted Confidant to Signal You When You Fall Back Into Your "I'm"
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Identify a trusted confidant who will help you combat the powers of the past.
  2. Ask this person to signal you silently with a pre-arranged signal (e.g., a tug to his ear) each time he notices you falling back into your "I'm."
  3. Use the signal as a real-time reminder to correct yourself in that moment.

### Lesson 12: Keep a Journal on Your Self-Destructive "I'm" Behavior
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Keep a journal dedicated to tracking your self-destructive "I'm" behavior.
  2. For one week, record in a notebook the exact time, date, and occasion when you used any of the self-destructive "I'm" labels.
  3. Also record what action you took and how you were feeling about yourself while you were behaving that way.
  4. Use the list of ten typical "I'm" categories provided in this chapter as a guide for your journal-keeping.
  5. Work at diminishing the entries over time.

- **Examples from book:** "For one week record in a notebook the exact time, date and occasion when you used any of the self-destructive I'ms, and work at diminishing the entries. Use the list provided earlier in this chapter as a guide to your journal-keeping."

---

### Lesson 13: Correct the Four Neurotic Sentences Out Loud When You Catch Yourself Using Them
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Watch out for the four neurotic sentences in your own speech.
  2. Whenever you catch yourself using one, correct yourself out loud in the following ways:
     - Change **"That's me."** → **"That was me."**
     - Change **"I can't help it."** → **"I can change that if I work on it."**
     - Change **"I've always been that way."** → **"I'm going to be different."**
     - Change **"That's my nature."** → **"That's what I used to believe was my nature."**
  3. Say the corrected version aloud, in the moment, each time you catch yourself.

- **Examples from book:** The book provides the exact substitutions as listed above: "That's me."…to…"That was me." / "I can't help it."…to…"I can change that if I work on it." / "I've always been that way."…to…"I'm going to be different." / "That's my nature."…to…"That's what I used to believe was my nature."

### Lesson 14: Work on Eliminating One "I'm" Per Day
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Select one "I'm" label to eliminate for just that single day.
  2. Devote the entire day to working specifically at being aware of this tendency.
  3. Actively alter one or two behaviors associated with that "I'm" on that day.
  4. Repeat with a different "I'm" the following day, working on them one at a time until each is eliminated.

- **Examples from book:** "If you've used I'm forgetful as a self-descriptor, devote Monday to working specifically at being aware of this tendency, and see if you can alter one or two forgetful behaviors. Similarly, if you don't like your stubbornness I'm, give yourself one day to be tolerant of contrary opinions, and see if you can rid yourself of I'ms one day at a time."

### Lesson 15: Interrupt Your Own "I'm Circle" Between Point 3 and Point 4
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Visualize your own "I'm circle" — the cycle where a thought leads to a feeling, which leads to behavior, which reinforces the original thought.
  2. Learn to catch yourself between the feeling (point 2) and the behavior (point 3) — or more precisely, between the behavior impulse (point 3) and its reinforcing completion (point 4).
  3. At that moment of interruption, choose to toss out the ancient excuse and act in a way that breaks the pattern.
  4. Resolve at that moment to not let the "I'm" complete the cycle.

- **Examples from book:** "You can interrupt your own 'I'm-Circle' between point 3 and 4, and resolve to toss out those ancient excuses for avoidance."

---

### Lesson 16: Try Something Totally New That You Have Always Avoided
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Identify an activity you have never done before and that you have always avoided in the past due to an "I'm" label.
  2. Set aside an entire afternoon (minimum three hours) for full immersion in that new activity.
  3. Engage deeply in the activity for the full three hours.
  4. After your immersion, ask yourself: Can you still use the same "I'm" label that you applied that morning?
  5. Use the experience as evidence to challenge and potentially discard that label.

- **Examples from book:** "Find something you've never done and set aside an afternoon for that activity. After your three-hour immersion in a totally new activity, one that you've always avoided in the past, see if you can still use the same I'm that you applied that morning."

### Lesson 17: Reframe Your Self-Description Using Present-Moment, Choice-Based Language
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Go back to the opening questions: "Who are you?" and "How do you describe yourself?"
  2. Think about new labels that are in no way connected to choices others have made for you or those you have made in the past.
  3. Reframe every old "I'm" label using present-moment language:
     - Instead of "I'm [negative trait]," say "Up until now, I've behaved in a [negative] manner."
  4. Recognize that "You are what you choose today, not what you've chosen before."
  5. Treat yourself as a sum product of your choices, not of your past.

- **Examples from book:** "Go back to the opening questions in this chapter, Who are you? and How do you describe yourself? Think about some delicious new labels that are in no way connected to the choices that others have made for you, or those that you've made until now. Those old tiresome labels may be keeping you from living your life as fully as you might." The reframe example given: change "I'm shy" to "Up until now, I've behaved in a shy manner."

### Lesson 18: Adopt the "I'm an 'I'm' Exorcist" Identity
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. If you must keep an "I'm" label, choose this one: "I'm an 'I'm' exorcist — and I like it."
  2. Make the exorcising of erroneous "I'm" labels a conscious part of your identity.
  3. Treat every limiting "I'm" as a demon to be exorcised.
  4. Embrace the identity of someone who actively dismantles their own self-imposed limitations.

- **Examples from book:** "Any I'm that keeps you from growing is a demon to be exorcised. If you must have an I'm, try this one on for size. 'I'm an "I'm" exorcist—and I like it.'"

---

### Lesson 19: Understand That All "I'm" Labels Are Learned Avoidance Patterns — and Can Be Unlearned
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Accept the foundational truth: All of your "I'm" labels are learned avoidance patterns, not fixed traits.
  2. Remind yourself: "You can learn to be almost anything if you make the choice to do so."
  3. Let go of the idea that your current limitations are permanent or inherent.
  4. Make the active choice to learn something new, since "learning is the only thing which the mind can never exhaust, never alienate, never be tortured by, never fear or distrust, and never dream of regretting."
  5. Use learning itself as the antidote to sadness and stagnation.

- **Examples from book:** "All of your I'ms are learned avoidance patterns, and you can learn to be almost anything if you make the choice to do so." Quoting Merlin: "The best thing for being sad... is to learn something. That is the only thing that never fails."
