## Chapter V: The Useless Emotions—Guilt and Worry

### Lesson 1: Understanding That Guilt and Worry Are Useless
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize that guilt immobilizes you in the present over past behavior, and worry immobilizes you in the present over future events.
  2. Understand that both guilt and worry serve the identical purpose of keeping you upset or immobile in your present moment.
- **Examples from book:** Robert Jones Burdette's quote from Golden Day: "It isn't the experience of today that drives men mad. It is the remorse for something that happened yesterday, and the dread of what tomorrow may disclose." Also, the quote: "There are two days in the week about which and upon which I never worry. Two carefree days, kept sacredly free from fear and apprehension. One of these days is yesterday…and the other day I do not worry about is tomorrow."

### Lesson 2: Distinguishing Guilt From Learning From Your Past
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Identify whether you are simply learning from your past and vowing to avoid the repetition of some specific behavior (this is NOT guilt).
  2. Determine if you are prevented from taking action now as a result of having behaved in a certain way previously (this IS guilt).
- **Examples from book:** Learning from mistakes is healthy and necessary for growth. Guilt is unhealthy because you are ineffectively using up your energy in the present feeling hurt, upset, and depressed about a historical happening. No amount of guilt can ever undo anything.

### Lesson 3: Understanding the Two Basic Origins of Guilt
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Identify whether your guilt is "Leftover Guilt" — emotional reactions carried around from childhood memories.
  2. Identify whether your guilt is "Self-imposed Guilt" — guilt imposed on yourself for breaking an adult rule or moral code.
  3. Recognize that in either case, guilt is stupid and useless behavior.
- **Examples from book:** 
  - Leftover guilt examples: "Daddy won't like you if you do that again." "You should feel ashamed of yourself." "Oh, all right, I'm only your mother."
  - Self-imposed guilt examples: having told someone off and hating yourself for it, being emotionally drained because of shoplifting, not going to church, or having said the wrong thing in the past.

### Lesson 4: Rejecting Cultural Messages That Promote Guilt
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize cultural puritanical thinking that says, "If it's fun, you're supposed to feel guilty about it."
  2. Learn to savor pleasure without a sense of guilt.
  3. See yourself as someone capable of doing anything that fits your own value system and does not harm others — and doing it without guilt.
  4. If you do something and don't like it or yourself after having done it, vow to eliminate such behavior in the future rather than going through self-inflicted guilt.
- **Examples from book:** "You can learn to see yourself as someone who is capable of doing anything that fits into your own value system and does not harm others—and doing it without guilt."

### Lesson 5: Recognizing Parental Guilt Tactics
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Identify parental guilt manipulation techniques such as: "Never mind then, I'll do it—with my bad back. You just sit there and enjoy yourself."
  2. Recognize the "I sacrificed for you" mentality (e.g., "I went through eighteen hours of labor just to bring you into the world," "I stayed married to your father because of you").
  3. Notice when parents use phrases like "We'll stay here by ourselves. You just enjoy yourself the way you've always done. Don't worry about us."
  4. Be aware of the "You disgraced us" tactic or "What will the neighbors think?"
  5. Recognize parental illness guilt: "You've made my blood pressure go up," "You're killing me," "giving me a heart attack."
  6. Notice sexual guilt imposed by parents (e.g., "God forbid you should masturbate. It's bad," "You should be ashamed for reading such magazines").
- **Examples from book:** The example of Donny and his mother using her bad back to manipulate him into bringing up chairs. The example of the 52-year-old pediatrician who keeps his marriage a secret from his 85-year-old mother because he fears it might "kill her."

### Lesson 6: Recognizing Lover- and Spouse-Related Guilt Tactics
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Identify when someone uses the "If you loved me" guilt tactic to punish a partner for particular behavior.
  2. Recognize that grudges, long silences, and hurtful looks are methods of engendering guilt (e.g., "I'm not talking to you, that'll fix you").
  3. Notice when past incidents are recalled to maintain guilt (e.g., "But don't forget what you did in 1951").
  4. Identify guilt used to make a love partner conform (e.g., "If you were responsible, you would have called me").
- **Examples from book:** "How do you expect me to be loving after what you've done?" "How could I ever trust you again, when you let me down before?"

### Lesson 7: Recognizing Children-Inspired Guilt Tactics
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize when children use guilt to manipulate parents (e.g., "Sally's father lets her do it," "You don't love me. If you did you wouldn't treat me this way," "I must be adopted. My real parents wouldn't treat me like this").
  2. Understand that children learn this behavior by watching adults use it.
  3. Remember: "Guilt is not a natural behavior. It is a learned emotional response that can only be used if the victim teaches the exploiter that he is vulnerable."
- **Examples from book:** A tantrum in the supermarket may produce the desired candy because the child knows the parent cannot cope with being a "bad parent."

### Lesson 8: Recognizing School-Inspired Guilt Tactics
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Identify guilt messages from teachers/authority figures (e.g., "Your mother is really going to be disappointed in you," "You should be ashamed of yourself for getting a C—a smart boy like you," "How could you hurt your parents like that, after all they've done for you?").
  2. Remember that even as an adult, you are a product of those schools and may still be affected by these messages.
- **Examples from book:** "You failed the test because you didn't study, now you'll have to live with yourself."

### Lesson 9: Recognizing Church-Related Guilt Tactics
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Identify guilt messages tied to religious doctrine (e.g., "If you loved God, you wouldn't behave that way," "You won't get into heaven unless you repent for your sins," "You should feel bad because you haven't gone to church every week").
  2. Recognize that these messages use the threat of divine punishment to create present-moment guilt.
- **Examples from book:** "You've disobeyed one of God's rules and you should feel ashamed of yourself."

### Lesson 10: Recognizing Other Institutional Guilt Producers
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize that prisons operate on the guilt theory — if a person sits long enough thinking how bad he's been, he will be better for the guilt.
  2. Notice how tipping has become a practice reflecting the guilt of the person served rather than superior service.
  3. Be aware that littering, smoking, and other behaviors can be used to make you feel guilty, and that a stern look by a stranger can send you into paroxysms of guilt.
  4. Recognize dieting as an area loaded with guilt — if you give in to counterproductive behavior, learn from it rather than feeling guilty.
- **Examples from book:** The example of Harold who worried for months about being laid off, then got a better job within three days. The example of effective waiters and waitresses using blatant hand extending and nasty comments to produce guilt and get bigger tips.

### Lesson 11: Recognizing the Psychological Payoffs for Choosing Guilt
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Understand that guilt is an avoidance technique for working on yourself in the present — it shifts responsibility for what you are or are not now to what you were or were not in the past.
  2. Recognize that guilt helps you avoid the hard work of changing yourself now and the attendant risks that go with change.
  3. Notice the tendency to believe that if you feel guilty enough, you will eventually be exonerated for having been naughty.
  4. See that guilt can be a means of returning to the safety of childhood, where others made decisions for you.
  5. Recognize that guilt is a useful method for transferring responsibility for your behavior from yourself to others.
  6. Understand that guilt can help you win the approval of others even when they don't approve of your behavior.
  7. Recognize that guilt is a superb way to win pity from others, indicating low self-esteem.
- **Examples from book:** The prison mentality where inmates pay for sins by feeling terrible for a long period of time. The greater the transgression, the longer the period of remorse necessary for pardon.

### Lesson 12: Using the Strategy: View the Past as Unchangeable
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Begin to view the past as something that can never be changed, despite how you feel about it.
  2. Emblazon this sentence on your consciousness: "My feeling guilty will not change the past, nor will it make me a better person."
  3. Use this thinking to help differentiate guilt from learning as a result of your past.
- **Examples from book:** The client who had an extramarital affair and felt guilty, but continued the affair. The point was made that his guilt was easier than examining his marriage closely.

### Lesson 13: Using the Strategy: Ask What You Are Avoiding
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Ask yourself what you are avoiding in the present with guilt about the past.
  2. Go to work on that particular thing, which will eliminate the need for guilt.
- **Examples from book:** The extramarital affair client who had two choices: recognize that guilt was easier than examining his marriage, or accept his behavior and live up to his own value system.

### Lesson 14: Using the Strategy: Accept Your Choices Despite Others' Disapproval
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Begin to accept certain things about yourself that you've chosen but which others may dislike.
  2. See that it is natural for parents, boss, neighbors, or spouse to take a stand against some of your behavior.
  3. Remember that it is necessary that you approve of yourself; the approval of others is pleasant but beside the point.
  4. Once you no longer need approval, the guilt for behavior which does not bring approval will disappear.
- **Examples from book:** Referenced from earlier in the book about approval seeking.

### Lesson 15: Using the Strategy: Keep a Guilt Journal
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Keep a Guilt Journal and write down any guilty moments.
  2. Note precisely when the guilt occurs, why it occurs, and with whom it occurs.
  3. Document what you are avoiding in the present with this agonizing over the past.
  4. Use the journal to gain helpful insights into your particular guilt zone.
- **Examples from book:** The journal should provide insights into patterns of guilt triggers.

### Lesson 16: Using the Strategy: Reconsider Your Value System
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Reconsider your value system — determine which values you actually believe in and which you only pretend to accept.
  2. List all of your "phony values."
  3. Resolve to live up to a code of ethics that is self-determined, not one imposed by others.
- **Examples from book:** "List all of these phony values and resolve to live up to a code of ethics that is self-determined, not one that has been imposed by others."

### Lesson 17: Using the Strategy: Make a Guilt Points List
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Make a list of all the bad things you've ever done.
  2. Give yourself guilt points for each of them on a scale of one to ten.
  3. Add up your score.
  4. Observe that it makes no difference in the present whether the score is one hundred or one million — the present-moment is still the same, and all of your guilt is merely wasteful activity.
- **Examples from book:** The exercise is designed to show that accumulated guilt points have no real impact on the present.

### Lesson 18: Using the Strategy: Assess Real Consequences Rather Than Mystical Feelings
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Assess the real consequences of your behavior.
  2. Rather than looking for a mystical feeling to determine yes's and no's in your life, determine whether the results of your actions are pleasing and productive for you.
- **Examples from book:** The contrast between using guilt as a mystical guide versus evaluating actual outcomes.

### Lesson 19: Using the Strategy: Teach Others They Cannot Manipulate You With Guilt
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Teach those in your life who attempt to manipulate you with guilt that you are perfectly capable of handling their disappointment in you.
  2. Learn new responses that do not accept guilt (e.g., "Okay, Mom, if you want to risk your back on a few chairs because you can't wait a few minutes, I guess there is little I can do to dissuade you").
  3. Understand that over time, their behavior will begin to change once they see they cannot force you to choose guilt.
  4. Once you defuse the guilt, the emotional control over you and the possibility of manipulation are eliminated forever.
- **Examples from book:** The example of the 52-year-old pediatrician who continues to live a bachelor fantasy because of guilt about his mother.

### Lesson 20: Using the Strategy: Do Something That Will Result in Guilt
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Do something which you know is bound to result in feelings of guilt.
  2. As you check into a hotel and a bellboy is assigned to show you to a room you can find alone, announce that you'll do it alone.
  3. If rejected, tell your unwanted companion that he is wasting his time and energy since you will not be leaving a tip for a service you don't want.
  4. Take a week to be alone if you've always wanted to do so, despite guilt-engendering protestations from other family members.
  5. Use these behaviors to tackle the omnipresent guilt that so many sectors of the environment are adept at helping you choose.
- **Examples from book:** The bellboy example and the example of taking a week alone despite family protests.

### Lesson 21: Using the Strategy: Role-Working Exercise to Defuse Parental Guilt
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Engage in role-playing exercises to practice outmaneuvering guilt-producing statements.
  2. When responding to guilt-producing statements, begin responses with references to the other person as responsible for their own feelings (e.g., say "You feel" rather than "I feel") to tactfully minimize the potential for guilt.
  3. Practice refusing to yield to obvious guilt producers.
  4. Learn new responses that do not accept the guilt (e.g., "Is there anything you'd like to say to me before you leave?" in response to "I'm having a tachycardia attack right now. I think I'm going to die. You're killing me").
- **Examples from book:** The dialogue between daughter and mother where the daughter refuses to yield to guilt producers. The daughter's responses begin with "You feel" rather than "I feel," minimizing the potential for guilt.

### Lesson 22: Understanding Worry — Definition and Distinction From Planning
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Understand that worry is defined as being immobilized in the present as a result of things that are going or not going to happen in the future.
  2. Do NOT confuse worrying with planning for the future — if the present-moment activity contributes to a more effective future, this is NOT worry.
  3. Recognize that worry is only present when you are immobilized now about a future happening.
- **Examples from book:** "If you are planning, and the present-moment activity will contribute to a more effective future, then this is not worry. It is worry only when you are in any way immobilized now about a future happening."

### Lesson 23: Recognizing That Worry Does Not Equal Caring
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize the cultural equating of worrying with caring ("Of course I'm worried, it's only natural when you care about someone").
  2. Understand that worry has nothing to do with love, which predicates a relationship in which each person has the right to be what he chooses without any necessary conditions imposed by the other.
  3. Stop proving your love by worrying at the appropriate time.
- **Examples from book:** The Civil War example: "In 1975, some 115 years later, all of those worriers are dead and all their combined worrying did not change a moment of what is now history."

### Lesson 24: Using the Strategy: Ask What You Are Avoiding With Worry
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. When you catch yourself worrying, ask yourself: "What am I avoiding now by using up this moment with worry?"
  2. Begin to attack whatever it is you're avoiding.
  3. Remember that the best antidote to worry is action.
- **Examples from book:** The client who spent three hours playing tennis, touch football, and Ping-Pong while another man continued to worry about the stock market. Neither man's behavior had any effect on the stock market.

### Lesson 25: Using the Strategy: Recognize the Preposterousness of Worry
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Ask yourself over and over: "Is there anything that will ever change as a result of my worrying about it?"
  2. Repeatedly confront yourself with this question to see the absurdity of worry.
- **Examples from book:** The rhetorical question designed to make worriers realize their worry accomplishes nothing.

### Lesson 26: Using the Strategy: Designate "Worry-Time"
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Designate ten minutes in the morning and afternoon as your worry segments.
  2. Use these periods to fret about every potential disaster you can fit into the time slot.
  3. Using your ability to control your own thoughts, postpone any further worry until your next designated "worry-time."
  4. You will soon see the folly of using any time in this wasteful fashion and will eventually eliminate your worry zone completely.
- **Examples from book:** The structured worry-time exercise to demonstrate the futility of worry.

### Lesson 27: Using the Strategy: Make a Worry List
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Make a worry list of everything you worried about yesterday, last week, and even last year.
  2. See if any of your worry did anything productive for you.
  3. Assess how many of the things you worried about ever materialized at all.
  4. Recognize that worry is doubly wasteful: it does nothing to alter the future, and the projected catastrophe often turns out to be minor or even a blessing when it arrives.
- **Examples from book:** The complete Worry Sheet listing 27 common worries (children, health, dying, job, economy, heart attack, security, spouse's happiness, doing the right thing, healthy child, prices, accidents, what others think, weight, money, car breaking down, bills, parents dying, getting into heaven, weather, getting old, flying, daughter's virginity, talking in front of groups, spouse not calling, going into the city, having nothing to worry about).

### Lesson 28: Using the Strategy: "Just Worry!" Demonstration
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. When tempted to worry, stop and turn to someone and say, "Watch me—I'm about to worry."
  2. You will likely be confounded since you probably won't even know how to demonstrate the thing you do so well, so often.
- **Examples from book:** The exercise designed to make you realize that worry is not a tangible, demonstrable behavior.

### Lesson 29: Using the Strategy: The Worry-Eradicating Question
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Ask yourself: "What's the worst thing that could happen to me (or them) and what is the likelihood of it occurring?"
  2. You will discover the absurdity of worry by honestly evaluating both the severity and probability of your feared outcome.
- **Examples from book:** The question is designed to reveal that most worries are either improbable or manageable.

### Lesson 30: Using the Strategy: Act in Direct Conflict With Your Worry Areas
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Deliberately choose to act in a manner that is directly in conflict with your usual areas of worry.
  2. If you compulsively save for the future, always worried about having enough money, begin to use your money today.
  3. Adopt the mindset: "Being of sound mind, I spent all my money while I was alive."
- **Examples from book:** The rich uncle's will: "Being of sound mind, I spent all my money while I was alive."

### Lesson 31: Using the Strategy: Face Your Fears With Productive Behavior
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Begin to face the fears you possess with productive thought and behavior.
  2. Identify a specific fear or worry (e.g., fear of wild dogs).
  3. Prepare yourself with a sense of confidence (e.g., carry a rock for "insurance").
  4. Refuse to show fear or back down when confronted with the feared situation.
  5. The effective challenge to a fear or worry is the most productive way to eradicate it from your life.
- **Examples from book:** The woman who carried a rock to fight her fear of wild dogs on an island off the coast of Connecticut. She refused to slow down when the dogs growled and came running toward her. As the dogs charged forward and encountered someone who refused to back down, they gave up and ran away.

### Lesson 32: The Core Principle — Live in the Present Moment
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Learn to live now and not waste your current moments in immobilizing thoughts about the past or future.
  2. Recognize that there is no other moment to live but now, and all futile guilt and worry are done in the elusive now.
  3. Apply this understanding to both guilt (past) and worry (future) as they both steal the present moment.
- **Examples from book:** Lewis Carroll's Alice Through the Looking Glass: "The rule is, jam tomorrow, and jam yesterday…but never jam today." "It must come sometimes to 'jam-today,' " Alice objected. The challenge: "Any jam today? Since it must come sometime, how about now?"
