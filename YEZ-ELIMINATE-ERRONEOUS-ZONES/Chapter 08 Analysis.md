# Chapter VIII: The Fairness Trap

## Overview

This chapter examines the insistence that life be fair, that others behave equitably, and that outcomes be evenly distributed. The author presents this demand — limited in its applicability to readers who find themselves caught in the Fairness Trap thinking pattern, defined as readers who insist on perfect fairness in life and human interactions — as an erroneous thinking pattern that keeps those people caught in that pattern stuck in resentment, jealousy, and constant comparison. He argues that the idea of perfect fairness is a concept not found in nature or in human systems, and that causing yourself ongoing negative feelings when you encounter unfair treatment is as self-damaging — defined here by the measurable persistence of resentment, jealousy, and constant comparison that erodes well-being — as guilt or approval-seeking. Through a series of lessons, the chapter shows how jealousy, tally-sheet relationships, and the "It's not fair!" refrain harm personal well-being, then provides concrete strategies for letting go of the demand for fairness, reducing jealousy, and living by your own chosen standards rather than external ideas of fairness.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you find yourself stewing in resentment because someone else got something you believe you deserved
- When you keep a mental or literal tally sheet in a relationship, measuring give-and-take and resenting perceived imbalances
- When jealousy, bitterness, or the "It's not fair!" refrain is preventing you from taking productive action

## Steps

### Step 1: Understand That Perfect Fairness Is Not Found in Nature or in Human Systems

Look at nature to confirm there is no system of fairness in the world: robins eat worms, spiders eat flies, cougars kill coyotes, and similar predator-prey relationships. Recognize that tornadoes, floods, tidal waves, and droughts cause harm without regard for who they affect. Per the book's framework, accept that perfect fairness has limited applicability — defined here as not being a reliable guide for personal decision-making — to your own choices about personal fulfillment and well-being. Accept that the world and the people in it, meaning all people outside the scope of the book's framework, will continue to act in ways you may consider unfair, and per the book's framework this will continue for the foreseeable future; you can choose how to respond, but your well-being does not depend on the presence or absence of fairness around you. The author describes the food chain (cougars killing coyotes, coyotes killing badgers, badgers killing mice, mice killing bugs) and natural disasters as evidence that perfect fairness is not a feature of the natural world. He also cites political and legal realities: Spiro Agnew evading taxes and becoming rich, Richard Nixon being exonerated while his subordinates serve short sentences in minimum-security prison, and the poor and minority-group members filling jails while the influential operate under separate rules.

### Step 2: Do Not Cause Yourself Lasting Negative Emotions When You Encounter Unfairness

Understand that the harmful pattern is not the desire for fairness itself, but the sense of being stuck that may result from unfair circumstances, defined here as situations where treatment diverges from what you believe you deserve. Decide that you will work to address unfairness where you can, defined here as wherever you have genuine personal influence or agency, but refuse to be psychologically defeated or emotionally immobilized by it. Work at helping to reduce unfairness while deciding that you will not experience ongoing negative emotions about the absence of perfect fairness. Choose to address unfairness and refuse to be pulled into lasting negative emotions about it. The author asserts — within the book's framework and drawing on the exact historical record from the 3rd millennium BCE through the 20th century CE — that poverty, war, pestilence, crime, prostitution, drug abuse, and murder have persisted across that entire span of recorded human history and, per the book's framework on that basis, will continue. He argues that choosing to continue experiencing ongoing negative emotions about this is as self-damaging as guilt, approval-seeking, or any other pattern of self-criticism.

### Step 3: Eliminate the "It's Not Fair!" Slogan From Your Relationships

Stop comparing yourself to other individuals or groups, defined here as any person or group used as a basis for an unfair comparison. Stop using sentences like "If that person can do it, I can do it too," "It's not right for you to have more than I," and "But I didn't get to do that, why should you?" Stop deciding what is good for you on the basis of someone else's choices. Stop letting other people set your emotional state. Stop running your life on the basis of someone else and begin creating your own life. Discard the habit of focusing on what others are doing; eliminate comparisons to others. I never say things like that to you." When he mentioned their children, she said: "That isn't fair. I never bring the children into an argument." The author notes that, within the Fairness Trap framework, the majority of emotional distress — defined here as more than 50% of emotional distress attributable specifically to the demand for fairness, rather than all emotional distress overall — stems from making other people's behavior more important to your emotional state than your own.

### Step 4: Reduce Jealousy by Recognizing It as a Demand for Unfair Treatment From Others

Set as a goal the reduction of jealousy as a harmful thinking pattern if it creates any measurable degree of emotional stuckness — defined here as any persistent fixation on another person's behavior that produces at least two of the following: resentment, self-critical rumination, or an inability to act in your own best interest, assuming here that you can identify your own best interest. Decide that another person's choices (such as a partner loving another) were made independently of you and may have many reasons of their own, defined here as reasons unrelated to you and within that person's own life context, all unrelated to you. Recognize that someone else's behavior has nothing to do with your sense of personal adequacy. Stop bruising yourself with self-critical jealousy in which you are less important — defined here as your sense of personal adequacy is of lesser priority to you — than your partner or their other partner. ### Step 5: Recognize Common "Demand for Fairness" Behaviors in Yourself

The book identifies the following behaviors to watch for and reduce:

1. Complaining that others make more money for performing substantially equivalent work — defined here as the same role carrying the same core responsibilities, at the same level of experience and effort.

3. Resenting that others get away with comparable infractions while you personally are caught and penalized on a consistent, repeatable basis — defined here as similar infractions treated differently across multiple comparable instances.
4. Using "Would I treat you that way?" sentences, assuming everyone — defined here as all other people universally — should be exactly like you.
5. Always — defined here as in every instance without exception — returning favors immediately to keep exchanges feeling equal.
6. Kissing someone back or saying "I love you, too" instead of accepting it — where "it" refers to the expression of affection or love — and expressing your own feelings whenever you choose.

8. Always — defined here as consistently in every situation without variation — insisting on things being consistent (avoiding the trap of requiring others to match your behavior exactly).
9. In arguments, insisting on a clear-cut decision with winners considered correct and losers considered incorrect.
10. Using the fairness argument to get your own way and then resenting the lack of fairness.
11. Saying it isn't fair to kids, parents, or neighbors and consequently doing things you'd rather not do, feeling resentment.
12. Playing the "If that person can do it, so can I" game to justify behavior outside your value system.
13. Cutting off another driver on the highway, hurrying to get in front of a slow driver to slow them down, or leaving highbeams on because other drivers are doing it.
14. Spending the same amount of money on a gift that someone else spent on you — where "you" refers to the reader practicing the Fairness Trap framework.

16. Keeping a tally sheet equal rather than doing what you — meaning the reader caught in the Fairness Trap framework — would like.

The author lists all of the above behaviors as manifestations of the demand for fairness, noting they all stem from the assumption in your thinking that things must be fair.

### Step 6: Recognize the Emotional Benefits That Keep You Stuck in the Demand for Fairness

Release the benefit of feeling superior by keeping your tally sheet in balance; give up the feeling of being more upright than others. Release the benefit of avoiding responsibility for yourself and justifying inaction by blaming unfair people and events — where "unfair people and events" means any person or occurrence you perceive as unjust; "action" means any chosen response or behavior taken independently — and instead take action. Release the benefit of gaining attention, pity, and self-pity by positioning yourself as a victim of unfairness — where "unfairness" means the perceived gap between expected and actual treatment; stop using these to support your sense of self. Release the benefit of justifying behavior by making your actions someone else's responsibility ("If that person can do it, so can I"). Release the benefit of giving yourself an excuse for being ineffective ("If they aren't going to do anything, then neither am I"). Release the benefit of having a topic for conversation — defined here as a subject you can discuss to avoid talking honestly about yourself with others — that helps you avoid talking honestly about yourself with others. Release the benefit of always making "the just decision" — defined here as any choice framed as fair or moral — based on an external idea of fairness. Release the benefit of manipulating others (especially children) by telling them they are being unfair if they are not exactly like you or do not keep a precise tally — defined here as an exact count of favors given versus received — of give-and-take. Release the benefit of justifying aggressive behavior and retaliation because everything must be fair and equal. The author explains that, per the book's framework, as long as unfairness is the cause of your problems, you may believe you can never change until the unfairness goes away — which, based on the full documented record of human civilization from the 3rd millennium BCE through the 20th century CE, is unlikely to happen. He also notes that complaining about unfairness cuts off moments — defined here as specific opportunities for honest personal engagement with people around you — and helps you avoid dealing more honestly and personally with people around you.

### Step 7: Use Practical Strategies to Let Go of the Pointless Insistence on Fairness

Make a list of everything in your world — defined here as all circumstances, people, events, and situations you perceive as unfair — that you think is unfair. Use your list as a guide for effective personal action. Ask yourself: "Will the unfairness — defined here as perceived unequal treatment — go away if I am experiencing ongoing negative emotions?" Address the thinking pattern — defined here as the habitual belief that life must be fair — that brings about your distress, and you — meaning the reader caught in the Fairness Trap framework — will be on your way to freeing yourself from the insistence on perfect fairness, where perfect fairness means complete equality in treatment and outcomes. When you — meaning the reader caught in the Fairness Trap framework — find yourself saying "Would I do that to you?" or any similar sentence, change it to: "You are different from me, although I find that hard to accept right now." This will open, rather than close, communication — defined here as honest interaction without fairness framing — between you and the other person. Begin to view your emotional life as independent of whatever anyone else does, meaning all behaviors and choices of other people. This will free you from the habit — defined here as the tendency to compare yourself to others — of being hurt when others behave differently from the way you want them to. Try to view each decision with reasonable proportion — defined here as judging its significance against the full span of your life from birth to death rather than treating it in isolation — rather than as an all-important life-changing event, where all-important means treating it as definitively determining your overall life trajectory — defined here as the complete direction and outcome of your life from birth to death. Follow the principle from Carlos Castaneda, as presented in the book's framework — the book's framework being the model presented in the book for responding to perceived unfairness — of a person with practical knowledge — defined here as actionable guidance for living well — who lives by acting authentically on your own chosen standards rather than conforming to others' behavior — who knows that no single event is more important than any other in the grand scheme, where the grand scheme means the total span of your life from beginning to end, chooses any act, and acts it out as if it mattered in the moment, yet knows it — where "it" refers to a single event — does not define the complete arc of your life — where "It" refers to the complete arc of your life from birth to death. Replace the sentence "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate," or "I'd prefer…" This begins to accept reality — defined here as the world as it actually operates, without inherent fairness — without necessarily approving of it. Eliminate comparisons to others — where comparisons means judgments of how you measure up to others. Have your own goals — defined here as personal objectives independent of others — independent of what any other person does. Set out to be what you want — where "what you want" means your own desires separate from external expectations — without references to what others have or don't have. Correct yourself out loud when you use a sentence such as "I always call you when I'm going to be late, so why didn't you call me?" to "I would have felt better if you had called me." This eliminates the assumption that another person's reason for calling is to become more like you. Rather than repaying someone by bringing wine or a gift in exchange for dinner or a party, wait until you feel like it and then send it with a note saying, "Just because I think you're a great person." Eliminate equal-value exchanges; do something kind because you choose to, not because a special occasion calls for it. Spend the amount of money you want to on any gift, and don't be swayed by what was spent for you. Eliminate invitations based on obligation and fairness. Decide whom you'll see on an internal rather than external basis. Set up your own standards of conduct in your family based upon what you think is appropriate for you; have everyone else do the same. See if it is possible to make it happen without infringing on each other's rights. If you feel that being out three nights a week is what you want, but can't because someone has to watch the children, "fairness" does not have to enter into your decision-making. Perhaps make a babysitting arrangement, or have the children go along, or whatever will be a mutually satisfying arrangement. Bringing in the "It isn't fair" routine will lead to everyone's resentment — and to staying at home as well. Be someone who takes action, not someone who only complains about unfairness. For every unfair situation you experience, there is a resolution that does not require you to be immobilized in any way. Remember that retaliation is just another way of being controlled by others. Do what you, not they, decide is for you.

## Security & Safety Notes

This chapter addresses emotional and psychological patterns — specifically the demand for fairness, jealousy, and resentment — rather than digital or financial security. The strategies described are intended to free you from emotional stuckness caused by perceived unfairness — defined here as the subjective experience of unequal treatment, not necessarily objective fact. The chapter's principle of acting on your own chosen standards rather than external rules is a call to personal authenticity, not an endorsement of breaking laws or harming others. Note: replacing "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate" assumes this reframing reduces negative emotions; this assumption is acknowledged but not empirically proven.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You keep a mental tally sheet in your relationships, tracking who did what for whom and resenting any perceived imbalance.
  **Solution:** Discard the habit of focusing on what others are doing. Ask yourself whether your tally-keeping is making you happier or more miserable. Choose to give and act based on what you genuinely want, not on what you believe you are owed.

- **Problem:** You are stuck by jealousy because you believe someone else's behavior is unfair toward you, and you define your sense of personal adequacy by how you compare to them.
  **Solution:** Recognize that another person's choices were made independently of you and have nothing to do with your sense of personal adequacy. View their behavior as something between other people rather than as something against you. Decide that you will not allow yourself to be distraught when someone else does not behave the way you want them to.

- **Problem:** You use "It isn't fair" constantly — at home, at work, in traffic — and this refrain has become a constant filter through which you experience life, breeding constant resentment.
  **Solution:** Replace "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate," or "I'd prefer…" This begins to accept reality without necessarily approving of it. When you catch yourself in a fairness comparison, ask: "Will ongoing negative emotions change this?" If not, shift to what you can do about it rather than what you believe you are owed.

- **Problem:** You justify behavior by pointing to others who do the same thing ("If that person can do it, so can I"), or you refuse to act effectively because you believe the world is too unfair for your effort to matter.
  **Solution:** Within the book's framework, recognize that other people's behavior is not a justification for yours, nor is it an excuse for your own ineffectiveness. Make a list of what you think is unfair and use it as a guide for effective personal action — not as a list of grievances that justify doing nothing. Decide that any unhappiness you experience will be the result of your own behavior, not someone else's.
