# Chapter VIII: The Justice Trap

## Overview

This chapter exposes the demand for justice — the insistence that life be fair, that others behave fairly, and that outcomes be evenly distributed — as a self-defeating erroneous zone that immobilizes people in resentment, jealousy, and endless comparison. The author argues that justice is a mythological concept that does not exist in nature or in human society, and that punishing yourself emotionally when you encounter injustice is as neurotic as guilt or approval-seeking. Through a series of lessons, the chapter shows how jealousy, tally-sheet relationships, and the "It's not fair!" slogan destroy personal happiness, then provides concrete strategies for releasing the demand for justice, eliminating jealousy, and living according to your own internal standards rather than external notions of fairness.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you find yourself stewing in resentment because someone else got something you believe you deserved
- When you keep a mental or literal tally sheet in a relationship, measuring give-and-take and resenting perceived imbalances
- When jealousy, bitterness, or the "It's not fair!" refrain is preventing you from being happy or taking productive action

## Steps

### Step 1: Understand That Justice Is a Mythological Concept That Does Not Exist in Reality

Look at nature to confirm there is no justice in the world: robins eat worms, spiders eat flies, cougars kill coyotes, and so on. Recognize that tornadoes, floods, tidal waves, and droughts are all unfair. Accept that justice has almost no applicability, particularly as it pertains to your own choices about fulfillment and happiness. Accept that the world and the people in it go on being unfair every day; you can choose to be happy or unhappy, but your happiness has nothing to do with the lack of justice you see around you. The author describes the food chain (cougars killing coyotes, coyotes killing badgers, badgers killing mice, mice killing bugs) and natural disasters as evidence that fairness is a mythological concept. He also cites political and legal realities: Spiro Agnew evading taxes and becoming rich, Richard Nixon being exonerated while his subordinates serve short sentences in minimum-security prison, and the poor and minority-group members filling jails while the influential operate under separate rules.

### Step 2: Do Not Punish Yourself With Negative Emotions When You Fail to See Evidence of the Justice You Demand

Understand that the self-defeating behavior is not the demand for justice itself, but the immobilization that may result from the no-justice reality. Decide that you will fight injustice where possible, but refuse to be psychologically defeated or emotionally immobilized by it. Work at helping to eradicate injustice while deciding that you will not be upset by the absence of perfect justice. Choose to fight injustice and refuse to be seduced into being emotionally immobilized over it. The author notes that poverty, war, pestilence, crime, prostitution, drug abuse, and murder have persisted century after century and, based on human history, will continue. He argues that your choosing to be upset by this is as neurotic as guilt, approval-seeking, or any other self-flagellating behavior.

### Step 3: Eliminate the "It's Not Fair!" Slogan From Your Relationships

Stop comparing yourself to other individuals or groups. Stop using sentences like "If they can do it, so can I," "It's not fair for you to have more than I," and "But I didn't get to do that, why should you?" Stop determining what is good for you on the basis of someone else's conduct. Stop letting other people be in charge of your emotions. Stop running your life on the basis of someone else and begin creating your own life. Throw away the binoculars that focus on what others are doing; eliminate other-references. The author presents Judy, a client in an unhappy five-year marriage, who ran her marriage on a tally sheet. When her husband said something unpleasant, she countered: "Why did you say that? I never say things like that to you." When he mentioned their children, she said: "That isn't fair. I never bring the children into an argument." When discussing an evening's entertainment, she said: "That isn't fair. You get out all the time while I have to stay home with the children." Judy was more concerned with righting imaginary injustices than examining and improving her marriage. The author notes that the backdrop for virtually all neurosis is making others' behavior more significant than your own.

### Step 4: Eliminate Jealousy by Recognizing It as a Demand for Justice

Set as a goal the elimination of jealousy as wasteful thinking if it creates any amount of emotional immobility. Decide that another person's choices (such as a partner loving another) were made independent of you and may have a thousand reasons of their own, all unrelated to you. View someone else's affair or affectionate behavior as something between other people rather than as something against you. Recognize that someone else's behavior has nothing to do with your self-worth. Stop bruising yourself with self-flagellating jealousy in which you are less important than your partner or their other partner. Decide that you will not allow yourself to be distraught when someone else doesn't play fair. The author presents Helen, a client furious because her husband had an affair. Helen became obsessed with questions like "Where did I go wrong?" "What's wrong with me?" and "Wasn't I good enough for him?" She even contemplated having an affair herself to right the balance. The author explains that Helen's emotional condition will not improve until she decides that her husband's decision was independent of her and that it has nothing to do with her self-worth. John Dryden called jealousy "the jaundice of the soul."

### Step 5: Recognize Common "Demand for Justice" Behaviors in Yourself

The book identifies the following behaviors to watch for and eliminate:

1. Complaining that others make more money for doing the same work.
2. Being upset that celebrities (Frank Sinatra, Sammy Davis, Barbra Streisand, Catfish Hunter, Joe Namath) make large salaries.
3. Being upset that others get away with infractions while you always get caught.
4. Using "Would I treat you that way?" sentences, assuming everyone should be exactly like you.
5. Always reciprocating when someone does a favor for you (keeping the scales of justice in balance).
6. Kissing someone back or saying "I love you, too" instead of accepting it and expressing your own feelings whenever you choose.
7. Having sex with someone out of obligation because it isn't fair not to be cooperative.
8. Always insisting on things being consistent (avoiding the "foolish consistency" trap).
9. In arguments, insisting on a clear-cut decision with winners right and losers wrong.
10. Using the fair argument to get your own way and then being upset about the lack of fairness.
11. Saying it isn't fair to kids, parents, or neighbors and consequently doing things you'd rather not do, feeling resentment.
12. Playing the "If he can do it, so can I" game to justify cheating, stealing, flirting, lying, being late, or any behavior outside your value system.
13. Cutting off another driver on the highway, hurrying to get in front of a slow driver to slow them down, or leaving highbeams on because others are doing it.
14. Spending the same amount of money on a gift that someone else spent on you.
15. Paying back every favor with a favor of equal value.
16. Keeping a tally sheet equal rather than doing what you would like.

The author lists all of the above behaviors as common manifestations of the demand for justice, noting they all stem from the unserviceable sentence in your head that things must be fair.

### Step 6: Recognize the Psychological Payoffs That Keep You Trapped in the Demand for Justice

Release the payoff of feeling smug and superior by keeping your tally sheet in balance; give up the holier-than-thou feeling. Release the payoff of giving up responsibility for yourself and justifying being immobilized by shifting responsibility to unfair people and events; stop scapegoating and take risks instead. Release the payoff of winning attention, pity, and self-pity by positioning yourself as a victim of injustice; stop using these to prop yourself up. Release the payoff of justifying immoral, illegal, and inappropriate behavior by making your action someone else's responsibility ("If he can do it, so can I"). Release the payoff of giving yourself a sensational self-excuse for being ineffective ("If they aren't going to do anything, then neither am I"). Release the payoff of having a conversation topic that helps you avoid talking honestly about yourself with others. Release the payoff of always making "the just decision" based on an external concept of fairness. Release the payoff of manipulating others (especially children) by reminding them they are being unfair if they are not exactly like you or do not keep a precise tally of give-and-take. Release the payoff of justifying vindictive behavior and vengeance because everything must be fair and even. The author explains that as long as injustice is the cause of your problems, you can never change until the injustice goes away — which, of course, it never will. He also notes that complaining about injustice kills moments and helps you avoid dealing more honestly and personally with people around you.

### Step 7: Use Concrete Strategies to Give Up the Futile Insistence on Justice

Make a list of everything in your world that you think is unfair. Use your list as a guide for effective personal action. Ask yourself: "Will the inequities go away if I am upset?" Attack the erroneous thinking that brings about your upset, and you will be on your way to escape from the justice trap. When you find yourself saying "Would I do that to you?" or any similar sentence, change it to: "You are different from me, although I find that hard to accept right now." This will open, rather than close, communication between you and the other person. Begin to view your emotional life as independent of whatever anyone else does. This will free you from the chains of being hurt when others behave differently from the way you want them to. Try to view each decision not as a monumental life-changing event, but in perspective. Follow the principle from Carlos Castaneda of a man of knowledge who lives by acting, not by thinking about acting — who knows that nothing is more important than anything else, chooses any act, and acts it out as if it mattered, yet knows it doesn't. Replace the sentence "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate," or "I'd prefer…" This begins to accept reality without necessarily approving of it. Eliminate external references of comparison. Have your own goals independent of what Tom, Dick or Harry do. Set out to be what you want, without references to what others have or don't have. Correct yourself out loud when you use a sentence such as "I always call you when I'm going to be late, so why didn't you call me?" to "I would have felt better if you had called me." This eliminates the erroneous notion that another's reason for calling is to become more like you. Rather than paying someone off by bringing wine or a gift in exchange for dinner or a party, wait until you feel like it and then send a bottle of wine with a note saying, "Just because I think you're a great person." Eliminate reciprocal trades; do something nice because you, rather than a special occasion, call for it. Spend the amount of money you want to on any gift, and don't be swayed by what was spent for you. Eliminate invitations based on obligation and fairness. Decide whom you'll see on an internal rather than external standard. Set up your own standards of conduct in your family based upon what you think is appropriate for you; have everyone else do the same. See if it isn't quite possible to make it happen without infringing on each other's rights. If you feel that being out three nights a week is what you want, but can't because someone has to watch the children, "fairness" doesn't have to enter into your decision-making. Perhaps make a babysitting arrangement, or have the kids go along, or whatever will be a mutually satisfying settlement. Bringing in the "It isn't fair" routine will lead to everyone's resentment — and to staying at home as well. Be a doer, not a complainer about injustice. For every injustice you suffer, there is a resolution that does not require you to be immobilized in any way. Remember that revenge is just another way of being controlled by others. Do what you, not they, decide is for you.

The chapter's concluding principle is: "It's not the injustice that counts, it's what you do about it."

## Best Practices

- ✅ Accept that justice is a mythological concept that does not exist in nature or in human society
- ✅ Fight injustice where possible through effective personal action, but refuse to be emotionally immobilized by its absence
- ✅ Replace "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate" or "I'd prefer…" to accept reality without approving of it
- ✅ Eliminate external comparisons — have your own goals independent of what others have or don't have
- ✅ Correct yourself out loud when you catch yourself making fairness-based comparisons ("I would have felt better if…")
- ✅ Eliminate reciprocal trades and obligation-based giving — do something nice because you choose to, not because you owe it
- ✅ Set your own standards of conduct in relationships and family life rather than enforcing external notions of fairness
- ✅ View jealousy as wasteful thinking that has nothing to do with your self-worth
- ✅ Be a doer, not a complainer about injustice — find resolutions that don't require you to be immobilized
- ❌ Don't keep a tally sheet of give-and-take in relationships — it breeds resentment and prevents genuine connection
- ❌ Don't use "It isn't fair" as a justification for doing things you don't want to do or resenting people you love
- ❌ Don't punish yourself with jealousy, bitterness, or self-pity when someone else's behavior disappoints you
- ❌ Don't justify immoral or inappropriate behavior by pointing to others who do the same ("If he can do it, so can I")
- ❌ Don't seek revenge — it is just another way of letting others control your emotional life
- ❌ Don't measure your life, your gifts, or your behavior by what others do or what you believe you are owed

## Security & Safety Notes

This chapter addresses emotional and psychological patterns — specifically the demand for justice, jealousy, and resentment — rather than digital or financial security. The strategies described are intended to liberate you from emotional immobility caused by perceived unfairness. The chapter's principle of acting on your own internal standards rather than external rules is a call to personal authenticity, not an endorsement of breaking laws or harming others.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You keep a mental tally sheet in your relationships, tracking who did what for whom and resenting any perceived imbalance.
  **Solution:** Throw away the binoculars that focus on what others are doing. Ask yourself whether your tally-keeping is making you happier or more miserable. Choose to give and act based on what you genuinely want, not on what you believe you are owed.

- **Problem:** You are immobilized by jealousy because you believe someone else's behavior is an injustice against you, and you define your self-worth by how you compare to them.
  **Solution:** Recognize that another person's choices were made independently of you and have nothing to do with your self-worth. View their behavior as something between other people rather than as something against you. Decide that you will not allow yourself to be distraught when someone else doesn't play fair.

- **Problem:** You use "It isn't fair" constantly — at home, at work, in traffic — and this refrain has become a permanent filter through which you experience life, breeding constant resentment.
  **Solution:** Replace "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate," or "I'd prefer…" This begins to accept reality without necessarily approving of it. When you catch yourself in a fairness comparison, ask: "Will being upset change this?" If not, shift to what you can do about it rather than what you believe you are owed.

- **Problem:** You justify bad behavior by pointing to others who do the same thing ("If he can do it, so can I"), or you refuse to act effectively because you believe the world is too unfair for your effort to matter.
  **Solution:** Recognize that other people's behavior is not a license for yours, nor is it an excuse for your own ineffectiveness. Make a list of what you think is unfair and use it as a guide for effective personal action — not as a catalogue of grievances that justify doing nothing. Decide that any unhappiness you experience will be the result of your own behavior, not someone else's.
