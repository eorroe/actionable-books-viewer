# Chapter VIII: The Fairness Trap

## Overview

This chapter examines the insistence that life be fair, that others behave equitably, and that outcomes be evenly distributed. The author presents this demand as an erroneous thinking pattern that keeps people stuck in resentment, jealousy, and constant comparison. He argues that the idea of perfect fairness is a concept not found in nature or in human systems, and that causing yourself ongoing negative feelings when you encounter unfair treatment is as self-damaging as guilt or approval-seeking. Through a series of lessons, the chapter shows how jealousy, tally-sheet relationships, and the "It's not fair!" refrain harm personal well-being, then provides concrete strategies for letting go of the demand for fairness, reducing jealousy, and living by your own chosen standards rather than external ideas of fairness.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you find yourself stewing in resentment because someone else got something you believe you deserved
- When you keep a mental or literal tally sheet in a relationship, measuring give-and-take and resenting perceived imbalances
- When jealousy, bitterness, or the "It's not fair!" refrain is preventing you from taking productive action

## Steps

### Step 1: Understand That Perfect Fairness Is Not Found in Nature or in Human Systems

Look at nature to confirm there is no system of fairness in the world: robins eat worms, spiders eat flies, cougars kill coyotes, and similar predator-prey relationships. Recognize that tornadoes, floods, tidal waves, and droughts cause harm without regard for who they affect. Accept that perfect fairness has limited applicability to your own choices about personal fulfillment and well-being. Accept that the world and the people in it will continue to act in ways you may consider unfair; you can choose how to respond, but your well-being does not depend on the presence or absence of fairness around you. The author describes the food chain (cougars killing coyotes, coyotes killing badgers, badgers killing mice, mice killing bugs) and natural disasters as evidence that perfect fairness is not a feature of the natural world. He also cites political and legal realities: Spiro Agnew evading taxes and becoming rich, Richard Nixon being exonerated while his subordinates serve short sentences in minimum-security prison, and the poor and minority-group members filling jails while the influential operate under separate rules.

### Step 2: Do Not Cause Yourself Lasting Negative Emotions When You Encounter Unfairness

Understand that the harmful pattern is not the desire for fairness itself, but the sense of being stuck that may result from unfair circumstances. Decide that you will work to address unfairness where you can, but refuse to be psychologically defeated or emotionally immobilized by it. Work at helping to reduce unfairness while deciding that you will not experience ongoing negative emotions about the absence of perfect fairness. Choose to address unfairness and refuse to be pulled into lasting negative emotions about it. The author notes that poverty, war, pestilence, crime, prostitution, drug abuse, and murder have persisted over centuries and, based on human history, will continue. He argues that choosing to continue experiencing ongoing negative emotions about this is as self-damaging as guilt, approval-seeking, or any other pattern of self-criticism.

### Step 3: Eliminate the "It's Not Fair!" Slogan From Your Relationships

Stop comparing yourself to other individuals or groups. Stop using sentences like "If that person can do it, I can do it too," "It's not right for you to have more than I," and "But I didn't get to do that, why should you?" Stop deciding what is good for you on the basis of someone else's choices. Stop letting other people set your emotional state. Stop running your life on the basis of someone else and begin creating your own life. Discard the habit of focusing on what others are doing; eliminate comparisons to others. The author presents Judy, a client in an unhappy five-year marriage, who ran her marriage on a tally sheet. When her husband said something unpleasant, she countered: "Why did you say that? I never say things like that to you." When he mentioned their children, she said: "That isn't fair. I never bring the children into an argument." When discussing an evening's entertainment, she said: "That isn't fair. You get out all the time while I have to stay home with the children." Judy was more concerned with correcting perceived wrongs than examining and improving her marriage. The author notes that the backdrop for most emotional distress is making other people's behavior more important to your emotional state than your own.

### Step 4: Reduce Jealousy by Recognizing It as a Demand for Unfair Treatment From Others

Set as a goal the reduction of jealousy as a harmful thinking pattern if it creates any degree of emotional stuckness. Decide that another person's choices (such as a partner loving another) were made independently of you and may have many reasons of their own, all unrelated to you. View someone else's affair or affectionate behavior as something between other people rather than as something directed against you. Recognize that someone else's behavior has nothing to do with your sense of personal adequacy. Stop bruising yourself with self-critical jealousy in which you are less important than your partner or their other partner. Decide that you will not allow yourself to be distraught when someone else does not behave the way you want them to. The author presents Helen, a client furious because her husband had an affair. Helen became obsessed with questions like "Where did I go wrong?" "What's wrong with me?" and "Wasn't I good enough for him?" She even contemplated having an affair herself to restore a sense of balance. The author explains that Helen's emotional condition will not improve until she decides that her husband's decision was independent of her and that it has nothing to do with her sense of personal adequacy. The 17th-century poet John Dryden called jealousy "the jaundice of the soul."

### Step 5: Recognize Common "Demand for Fairness" Behaviors in Yourself

The book identifies the following behaviors to watch for and reduce:

1. Complaining that others make more money for doing the same work.
2. Resenting that celebrities (Frank Sinatra, Sammy Davis, Barbra Streisand, Catfish Hunter, Joe Namath) make large salaries.
3. Resenting that others get away with infractions while you always get caught.
4. Using "Would I treat you that way?" sentences, assuming everyone should be exactly like you.
5. Always returning favors immediately to keep exchanges feeling equal.
6. Kissing someone back or saying "I love you, too" instead of accepting it and expressing your own feelings whenever you choose.
7. Having sex with someone out of obligation because it isn't fair not to be cooperative.
8. Always insisting on things being consistent (avoiding the trap of requiring others to match your behavior exactly).
9. In arguments, insisting on a clear-cut decision with winners considered correct and losers considered incorrect.
10. Using the fairness argument to get your own way and then resenting the lack of fairness.
11. Saying it isn't fair to kids, parents, or neighbors and consequently doing things you'd rather not do, feeling resentment.
12. Playing the "If that person can do it, so can I" game to justify behavior outside your value system.
13. Cutting off another driver on the highway, hurrying to get in front of a slow driver to slow them down, or leaving highbeams on because other drivers are doing it.
14. Spending the same amount of money on a gift that someone else spent on you.
15. Paying back every favor with a favor of equal value.
16. Keeping a tally sheet equal rather than doing what you would like.

The author lists all of the above behaviors as manifestations of the demand for fairness, noting they all stem from the assumption in your thinking that things must be fair.

### Step 6: Recognize the Emotional Benefits That Keep You Stuck in the Demand for Fairness

Release the benefit of feeling superior by keeping your tally sheet in balance; give up the feeling of being more upright than others. Release the benefit of avoiding responsibility for yourself and justifying inaction by blaming unfair people and events; stop blaming others and take action instead. Release the benefit of gaining attention, pity, and self-pity by positioning yourself as a victim of unfairness; stop using these to support your sense of self. Release the benefit of justifying behavior by making your actions someone else's responsibility ("If that person can do it, so can I"). Release the benefit of giving yourself an excuse for being ineffective ("If they aren't going to do anything, then neither am I"). Release the benefit of having a topic for conversation that helps you avoid talking honestly about yourself with others. Release the benefit of always making "the just decision" based on an external idea of fairness. Release the benefit of manipulating others (especially children) by telling them they are being unfair if they are not exactly like you or do not keep a precise tally of give-and-take. Release the benefit of justifying aggressive behavior and retaliation because everything must be fair and equal. The author explains that as long as unfairness is the cause of your problems, you may believe you can never change until the unfairness goes away — which, based on history, is unlikely to happen. He also notes that complaining about unfairness cuts off moments and helps you avoid dealing more honestly and personally with people around you.

### Step 7: Use Practical Strategies to Let Go of the Pointless Insistence on Fairness

Make a list of everything in your world that you think is unfair. Use your list as a guide for effective personal action. Ask yourself: "Will the unfairness go away if I am experiencing ongoing negative emotions?" Address the thinking pattern that brings about your distress, and you will be on your way to freeing yourself from the insistence on perfect fairness. When you find yourself saying "Would I do that to you?" or any similar sentence, change it to: "You are different from me, although I find that hard to accept right now." This will open, rather than close, communication between you and the other person. Begin to view your emotional life as independent of whatever anyone else does. This will free you from the habit of being hurt when others behave differently from the way you want them to. Try to view each decision with reasonable proportion, rather than as an all-important life-changing event. Follow the principle from Carlos Castaneda of a person with practical knowledge who lives by acting, not by thinking about acting — who knows that no single event is more important than any other, chooses any act, and acts it out as if it mattered, yet knows it does not determine the whole of life. Replace the sentence "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate," or "I'd prefer…" This begins to accept reality without necessarily approving of it. Eliminate comparisons to others. Have your own goals independent of what any other person does. Set out to be what you want, without references to what others have or don't have. Correct yourself out loud when you use a sentence such as "I always call you when I'm going to be late, so why didn't you call me?" to "I would have felt better if you had called me." This eliminates the assumption that another person's reason for calling is to become more like you. Rather than repaying someone by bringing wine or a gift in exchange for dinner or a party, wait until you feel like it and then send it with a note saying, "Just because I think you're a great person." Eliminate equal-value exchanges; do something kind because you choose to, not because a special occasion calls for it. Spend the amount of money you want to on any gift, and don't be swayed by what was spent for you. Eliminate invitations based on obligation and fairness. Decide whom you'll see on an internal rather than external basis. Set up your own standards of conduct in your family based upon what you think is appropriate for you; have everyone else do the same. See if it is possible to make it happen without infringing on each other's rights. If you feel that being out three nights a week is what you want, but can't because someone has to watch the children, "fairness" does not have to enter into your decision-making. Perhaps make a babysitting arrangement, or have the children go along, or whatever will be a mutually satisfying arrangement. Bringing in the "It isn't fair" routine will lead to everyone's resentment — and to staying at home as well. Be someone who takes action, not someone who only complains about unfairness. For every unfair situation you experience, there is a resolution that does not require you to be immobilized in any way. Remember that retaliation is just another way of being controlled by others. Do what you, not they, decide is for you.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Accept that perfect fairness is not a feature of nature or of human systems
- ✅ Address unfairness where possible through effective personal action, but refuse to be emotionally immobilized by its absence
- ✅ Replace "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate" or "I'd prefer…" to accept reality without approving of it
- ✅ Eliminate comparisons to others — have your own goals independent of what others have or don't have
- ✅ Correct yourself out loud when you catch yourself making fairness-based comparisons ("I would have felt better if…")
- ✅ Eliminate equal-value exchanges and obligation-based giving — do something kind because you choose to, not because you owe it
- ✅ Set your own standards of conduct in relationships and family life rather than enforcing external ideas of fairness
- ✅ View jealousy as a harmful thinking pattern that has nothing to do with your sense of personal adequacy
- ✅ Be someone who takes action, not someone who only complains about unfairness — find responses that do not require you to stay stuck
- ❌ Don't keep a tally sheet of give-and-take in relationships — it breeds resentment and prevents genuine connection
- ❌ Don't use "It isn't fair" as a justification for doing things you don't want to do or resenting people you care about
- ❌ Don't cause yourself lasting negative emotions — jealousy, bitterness, or self-pity — when someone else's behavior disappoints you
- ❌ Don't justify behavior by pointing to others who do the same ("If that person can do it, so can I")
- ❌ Don't seek retaliation — it is just another way of letting others control your emotional life
- ❌ Don't measure your life, your gifts, or your behavior by what others do or what you believe you are owed

## Security & Safety Notes

This chapter addresses emotional and psychological patterns — specifically the demand for fairness, jealousy, and resentment — rather than digital or financial security. The strategies described are intended to free you from emotional stuckness caused by perceived unfairness. The chapter's principle of acting on your own chosen standards rather than external rules is a call to personal authenticity, not an endorsement of breaking laws or harming others.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You keep a mental tally sheet in your relationships, tracking who did what for whom and resenting any perceived imbalance.
  **Solution:** Discard the habit of focusing on what others are doing. Ask yourself whether your tally-keeping is making you happier or more miserable. Choose to give and act based on what you genuinely want, not on what you believe you are owed.

- **Problem:** You are stuck by jealousy because you believe someone else's behavior is unfair toward you, and you define your sense of personal adequacy by how you compare to them.
  **Solution:** Recognize that another person's choices were made independently of you and have nothing to do with your sense of personal adequacy. View their behavior as something between other people rather than as something against you. Decide that you will not allow yourself to be distraught when someone else does not behave the way you want them to.

- **Problem:** You use "It isn't fair" constantly — at home, at work, in traffic — and this refrain has become a constant filter through which you experience life, breeding constant resentment.
  **Solution:** Replace "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate," or "I'd prefer…" This begins to accept reality without necessarily approving of it. When you catch yourself in a fairness comparison, ask: "Will ongoing negative emotions change this?" If not, shift to what you can do about it rather than what you believe you are owed.

- **Problem:** You justify behavior by pointing to others who do the same thing ("If that person can do it, so can I"), or you refuse to act effectively because you believe the world is too unfair for your effort to matter.
  **Solution:** Recognize that other people's behavior is not a justification for yours, nor is it an excuse for your own ineffectiveness. Make a list of what you think is unfair and use it as a guide for effective personal action — not as a list of grievances that justify doing nothing. Decide that any unhappiness you experience will be the result of your own behavior, not someone else's.
