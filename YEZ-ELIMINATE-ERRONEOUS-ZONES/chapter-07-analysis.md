## Chapter VII: Breaking the Barrier of Convention

### Lesson 1: Shift from External to Internal Locus of Control
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Examine whether you assign responsibility for your emotional state to someone or something external to yourself.
  2. If asked why you feel bad, notice whether you respond with answers like “My parents mistreat me,” “She hurt my feelings,” “My friends don’t like me,” “My luck is down,” or “Things just aren’t going well.”
  3. If asked why you are so happy, notice whether you respond with answers like “My friends treat me well,” “My luck has changed,” “Nobody is bugging me,” or “She came through for me.”
  4. Shift to internally oriented answers such as: “I tell myself the wrong things,” “I put too much emphasis on what others say,” “I worry about what someone else thinks,” “I’m not strong enough now to avoid being unhappy,” and “I don’t have the skills to keep me from being miserable.”
  5. When you are feeling good, use “I” references such as: “I worked hard at being happy,” “I made things work for me,” “I’m telling myself the right things,” “I’m in charge of me, and this is where I choose to be.”
  6. Take responsibility for everything that you experience emotionally.
  7. Recognize that virtually all shoulds and traditions are imposed by external sources, and if you are loaded with shoulds and unable to break conventions prescribed by others, you are operating from an external locus of control.
  8. Exercise internal control over your own thinking, feeling and behavior.
- **Examples from book:** Barbara, a client with an obesity problem, blamed her mother (who forced food on her as a child), her husband (who neglected her), her children (who were inconsiderate), and even her own metabolism and the stars. She tried Weight Watchers, pills, diet doctors, and astrology—all externally directed. After several weeks of counseling, Barbara began to recognize that her unhappiness was the result of her own choices. Her first decision was to change her eating habits through sheer self-discipline. “Next time she felt hungry she resolved to reward herself with thoughts of her own internal strength rather than a cookie. Instead of blaming her husband and children for treating her badly and driving her to food, she began to see that she had been playing the martyr for years, virtually begging them to exploit her. Once Barbara began demanding to be treated well, she found that her family was only too ready to treat her that way, and instead of seeking solace in food she found fulfillment in relationships based on mutual respect and love. Barbara even decided to spend less time with her mother who she had come to see as running her life and ruining it with overdoses of food. Once Barbara came to recognize that her mother did not control her and that she could see her when she chose to, not when her mother said she should, and similarly that she didn’t have to eat that piece of chocolate cake just because her mother said she should, Barbara began to enjoy rather than resent the time they spent together. Finally Barbara realized that therapy had nothing to do with anything outside herself. She had to change herself. She is now not only thinner, but happier as well. She knows that it isn’t her husband or her children, her mother or the stars that are making her happy. She knows it is herself, for now she controls her own mind.”

### Lesson 2: Eliminate Blame and Hero Worship
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize that all blame is a waste of time. No matter how much fault you find with another, and regardless of how much you blame him, it will not change you.
  2. Understand that blame keeps the focus off you when you are looking for external reasons to explain your unhappiness or frustration.
  3. Realize that even if blame has some effect, it will not be on you. You may succeed in making another feel guilty for something by blaming him, but you won’t succeed in changing whatever it is about you that is making you unhappy.
  4. Avoid looking to others to determine your values. If so-and-so does it, then I should do it too.
  5. Do not model your own behavior on the standards of heroes.
  6. Recognize that all of your heroes are people. They are all human. They do the same things that you do every day.
  7. Stop elevating others to positions above yourself and giving them the responsibility for your good feelings.
  8. Take credit as well as responsibility for yourself.
  9. Be your own hero.
- **Examples from book:** The book states: “The tendency to focus on others can go to the opposite extreme where it surfaces as hero worship. In this case you may find yourself looking to others to determine your values. If so-and-so does it, then I should do it too. Hero worship is a form of self-repudiation. It makes others more important than you, and relates your own fulfillment to something outside of yourself. While there is nothing self-defeating about appreciating others and their accomplishments, it becomes an erroneous zone when you model your own behavior on their standards. All the great heroes of your life have taught you nothing. And they are no better than you, in any way. Politicians, actors, athletes, rock-stars, your boss, therapist, teacher, spouse, or whoever, are just skillful at what they do—nothing more. And if you make them your heroes and elevate them to positions above yourself, then you are into that external bag of giving others the responsibility for your good feelings. If you blame at one end or hero worship at the other, you are someplace on this Focusing On Others Line. You are behaving as a fool if you look outside of you for an explanation of how you should feel or what you should do.”

### Lesson 3: Escape the Right vs. Wrong Trap
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Stop thinking in erroneous right vs. wrong ways. Instead of attempting to convince someone how wrong they are, have discussions in which you have no should-expectations for the other person.
  2. As long as the other person is allowed to be different from you, you will eliminate the incessant arguing in which you doggedly, albeit frustratedly, pursue being right.
  3. Recognize that all rights and wrongs of every description represent shoulds of one kind or another, and the shoulds get in your way, particularly when they conflict with another person’s need to have his own as well.
  4. Think of any decision as bringing about something different, or more effective, or legal, but the moment it becomes a question of right vs. wrong, recognize that you are trapped into the “I’ve always got to be right and when things or people are not right, then I’m going to be unhappy” trap.
  5. Stop dividing the world neatly into extremes such as black/white, yes/no, good/bad and right/wrong.
- **Examples from book:** Clifford, who was in a marriage in which he argued every day about every conceivable topic, was told: “Instead of attempting to convince your wife how wrong she is, why not simply have discussions in which you have no should-expectations for her. As long as she is allowed to be different from you, you’ll eliminate the incessant arguing in which you doggedly, albeit frustratedly, pursue being right.” Clifford was able to set aside this neurotic need, and also to put some communication and love back in his marriage.

### Lesson 4: Overcome Indecisiveness as a Spin-Off of Right vs. Wrong Thinking
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. When making a decision, take away the rightness and wrongness of every decision, because right implies a guarantee.
  2. If you are trying to decide on the right college to attend, shift the process to: “There is no such thing as the right college. If I select A then these are the more likely consequences, while B will probably bring about these.” Neither is right, one is simply different than the other, and you’ll have no guarantees regardless of the choice.
  3. View all potential outcomes as being neither right nor wrong, good nor bad, or even better or worse. Simply different.
  4. If you buy one dress which you like, this is how you are going to look, which is only different from (not better than) wearing that other dress.
  5. Once you give up those inaccurate and self-destructive rights and wrongs, find decision-making a simple matter of weighing which consequences you’d prefer at a given present-moment.
  6. If you begin to choose regret over the decision, rather than deciding that regret is a waste of your time (because it keeps you living in the past), simply resolve to make a different decision in your next present moment, one that will bring consequences that the earlier decision failed to bring. But never attempt to put it into a right or wrong categorization.
  7. Recognize that nothing is more important than anything else. The child collecting seashells is not doing something more right or wrong than the President of General Motors making a major corporate decision. They are different. Nothing more!
- **Examples from book:** “If you buy this dress which you like, this is how you are going to look, which is only different from (not better than) wearing that dress. Once you give up those inaccurate and self-destructive rights and wrongs, you’ll find decision-making a simple matter of weighing which consequences you’d prefer at a given present-moment. And if you begin to choose regret over the decision, rather than deciding that regret is a waste of your time (because it keeps you living in the past) you’ll simply resolve to make a different decision in your next present moment, one that will bring consequences that the earlier decision failed to bring. But never attempting to put it into a right or wrong categorization. Nothing is more important than anything else. The child collecting seashells is not doing something more right or wrong than the President of General Motors making a major corporate decision. They are different. Nothing more!”

### Lesson 5: Stop Musterbating (Eliminate Shoulds, Musts, and Oughts)
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize that you are “musterbating” whenever you find yourself behaving in ways that you feel you must, even though you might prefer some other form of behavior.
  2. Understand that the shoulds always produce a feeling of strain, which is all the greater the more a person tries to actualize his shoulds in his behavior.
  3. Recognize that because of externalizations, the shoulds always contribute to disturbance in human relations in one way or another.
  4. Ask yourself: Do you feel you should be kind to your colleagues, supportive of your spouse, helpful to your children and always work hard? And if at any time you fail in one of these shoulds, do you berate yourself and hence take on that strain and disturbance?
  5. Determine whether these shoulds belong to others and you have merely borrowed them. If so, you are musterbating.
  6. Realize that there are just as many should-nots as there are shoulds.
  7. Understand that you are allowed to be undignified if you choose to. No one is keeping score on you, or going to punish you for not being something that someone else said you must be.
  8. Recognize that you can never be anything that you don’t want to be all of the time. It’s just not possible. Therefore, any should will have to produce strain in you, since you won’t be able to fulfill your erroneous expectation.
  9. Realize that the strain does not result from your undignified, nonsupportive, indiscreet or whatever behavior, but from the imposition of the should.
- **Examples from book:** “Perhaps these are not your shoulds. If, in fact, they belong to others and you have merely borrowed them, then you are musterbating. There are just as many should-nots as there are shoulds. These include: you should not be rude, angry, foolish, silly, juvenile, lewd, gloomy, offensive and scores of others. But you don’t have to musterbate. Ever. It’s all right to be lacking in composure, or to not understand. You’re allowed to be undignified if you choose to. No one is keeping score on you, or going to punish you for not being something that someone else said you must be.”

### Lesson 6: Evaluate and Reform Etiquette Rules
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Distinguish between good manners (which simply entail consideration for other people) and etiquette guidelines (which are meaningless rules that were composed arbitrarily at one time).
  2. Recognize that about ninety percent of all etiquette guidelines are meaningless rules.
  3. There is no proper way for you; there is only what you decide is right for you—as long as you don’t make it hard for others to get along.
  4. Choose how you’ll introduce people, what you’ll tip, what you’ll wear, how you’ll speak, where you’ll sit, how you’ll eat, and so on, strictly on the basis of what you want.
  5. Any time you fall into the trap of “What should I wear,” or “How should I do it,” recognize that you’re giving up a chunk of yourself.
  6. Be self-rather than other-directed in the everyday running of your life.
  7. Being true to yourself means being devoid of the need for an external support system.
- **Examples from book:** “Think of all the little meaningless rules you’ve been encouraged to adopt simply because an Emily Post, Amy Vanderbilt, or Abigail van Buren has so written. Eat your corn on the cob this way, always wait for the hostess to start before eating, introduce the man to the woman, sit on that side of the church at a wedding, tip this, wear that, use these words. Don’t consult yourself; look it up in the book.”

### Lesson 7: Resist Blind Obedience to Rules and Laws
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Determine for yourself which rules work, and are necessary to preserve order in our culture, and which can be broken without harm to yourself or others.
  2. Understand that there is no percentage in rebelling just for the sake of rebelling, but there are great rewards in being your own person and living your life according to your own standards.
  3. When a rule is foolish or no longer makes sense, and you are unable to function effectively because you must follow senseless rules, reconsider the rules and your behavior.
  4. If you must comply with all the rules all the time, recognize that you are destined to a life of emotional servitude.
  5. Recognize that the law is there to serve you, not to make you a servant.
  6. Do not blindly do as you’re told. Once you recognize that the law is there to serve you, not to make you a servant, you can begin to eliminate that musterbation behavior.
- **Examples from book:** The Nazis executed six million Jews because it was the “Law.” A Suffolk County spokesman explained why people who had been inadvertently overcharged on their real estate taxes could not be reimbursed: “The law says that past tax bills cannot be reevaluated once they are paid. It’s the law, I can’t help it. My job is to enforce it, not to interpret it.” At a swimming pool, young people were lounging near the edge when the pool was empty because the rule said it was for adults between 6:00 and 8:00 P.M. “This was the rule, and despite the fact that no adults wanted to use it, the rule was still being enforced. No flexibility, no ability to alter the rule when circumstances so warrant, simply blind obedience to a rule that had no logical reason at that moment.” On Guam, enlisted men sat in the rain with one of their own ranks guarding a section of empty red benches reserved for officers at the midnight movie which officers never attended. When asked why, the answer was: “I don’t make the rules, I just enforce them.”

### Lesson 8: Resist Enculturation and Traditions When They Affect You Negatively - Become a "Shrugger"
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Make decisions for yourself and carry them out as efficiently and quietly as possible. No bandwagons or hostile demonstrations where they will do no good.
  2. Recognize that the foolish rules, traditions and policies will never go away, but you don’t have to be a part of them.
  3. Simply shrug as others go through their sheep motions. If they want to behave that way, fine for them but that’s not for you.
  4. To make a big fuss is almost always the surest way to incur wrath and create more obstacles for yourself. You’ll find scores of everyday occurrences where it is easier to circumvent the rules quietly than to start a protest movement.
  5. Decide to be the person you want to be, or the one others want you to be. It’s up to you.
  6. Learn to allow others their choice when they choose to obey even if it hurts them. No anger, only your own convictions.
  7. You may be viewed as insubordinate, which is the price you’ll pay for thinking for yourself. You may be seen as different, be labeled selfish or rebellious, incur disapproval from many “normal” people, and at times be ostracized.
  8. Do not be guided by the policies of others which have you being a victim as the final result.
  9. Do not stage a revolt or make a big fuss. Simply slip away and allow everyone else to participate in the demeaning ritual. Pass up your chance to participate. No put-down of those who chose otherwise, no useless fighting, simply shrugging and letting others go their own way.
- **Examples from book:** A colleague in the Navy stationed aboard an aircraft carrier was commanded to spell out in human formation the words, HI IKE, so that President Eisenhower could look down from his helicopter and view the message. “My friend decided the idea was insane, and decided not to do it, because it conflicted with everything he stood for. But rather than stage a revolt, he simply slipped away for the afternoon, and allowed everyone else to participate in this demeaning ritual. He passed up his one chance to dot the i in Hi. No put-down of those who chose otherwise, no useless fighting, simply shrugging and letting others go their own way.”

### Lesson 9: Identify and Eliminate Typical Should Behaviors
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Examine whether you believe there is a place for everything and everything must be in its place. Recognize that the organization syndrome means you are uncomfortable if things aren’t in their designated locations.
  2. Stop asking “What should I wear” on a regular basis, as if there were only one acceptable mode of dressing and it is determined by other people. White pants and pastel colors are only worn in the summer. Wool is always a winter fabric. Stop being a slave to the dictates of fashion critics, and wearing only “What’s in” because, after all, you have to fit in.
  3. Stop assuming that certain drinks must go with certain food. White wine must accompany fish and fowl. Red wine goes only with beef. Stop being locked into somebody’s rules on what to eat with what.
  4. Stop shifting blame for your actions to others. “It’s really her fault, she made us late.” “Don’t blame me, he’s the one who did it.”
  5. Stop feeling you must go to a wedding or send a gift, even if you don’t like them. You just don’t ignore invitations even when you want to. Stop feeling resentful about buying the gift, but going through the motions anyhow because that’s the way things are supposed to be done. Similarly, stop attending funerals that you’d rather not go to, but do because you’re supposed to.
  6. Stop attending religious services which you dislike and don’t believe in because it is expected of you and you want to do the right thing.
  7. Stop giving titles to those who serve you, which elevates them by implication to a position higher than you. If you call your dentist “doctor,” ask whether it is really just a vocational title. If he is paid to serve you, why is it that he gets a title and you get called by your name?
  8. Stop going to bed when it gets to be bedtime, rather than when you’re tired.
  9. Stop having sex only one or two ways because those are the only acceptable ways, or only participating in sexual activities when all conditions are met.
  10. Stop selecting roles in daily living because the culture demands it. Women do the dishes, men take out the garbage. Housework is for the wife, outdoor work is for the husband. Boys do this, girls do that.
  11. Stop obeying silly household rules and traditions that don’t work for your family, such as asking permission to leave the table, everyone eating at the same time when it is more inconvenient to do it that way or bedtimes that are without rationale.
  12. Stop following the dictates of all signs whether they make sense or not. No Talking! No Entry! No anything! Never challenge a sign, or even assume that it doesn’t belong there in the first place. People make signs and people also make mistakes.
  13. Stop keeping mattress tags on for years because they say do not remove under penalty of law.
  14. Stop always having Sunday dinner at Mamma’s, although you’d rather not. After all, it is a tradition, and even if everyone doesn’t like it, including Mamma, you must preserve tradition.
  15. Stop when reading a book, always starting at page one and reading every word to the end, even if half of it doesn’t apply. Stop finishing a book you don’t like because you’re halfway through it, and, if you’ve read half, you must read it all.
  16. Stop women never asking men for dates. After all, that’s the male’s role. Or never initiating a telephone call, or opening the door for a man, or paying the bill, or countless other absurd traditions that serve no real purpose.
  17. Stop sending out holiday greeting cards and resenting them. Stop doing so because you’ve always done it, and it is expected of you.
  18. Stop striving for grades in school or forcing your children to do so. Stop learning not for your own satisfaction but for the symbols that will eventually appear on the transcript.
  19. Stop always asking: “Is he/she right for me?” and being regularly perplexed in search of the right person.
  20. Stop going everywhere with your partner because it is expected, even though you both would prefer to be in different places at that particular time.
  21. Stop consulting a “how-to” book for everything, because every job must be done a certain way. Learn to differentiate between manuals that impart useful information and those that merely tell you how things ought to be.
  22. Stop asking “Is this the right dress, hat, automobile, furniture, salad dressing, appetizer, book, college, job, etc.” and being anxious over the search for the right item.
  23. Stop making rewards, plaques, titles, honors and all merit badges more important than your own evaluation of your achievements.
  24. Stop saying, “I could never be as great as———”
  25. Stop applauding in an audience when you didn’t like the performance.
  26. Stop tipping for poor service.
  27. Stop sports-fan behavior in which you go crazy over a home team victory or loss, and live vicariously through the accomplishments or lack of accomplishments of athletes.
- **Examples from book:** James Michener describes in Hawaii the New Englanders who arrived in the tropical climate of Hawaii, and when October arrived, even though it was still eighty-five degrees, they habitually trotted out their winter clothes and dressed uncomfortably for six months…Why? Because they were supposed to.

### Lesson 10: Recognize and Avoid the Common Payoffs of Musterbation
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize that you can take solace in being a “good boy” or a “good girl” by following all of your shoulds. You can pat yourself on the back for being obedient. This dividend is a regressive one in which you go back to an earlier developmental period when you were rewarded with approval whenever you behaved, which meant relying on someone else to establish your rules of conduct. Do not do this.
  2. Recognize that your obedience to the external should allows you to assign the responsibility for your standing still to the should rather than to yourself. As long as the should is the rationale for what you are (or aren’t), you can avoid the risks involved in trusting yourself to change. Thus your shoulds keep you from growing.
  3. Stop using your musts to maneuver others. By telling someone this is the way it should be done, you can make him do it the way you want it to be done.
  4. Stop hauling out a should when you lack confidence in yourself. As your self-image wanes, the should becomes your bulwark.
  5. Stop remaining self-righteous about your behavior and retaining your hostility when others don’t fit into the shoulds that you have for yourself and the rest of the world. Hence, you build yourself up in your mind at the expense of others who don’t obey the rule-patterns.
  6. Stop trying to win approval by conforming. You feel good by fitting-in, which is what you were told all along that you should be doing. That old approval-seeking need creeps in here as well.
  7. Stop focusing on others, and living through their successes and failures, so that you don’t have to work on yourself. Having heroes can reinforce your own low opinion of yourself and allow you to escape from having to work on yourself.
- **Examples from book:** Marjorie has a should in her head that all premarital sex is a taboo. She is thirty-four years old, and to date she has never had a sexual experience because of this learned should. But Marjorie has no inner peace. She would like to have a sexual relationship, and she is very much dissatisfied with herself in this area. Moreover, it is possible that Marjorie will never get married, and her should (in this case should-not) would then keep her from participating in sex for an entire lifetime. When she is confronted with this possibility she shudders at the thought, and yet her should-not is still there. Marjorie has spillover effects from her should. She can’t even stay overnight in the same house with her boyfriend out of fear of being judged by others. Thus she is constantly being inconvenienced because of her shoulds by having to come home to Mamma at night. Hanging onto them keeps her from the risky business of testing herself out in the scary act of sexual involvement. But her response is always, “I shouldn’t do it.” Plainly, her shoulds work against her own happiness.

### Lesson 11: Use Specific Strategies for Removing Your Shoulds
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Begin with insight into your own behavior. Study the neurotic dividends spelled out above. Then ask yourself why are you burdening yourself with so many shoulds. Ask yourself if you really believe in them, or if you’ve just become accustomed to behaving that way.
  2. List all of the rules you abide by, which just don’t seem to apply. Those stupid conventional behaviors that you complain about, but can’t seem to shed. Then, make your own “Rules of Conduct” that make the greatest amount of sense to you. Write them down, even if you don’t believe at this time that you are capable of living them out.
  3. Start your own traditions. For example, if you’ve always decorated your Christmas Tree on Christmas Eve, and you’d rather do it three days before, begin a new Christmas tradition—one that makes sense to you.
  4. Have a consultation with your relatives and friends about the many rules of conduct you all follow which you find distasteful. Perhaps you’ll be able to bring about some new rules which appear to be more reasonable to everyone. You’ll discover that the old rules are still in effect only because no one ever seriously thought to challenge them in the past.
  5. Keep an internal/external journal. Write down your “external” references in which you assign responsibility to others for how you are feeling. See if you can shift yourself to the “internal” side with some new acts of courage. Keep track of your success in moving over to the internal side.
  6. See how many rules you impose on others. Ask them if they really need those directives, or if they would behave in the identical fashion without them. Perhaps you’ll even learn that they could come up with more effective and flexible guidelines.
  7. Take a risk on challenging a rule or policy that you’d like to eliminate. But be prepared to deal without hostility with the consequences of your behavior. For example if you’ve always believed that a woman shouldn’t ask a man for a date and you find yourself without a date on a weekend, call up a man and see what happens. Or, take back a defective garment even if the rule is no refunds, and effectively challenge the policy, vowing to go to the top if necessary. Don’t be guided by the policies of others which have you being a victim as the final result.
  8. Think of decisions as having different consequences, rather than as being right or wrong. In making decisions, eliminate the notion of right and wrong and say either one is okay, and each will bring about different consequences. Trust yourself to make the decision rather than relying on some external kind of guarantee. Please yourself instead of meeting an outside standard.
  9. Try to live in your present moments and make your rules and shoulds apply only for that time. Rather than assuming them to be universals, recognize them as applicable only at this moment.
  10. Refuse to share your rule-breaking behavior with anyone. It is only for you, and you don’t want to get into an approval-seeking posture, in which the reason for resisting enculturation is to win attention and therefore adulation.
  11. Get rid of the roles that you (and others) are assuming in your life. Be whatever you want to be, rather than what you think you are supposed to be because you are a man, woman, middle-aged, or whatever.
  12. Refuse for a given segment of a conversation to focus on others. Practice in ever-increasing time periods not projecting blame, or talking about another person, event, or idea in a complaining or fault-finding manner.
  13. Stop waiting for others to change. Ask yourself why others should be different simply because you would like it better if they were. Recognize that every person has a right to be whatever they choose, even if you irritate yourself about it.
  14. Come up with a blame list, wherein you detail everything about yourself that you dislike. Create a table with columns: “Who, What Is To Blame” and “What I Dislike About Me and My Life.” For example: Sara Lee, metabolism, mother, McDonalds, heredity — I’m too fat…….. Parents, grandparents, God, genetics, homework, Con-Ed — I have poor eyesight…….. Elementary teachers, sister, deficient math genes, mother — I’m lousy in mathematics…. Luck, all creeps at my school, parents, I can’t wear makeup — I have no boy/girl friends…. Genetics, God, mother — I’m too tall…….. The economy, Dow-Jones, divorce, my children hate me, illness — I’m unhappy…….. Mother, genetics, luck, no early nourishment, God, Satan — My breasts are too small…….. Helena Rubenstein, heredity, girl friend, the sun — My hair is the wrong color…. Presidents Ford, Nixon, Johnson, etc., the Communists, humanity — The state of the world bugs me. The neighborhood, “Those kinds of people,” zoning regulations — My neighbors are bitchy…….. The wind, the sun, the net is too high/low, the distractions, my cramps, my sore arm, leg, etc. — My tennis outcome…….. My metabolism, my period, my doctor, the food, the heat, the cold, the wind, the rain, the pollen, You Name It. — I don’t feel well……..
  15. Add up your total blame score and see if you are any different now that you have fault and blame appropriately doled out to all of the precise people and things that are responsible for your feelings. “Isn’t that something? You are exactly the same. Whether you blame or don’t, you stay as you are unless you do something constructive to correct your dislikes. You can use this as an exercise in seeing the futility of blaming.”
  16. Announce out loud that you just blamed and that you are working on eliminating this behavior. By stating it as a goal, you’ll be mindful of your proclivities in this direction.
  17. Decide that any and all unhappiness that you choose will never be the result of someone else, but rather that it will be the result of you and your own behavior. Remind yourself constantly that any externally caused unhappiness reinforces your own slavery, since it assumes that you have no control over yourself or them, but they have control over you.
  18. When someone is blaming, politely ask them, “Would you like to know if I want to hear what you are telling me right now?” That is, teach others not to use you as a blame receptacle and begin to label other people’s blaming and fault-finding so that you get in touch with recognizing it in yourself. You can label it with a nonoffensive treatment such as, “You just made George responsible for how you feel. Do you really think it’s that way?” Or, “You keep saying that if only the market would go up, you’d be happier. You really give it a lot of control over your life.” Your recognition of others’ blames and shoulds will help you to eliminate such behavior in yourself.
  19. Refer back to the lists of shoulds discussed earlier in this chapter. Try to substitute some new and different behavior for these old habits—perhaps a late night dinner, or changing your sexual position, or wearing what you like. Begin to trust yourself and give less credence to those external musts.
  20. Remind yourself that what other people do is not what bothers you, it’s your reaction to it. Instead of saying “They shouldn’t do that,” say, “I wonder why I bother myself with what they are doing.”

### Lesson 12: Become Your Own Judge and Live According to Your Own Standards
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Become your own judge of your conduct.
  2. Learn to rely on yourself to make present-moment decisions.
  3. Cease leafing through a lifetime of policies and traditions for an answer.
  4. Sing your own song of happiness in any way that you choose, oblivious to how it is supposed to be.
- **Examples from book:** Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote in Literary Ethics in 1838, “Men grind and grind in the mill of a truism, and nothing comes out but what was put in. But the moment they desert the tradition for a spontaneous thought, then poetry, wit, hope, virtue, learning anecdote, all flock to their aid.” The book comments: “What a beautiful thought. Stay with tradition and you ensure that you’ll always be the same, but toss it aside, and the world is yours to use as creatively as you choose.”
