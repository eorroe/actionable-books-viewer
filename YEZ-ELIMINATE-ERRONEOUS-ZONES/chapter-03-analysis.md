## Chapter III: You Don't Need Their Approval

### Lesson 1: Understand the Difference Between Wanting Approval and Needing Approval
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize that approval in itself is not unhealthy; adulation is deliciously pleasurable.
  2. Understand that approval-seeking becomes an erroneous zone only when it becomes a need rather than a want.
  3. If you want approval, you are simply happy to have the endorsement of other people.
  4. If you need approval, you are going to collapse if you don't get it.
  5. When approval-seeking becomes a need, you give up a chunk of yourself to the "outside person" whose advocacy you must have.
- **Examples from book:** Ozzie, the middle-aged man who shifted his position on mercy killing depending on whether his listener approved. With his father-in-law showing censure, Ozzie modified his position to: "What I meant was, if someone is conscious and actually asks to be killed, then mercy killing is O.K." With his boss showing vociferous disagreement, he shifted again: "What I meant was, only in extreme cases, when a patient is declared legally dead, then it's all right to pull the plug." With his brother showing instant concurrence, he didn't have to change at all. "There is no Ozzie, only the happenstance reactions of others which determine not only how Ozzie feels, but also what he thinks and says as well. Ozzie is whatever others want him to be."

### Lesson 2: Accept That You Can Never Please Everyone and Expect Disapproval
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Accept the fact that you can never please everyone; if you please fifty percent of the people you are doing quite well.
  2. Know that at least half the people in your world are going to disagree with at least half the things you say.
  3. When someone disapproves of something you say, instead of being hurt or instantly shifting your opinion to gain praise, remind yourself that you've just run into one of those folks in the fifty percent who don't agree with you.
  4. Knowing that you'll always get some disapproval for everything you feel, think, say or do is the way out of the tunnel of despair.
  5. Once you expect disapproval, you won't be inclined to hurt yourself with it, and you'll simultaneously stop equating the repudiation of an idea or a feeling with the repudiation of you.
  6. You can never escape disapproval, no matter how much you may want it to go away. For every opinion you have, there is a counterpart out there with exactly the opposite view.
- **Examples from book:** Abraham Lincoln's conversation at the White House reported by Francis B. Carpenter: "If I were to read, much less to answer all the attacks made on me, this shop might as well be closed for any other business. I do the very best I know how—the very best I can; and I mean to keep doing so until the end. If the end brings me out alright, what is said against me won't amount to anything. If the end brings me out wrong, ten angels swearing I was right would make no difference."

### Lesson 3: Use the "You" Strategy to Label Disapproval
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. When you note that someone is not agreeing with you and is even getting angry, rather than shifting or defending yourself, simply respond with: "You're getting upset, and you feel that I shouldn't think the way I do."
  2. This will keep you in touch with the fact that disapproval belongs to him, not you.
  3. The you strategy can be employed any time, and with amazing results if you master the technique.
  4. Fight your temptation to start with "I," thereby putting yourself in the position of defending or modifying what you just said in order to gain acceptance.

### Lesson 4: Label Manipulation Attempts When Others Withhold Approval
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. If you think someone else is trying to manipulate you by withholding approval, say so.
  2. Instead of turning wishy-washy for the purpose of reaping some approval benefits, say out loud: "Generally I would shift my position now in order to get you to like me, but I really believe in what I said, and you'll have to deal with your own feelings about it."
  3. Or say: "I guess you'd like me to change what I just said."
  4. The act of labeling it will be helpful in keeping in touch with your own thinking and behavior.

### Lesson 5: Thank Someone for Providing Data Even When You Don't Like It
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. When someone says something you don't like about your behavior, rather than trying to please them, simply thank them for pointing it out.
  2. The act of thanking puts an end to any approval-seeking.
- **Examples from book:** Your husband says that you're acting shy and nervous and he doesn't like it. Rather than trying to please him, you simply thank him for pointing it out. The approval-seeking behavior is gone.

### Lesson 6: Actively Seek Disapproval and Practice Not Being Upset
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Select someone who is bound to disagree with you.
  2. Flying in the face of the disapproval, maintain your position calmly.
  3. Tell yourself that you expect this "contrariness," that it's all right for them to be the way they are, and that it really has nothing to do with you.
  4. By going after disapproval rather than avoiding it, you'll build up your repertoire of behavior for dealing effectively with it.
  5. You'll get better at not being upset, and not having to alter your own views.

### Lesson 7: Practice Ignoring Disapproval and Not Paying Attention to Manipulation
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Ignore disapproval and do not pay any attention to those who attempt to manipulate you with their denunciation.
- **Examples from book:** A colleague giving a lecture to a large audience in Berlin where a member of the audience was seething and rattled off abusive remarks. The colleague's response was a simple "O.K.," and he went on with his talk. By ignoring the abuse, he proved that he wasn't going to evaluate himself on the basis of what someone else felt. The heckler stopped.

### Lesson 8: Break the Chain Between Others' Thoughts and Your Self-Worth
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Talk to yourself when you encounter disapproval.
  2. Say: "This is her stuff. I expect her to behave that way. It has nothing to do with me."
  3. This approach will eliminate the self-hurt that you inflict when you connect someone else's feeling with your own thoughts.

### Lesson 9: Ask Yourself Whether Their Agreement Would Make You Better Off
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. When you acquire disapproval, ask yourself this important question: If they agreed with me, would I be better off?
  2. The answer is obviously no.
  3. Whatever they think can have no effect on you unless you let it.
  4. You will most likely find that you are better liked by those important folks like a boss, or loved one when you can disagree with them without worry.

### Lesson 10: Accept That Many Won't Ever Understand You and That It's Okay
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Accept the simple fact that many won't ever understand you, and that it is okay.
  2. Conversely, accept that you won't understand many people who are very close to you, and you don't have to.
  3. It's all right for them to be different.
  4. The most fundamental understanding you can have is that you don't understand.
- **Examples from book:** Gustav Ischheiser from Appearances and Realities: "If people who do not understand each other at least understand that they do not understand each other, then they understand each other better than when, not understanding each other, they do not even understand that they do not understand each other."

### Lesson 11: Refuse to Argue or Try to Convince Anyone of the Rightness of Your Stance
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Simply believe your stance without arguing or trying to convince others.

### Lesson 12: Trust Yourself When Buying Personal Items Without Checking With Others
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Trust yourself when buying clothes or other personal items without first checking it out with someone whose opinion you value more than your own.

### Lesson 13: Stop Verifying Your Facts Through Others
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Stop substantiating your facts by having them verified by a spouse or someone else.
  2. Eliminate sentences like: "Isn't that right, honey?" or "Didn't we, Ralph?" or "Just ask Marie, she'll tell you."

### Lesson 14: Correct Yourself Out Loud When You Behave in an Approval-Seeking Way
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Correct yourself out loud whenever you've behaved in an approval-seeking way.
  2. This makes you aware of this tendency and helps you practice new behaviors.

### Lesson 15: Work at Eliminating Unnecessary Apologies
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Work at eliminating the numerous apologies that you make even when you aren't really sorry for what you've just said.
  2. All apologies are pleas for forgiveness, and requests for forgiveness are approval-seeking which takes the form of: "I know you wouldn't like me if I really meant what I just did, so please say that I'm still okay."
  3. Apologizing is a waste of time. If you need someone else to forgive you before you can feel better, then you are giving them control over your feelings.
  4. You can resolve not to behave in certain ways again and see some of your behavior as unfortunate, but apologizing behavior is a sickness that invests control of one's feelings in another.

### Lesson 16: Monitor and Balance Your Speaking Time in Conversations
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Clock the amount of time that you speak and compare it with the amount spoken by your partner and acquaintances.
  2. Work at not being the one who speaks infrequently and only when asked to participate.

### Lesson 17: Observe and Reduce Being Interrupted in Social Settings
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Observe at your next get-together how many more times you are interrupted.
  2. Notice whether you always condescend when you speak simultaneously with another member of the group.
  3. Your approval-seeking may be taking the form of timidity.
  4. Devise strategies for speaking without being interrupted by labeling the behavior as it crops up in your social milieu.

### Lesson 18: Track and Reduce Interrogative Sentences in Favor of Declarative Ones
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Chronicle how many declarative versus interrogative sentences you make.
  2. Notice whether you ask questions, seek permission and approval, as opposed to making a statement.
  3. For example, the question "Nice day, isn't it?" puts the other person into the problem-solving role, and you into the approval-seeking position.
  4. A simple "Nice day" is a declaration, rather than an attempt to seek an answer.
  5. If you are always asking questions of others you are into approval-seeking in what may seem a petty area, but which is reflective of your lack of confidence in your own ability to take charge.

### Lesson 19: Rewrite Approval-Seeking Song Lyrics to Reflect Personal Mastery
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. The next time you find yourself listening to a song that sends out the communiqués of approval-seeking, check for those lyrics which reflect the way you've been taught to feel, that is, that you can't make it if someone disapproves of you or lets you down.
  2. Rewrite the songs to fit a mind-set of personal mastery rather than approval-seeking.
- **Examples from book:**
  - "I make myself feel like a natural woman; it has nothing to do with you."
  - "I chose to love you. I must have wanted to do it then, but now I've changed my mind."
  - "People who need people are the unluckiest people in the world. But people who want love and enjoy people are making themselves happy."
  - "I make myself so very happy because of the things I tell myself about you."
  - "I am the sunshine of my life, and having you in it makes it even brighter."
  - "I can stop loving you, but at this point I choose not to."
  - "Without you I'm nothing" must be translated to "Without me I'm nothing, but having you makes this present moment very nice."

### Lesson 20: Understand the Supreme Irony of Approval-Seeking Behavior
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize that the people who get the most approval in life are those who never seek it out, who have no desire for it, and who are not preoccupied with achieving it.
  2. If you want all of that approval, the most effective way to get it is to not want it and to avoid chasing after it and to not demand it from everyone.
  3. By being in touch with yourself and using your positive self-image as a consultant, much more approval will come to you.
  4. When you see yourself as worthy you'll never be depressed when approval is withheld.
  5. Disapproval will be viewed by you as the natural consequence of living on this planet where people are individual in their perceptions.
- **Examples from book:** The fable of the big cat and the little cat chasing its tail. The kitten says: "I have learned that the best thing for a cat is happiness, and that happiness is my tail. Therefore, I am chasing it: and when I catch it, I shall have happiness." The old cat says: "My son, I, too, have paid attention to the problems of the universe. I, too, have judged that happiness is in my tail. But, I have noticed that whenever I chase after it, it keeps running away from me, and when I go about my business, it just seems to come after me wherever I go."

### Lesson 21: Recognize Common Approval-Seeking Behaviors in Yourself
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Identify whether you change a position or alter what you believe because someone shows signs of disapproval.
  2. Notice whether you sugar-coat a statement to avoid the reaction of displeasure.
  3. Observe whether you apple-polish in order to make someone like you.
  4. Notice whether you feel depressed or anxious when someone disagrees with you.
  5. Observe whether you feel insulted or put-down when someone states a contrary sentiment to your own.
  6. Notice whether you label someone a snob or "stuck-up," which is just another way of saying "Pay more attention to me."
  7. Observe whether you are excessively agreeable and head nodding, even when you don't agree at all with what is being said.
  8. Notice whether you perform chores for someone and feel resentful about not being able to say no.
  9. Observe whether you are intimidated by a sharp salesperson and buy something you don't want, or fear taking it back because he won't like you.
  10. Notice whether you eat a steak in a restaurant that is not cooked the way you ordered it, because the waiter won't like you if you send it back.
  11. Observe whether you say things you don't mean just to avoid being disliked.
  12. Notice whether you spread bad news about deaths, divorce, muggings and the like, and enjoy the feeling of being noticed.
  13. Observe whether you get permission to speak, or to make a purchase, or to do anything, from a significant other in your life, because you fear the displeasure of that person.
  14. Notice whether you apologize for yourself at every turn—the excessive "I'm sorry's" that are designed to have others forgive you and approve of you all of the time.
  15. Observe whether you behave in nonconforming ways for the purpose of gaining attention, which is the same neurosis as conforming for the sake of external approval (wearing tennis shoes with your tuxedo, eating mashed potatoes and wanting to be noticed).
  16. Notice whether you are pathologically late for all occasions to be seen and distinguished.
  17. Observe whether you try to impress others with your knowledge of something that you know nothing about by "faking it."
  18. Notice whether you beg for compliments by setting yourself up for approval, and then feel bad when they don't come.
  19. Observe whether you are unhappy about someone you respect having a contrary point of view, and expressing it to you.

### Lesson 22: Recognize the Neurotic Payoffs That Keep You Hooked on Approval-Seeking
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Understand that placing responsibility for your feelings on others means they, not you, are responsible for how you feel.
  2. Recognize that if they are responsible for how you feel because of withholding their approval, then any change in you is also impossible, since it is their fault that you feel the way you do. Thus approval-seeking helps you avoid changing.
  3. Recognize that as long as they are responsible and you can't change, you don't have to take any risks. Hanging onto approval-seeking helps you conveniently avoid any risk-taking activities in your life.
  4. Recognize that approval-seeking reinforces a poor self-image and encourages self-pity and do-nothingism.
  5. Recognize that it reinforces the idea that others must take care of you, and therefore you can revert to the child in you and be coddled, protected—and manipulated.
  6. Recognize that blaming others for what you are feeling creates a scapegoating effect for everything you don't like in your life.
  7. Recognize that deluding yourself that you are liked by those others you've made more important than yourself lets you feel outwardly comfortable even though there is discontent seething inside of you.
  8. Recognize that taking solace in the fact that others notice you gives you something to boast about to other approval-seeking friends.
  9. Recognize that fitting into the culture which applauds such behavior wins you the favor of the many.

### Lesson 23: Understand the Historical Antecedents of Your Approval-Seeking
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Understand that the need for approval is based on a single assumption: "Don't trust yourself—check it out with someone else first."
  2. Recognize that our culture reinforces approval-seeking behavior as a standard of life.
  3. Recognize that independent thinking is not only unconventional, it is the enemy of the very institutions that constitute the bulwark of our society.
  4. Understand that "Don't swear by yourself" is the essence of the need for tribute—and the backbone of our culture.
  5. Recognize that any steps in the direction of self-approval and independence of the good opinion of others are movements away from others' control, and get labeled as selfish, uncaring, inconsiderate and the like, in an effort to keep you dependent.

### Lesson 24: Recognize Early Family Approval-Seeking Messages
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Understand that young children truly do need acceptance from significant adults in their formative years, but approval should not be contingent upon being proper.
  2. Recognize that a child should never have to get a parent's sanction for everything he says, thinks, feels, or does.
  3. Self-reliance can be taught in the crib, and approval-seeking ought not be confused with love-seeking.
  4. Notice when you were taught to check it out with Mommy or Daddy rather than trusting your own judgment.
  5. Recognize that parents who treat children as possessions rather than helping them think for themselves, solve their own problems and develop trust in themselves, are nurturing dependence and the need for approval.
- **Examples from book:** The exchange: "You can wear anything you like." "How do you like this, Mommy?" "No, no honey! Stripes and polka dots don't go together! Go back and change either the blouse or the slacks so that they match." One week later: "What should I wear, Mommy?" "I've told you, wear whatever you like. Why do you always ask me?" The child at the grocery store asking her mom whether she would like a piece of candy, having learned to check out everything with her parents including whether she wants something or not. The toilet training protest: "You can tell me what to eat, what to wear, who to play with, when to sleep, when to come in, where to put my toys, and even what to think. But I'll do this, when I am ready."

### Lesson 25: Recognize School Approval-Seeking Messages
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize that school is an institution designed expressly to instill approval-seeking thinking and behavior.
  2. Notice how you were taught to ask permission to do everything, never bank on your own judgment, and if you incur the teacher's wrath, you were expected to feel guilty for months.
  3. Recognize that your report card was a message to your parents telling them how much approval you had won.
  4. Notice that students who are independent, full of self-love, not susceptible to guilt and worry, are systematically labeled troublemakers.
  5. Recognize that if you gain the acclamation of the staff, behave in the ways that they dictate, study the curriculum laid out in front of you, you'll emerge successful with a strong need for approval, since self-reliance has been discouraged at virtually every turn.
  6. Notice how by graduation, after twelve straight years of being told how to think and what to think, you covet approval and learn that gaining the sanction of others is tantamount to being successful and happy.
  7. Recognize that when given freedom in college, the approval-seeker panics and asks: "But how many papers do we have to do?" "When are they due?" "Do you want it typed?" The student has been trained to do it for someone else, to please the professor, and to measure up to someone else's standards. He is terrified of thinking for himself.

### Lesson 26: Recognize Institutional Approval-Seeking Messages
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Recognize that organized religion appeals to approval-seeking needs by teaching you to behave because someone has told you to and because you will be punished if you don't, not because you know it to be the right behavior for you.
  2. Recognize that using yourself as a guide and not needing the approval of an outside force is the most religious experience you can have.
  3. Recognize that the government uses approval-seeking as a motivator for conformity, telling you "Don't trust yourself. You haven't got the skills and wherewithal to function alone. We'll take care of you."
  4. Recognize that there are more rules on the books than there are people to disobey them, and your rule-makers are often folks who insist that they know what is good for you more conclusively than you do yourself.

### Lesson 27: Recognize Approval-Seeking Messages in Popular Culture
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Be aware that songs are filled with lyrical messages of approval-seeking, particularly popular songs of the past three decades.
  2. Notice television commercials that reinforce the notion that other peoples' beliefs are more important than your own.
  3. Recognize that advertisers capitalize on your need for acceptance by creating little sketches that send out the right messages to manipulate you into buying their products.
- **Examples from book:** Song titles that send approval-seeking signals: "I can't live, if living is without you," "You make me so very happy," "You make me feel like a natural woman," "You're nobody till somebody cares," "It all depends on you," "You make me feel brand new," "As long as he needs me," "If you go away," "People who need people," "You are the sunshine of my life," "No one else can make me feel the colors that you bring," "Without you, I'm nothing." Commercial skits: friends disapproving of odors in your house; a wife shriveling in shame because a waitress notices ring around the collar; a woman shrinking in fear as she contemplates how her friends will view her if she has baggy pantyhose: "I just couldn't stand it if they thought something bad about me. I must have their approval, so I'll buy this brand over that one." Mouthwash, toothpaste, deodorant and special spray advertisements filled with psychological messages that you must have approval, and the way to get it is to purchase this particular item.

### Lesson 28: Coax Approval-Seeking Downstairs a Step at a Time
- **Actionable Steps:**
  1. Mark Twain gives a cogent description of how to break a habitual pattern such as approval-seeking: "Habit is habit, and not to be flung out of the window by any man, but coaxed downstairs a step at a time."
  2. Work at eliminating approval-seeking gradually, step by step.
  3. You can vow until you're orange in the face that you can handle disfavor and that you are not going to demand tribute from everyone, but until you are faced with the circumstances of contention you won't know how you are doing.
  4. It will require a great deal of practice, but it is worth every bit of effort you put into it.
