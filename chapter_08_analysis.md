# Chapter 8 Analysis: The Social Currency of Shared Ideas & Messages That Go Viral

## Chapter Title
**Lecture 8: The Social Currency of Shared Ideas** and **Lecture 9: Messages That Go Viral**

## Actionable Lessons

### Lesson 1: Apply the STEPPS Framework to Understand Why Things Go Viral
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Review the six key drivers of social transmission: **Social Currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical Value, Stories** (STEPPS).
2. Before creating or sharing any content, evaluate it against each of these six factors.
3. Identify which STEPPS factors are weak in your content.
4. Strengthen weak areas by incorporating elements that align with each driver.
5. Test content against all six factors to increase the likelihood of sharing and transmission.

---

### Lesson 2: Leverage Social Currency to Increase Sharing
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Make your audience feel like **insiders** by sharing exclusive, secret, or limited information.
2. Design campaigns that make people look smart, knowledgeable, or in the know when they share.
3. Find the **inner remarkability** in your product, idea, or content—what makes it surprising, novel, or interesting.
4. Emphasize concrete demonstrations over abstract claims (show, don't tell).
5. Ensure shared content signals a desirable identity (e.g., good parent, industry expert, early adopter).

**Example:** LinkedIn sent emails to subscribers noting they had one of the top 5 or 10 percent of profiles. Recipients felt special and shared the email to look good socially.

**Example:** Blendtec's CEO filmed himself destroying iPhones, golf balls, and BIC lighters in blenders. The videos got over 200 million views and increased sales by over 700 percent by finding inner remarkability in a mundane product.

---

### Lesson 3: Use Triggers to Keep Ideas Top of Mind
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Identify environmental cues or contexts that are naturally associated with your idea or product.
2. Name your product, campaign, or idea after something that occurs frequently in people's environments (e.g., days of the week, common objects, daily routines).
3. Ensure your trigger is **top of mind**—if people are thinking about it, they are more likely to talk about it.
4. Pair your message with existing triggers so you don't have to remind people it exists.

**Example:** Rebecca Black's song "Friday" was deemed the worst song ever, yet it went viral with over 300 million views. Google searches for "Rebecca Black" spiked every Friday—the song's name itself served as a weekly trigger.

**Example:** Saying "peanut butter" naturally triggers thoughts of "jelly." Peanut butter acts as an advertisement for jelly without any additional effort.

---

### Lesson 4: Design Content That Evokes Emotion to Drive Sharing
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Identify the **primary emotion** you want to evoke (awe, inspiration, amusement, excitement, anger, or anxiety).
2. Avoid emotions that reduce sharing, such as **sadness** (sadder articles were 16 percent less likely to be shared).
3. Focus on emotions that expand the frame of reference or create a sense of wonder (e.g., awe from science, discovery, or innovation).
4. Pair emotional content with opportunities to share, making it easy for people to pass along their feelings.
5. Test whether the emotion you evoke creates a desire to connect with others through sharing.

**Example:** New York Times science articles made the most-emailed list more often than politics, fashion, or business news because they frequently evoked **awe**—the sense of wonder from discoveries and innovations.

**Example:** Sharing a promoted story helps people celebrate good news; sharing a layoff story helps them vent frustration. Talking about emotions deepens social connections.

---

### Lesson 5: Apply the Principle of Public Visibility to Drive Imitation
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Make your product, idea, or behavior **visible and observable** to others.
2. Design products or campaigns that can be seen in public settings (e.g., distinctive clothing, visible logos, shareable status updates).
3. Avoid relying on private or invisible behaviors/products (like socks or toilet paper) unless you can make them visible.
4. Leverage **social proof**: when people see others using or endorsing something, they are more likely to imitate.
5. Use popularity signals (e.g., "bestseller," "most viewed," "trending") to signal that others are already engaging.

**Example:** In a foreign city, people choose restaurants that are full because they assume a crowded restaurant must be good—this is social proof in action.

---

### Lesson 6: Provide Practical Value to Encourage Sharing
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Identify information that is genuinely **useful** to your audience (how-to guides, tips, reviews, hacks).
2. Package practical information in a format that is easy to consume and share.
3. Focus on content that helps people save money, save time, or solve problems.
4. People share useful information both to look good and to genuinely help others.

**Example:** Articles about beating high airfares, restaurant reviews, and college admissions tips were more likely to be shared because they provided practical value.

---

### Lesson 7: Embed Messages Within Stories for Natural Transmission
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Wrap your key message, product, or idea inside a compelling narrative or story.
2. Use **idle chatter** or everyday conversation as a vehicle for your message.
3. Ensure the story is memorable and easy to retell.
4. Make the underlying message feel organic rather than like a forced advertisement.
5. Leverage the fact that people naturally share stories as part of social interaction.

## Key Concepts and Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Social Currency** | The value that shared information provides to the sharer's identity—making them look smart, interesting, knowledgeable, or in the know. |
| **Inner Remarkability** | The surprising, novel, or interesting quality inherent in something that makes people want to talk about it. Even mundane products can have inner remarkability. |
| **Triggers** | Environmental cues or reminders that prompt people to think about, talk about, and share a product, idea, or story. |
| **Top of Mind → Tip of Tongue** | The psychological principle that if something is top of mind, it is much more likely to be shared, even if it isn't inherently interesting. |
| **Emotion** | Feelings that drive sharing behavior; the principle that "when we care, we share." |
| **Awe** | The sense of wonder and amazement that occurs when someone is inspired by great knowledge, beauty, or might; the emotion most strongly correlated with sharing. |
| **Public** | The idea that if something is easier to see, it is easier to imitate; based on the psychological principle of social proof. |
| **Social Proof** | Using others' behavior as a signal of information—if many people are doing something, it must be good or correct. |
| **Practical Value** | The usefulness of information; the more helpful something is, the more likely people are to share it. |
| **Stories** | Information that travels under the guise of idle chatter; narratives that naturally carry messages through social interaction. |
| **STEPPS** | The acronym for the six key drivers of social transmission: Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories. |
| **Viral Transmission** | The spread of information, products, or ideas from one person to another, regardless of whether it generates a long chain in a population. |

## Examples from the Text

1. **LinkedIn Profile Emails:** LinkedIn identified top 5–10 percent profiles and emailed subscribers to notify them. Recipients felt like insiders and shared the email widely, driving growth.
2. **Blendtec "Will It Blend?" Videos:** The CEO blended iPhones, golf balls, and lighters, filming the results. Over 200 million views and 700 percent sales increase by making a mundane blender remarkable.
3. **Rebecca Black's "Friday":** Despite being widely considered a bad song, it achieved over 300 million views. Every Friday, the song's name served as a trigger, spiking searches and shares.
4. **Peanut Butter and Jelly:** The word "peanut butter" triggers thoughts of "jelly" without any advertising—demonstrating how one stimulus can advertise another.
5. **New York Times Science Articles:** Science articles made the most-emailed list more often than politics or business because they evoked awe—wonder and amazement from discoveries.
6. **Sad Articles Reduce Sharing:** Articles evoking sadness were 16 percent less likely to be shared, while anger and anxiety increased sharing.
7. **Restaurant Choice in a Foreign City:** People choose crowded restaurants because they use others' behavior as a signal of quality (social proof).
8. **Useful Content Sharing:** Articles about beating airfares, restaurant reviews, and college tips were shared more because of their practical utility.

## Best Practices

1. **Make People Feel Like Insiders:** Design campaigns and content that give sharers a sense of exclusivity, making them look smart or in the know.
2. **Find Inner Remarkability:** Even the most mundane products or ideas can be made remarkable. Focus on what is surprising, novel, or interesting about your offering.
3. **Show, Don't Tell:** The more concrete something is, the more likely people are to remember and share it. Use demonstrations and visual proof.
4. **Tie Ideas to Frequent Triggers:** Associate your product or message with environmental cues that occur regularly (days, objects, routines).
5. **Evoke High-Arousal Emotions:** Focus on emotions like awe, amusement, anger, or anxiety rather than sadness or contentment to drive sharing.
6. **Make It Visible:** Design products and campaigns for public visibility. If people can see it, they can imitate it.
7. **Provide Genuine Utility:** Share information that saves time, money, or effort. Practical value is a strong driver of transmission.
8. **Wrap Messages in Stories:** Embed key ideas within narratives so they travel naturally through conversation.
9. **Focus on Person-to-Person Spread:** Rather than obsessing over massive viral hits, design for the more reliable and sustainable spread from one person to another.
10. **Use Social Proof Strategically:** Signal that others are already engaging with your product or idea to leverage herd behavior.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Assuming Luck Drives Virality:** Believing that viral success is random or driven by luck (e.g., "cats") rather than applying the science of social transmission.
2. **Ignoring Mundane Products:** Assuming boring or everyday products (socks, blenders, toilet paper) cannot generate word-of-mouth. Inner remarkability can be found in anything.
3. **Relying on Negative Emotions Like Sadness:** Sadness actually reduces sharing by 16 percent. Avoid evoking sadness if your goal is transmission.
4. **Creating Invisible or Private Campaigns:** If your product or behavior cannot be seen, it cannot be imitated. Failing to make things public severely limits spread.
5. **Neglecting Triggers:** Creating great content but failing to tie it to environmental reminders ensures it will be forgotten.
6. **Focusing Only on Massive Hits:** Obsessing over rare, massive viral events rather than designing for consistent person-to-person spread.
7. **Being Abstract Instead of Concrete:** Vague or abstract claims are harder to remember and share. Failing to show rather than tell reduces memorability.
8. **Overlooking the Desire for Identity Signals:** Forgetting that people share to look good and signal desirable traits. Content that doesn't help sharers look good will spread less.
9. **Ignoring Practical Utility:** Assuming only emotional or identity-driven content spreads, while neglecting genuinely useful information that people want to pass along.
10. **Failing to Embed Messages in Stories:** Packing information into facts and figures without a narrative wrapper makes it harder for people to pass along naturally.
