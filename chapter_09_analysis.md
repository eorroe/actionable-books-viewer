## 2. Actionable Lessons

### Lesson 1: Make Private Behavior Public to Spark Social Influence
Social influence is stronger when behavior is more observable. To get people to adopt a behavior or product, make previously private actions visible to others.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Identify the behavior or action you want to encourage (e.g., donating to a cause, buying a product).
2. Determine whether this behavior is currently private or hidden.
3. Design mechanisms to make that behavior observable (e.g., public leaderboards, visible badges, shareable receipts, branded merchandise).
4. Ensure others can easily see who is engaging in the behavior and how frequently.
5. Test whether increased observability leads to higher adoption rates in your target audience.

---

### Lesson 2: Use Observability to Understand Social Proof Cues
People rely on what others are doing to decide their own actions. If others cannot see a behavior, they cannot imitate it.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Map out the decision-making context for your audience (e.g., choosing a restaurant, buying a car).
2. Identify what signals people use to infer social approval (fullness of a venue, others' purchases).
3. Make those signals visible and easy to interpret.
4. Remove barriers that prevent people from observing others' behavior.
5. Use visibility data to reinforce the popularity or quality of an option.

---

### Lesson 3: Apply the Rule of 100 When Framing Discounts
How you frame a discount dramatically changes whether people act on it.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Determine the price point of the product or service.
2. If the price is **below $100**, express the discount as a **percentage** (e.g., "25% off" instead of "$5 off").
3. If the price is **above $100**, express the discount as a **dollar amount** (e.g., "$500 off" instead of "25% off").
4. Always present the larger-sounding number first to maximize perceived value.
5. Test both framings with your audience to confirm which drives more conversions.

---

### Lesson 4: Avoid Backfiring Antidrug or Negative Campaigns
Campaigns that highlight negative behavior while trying to discourage it can inadvertently normalize that behavior.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Audit your messaging to ensure it does not accidentally broadcast that "many people are doing" the unwanted behavior.
2. Focus messaging on the **positive alternative** or the **negative consequences of the action**, rather than on peer prevalence.
3. Replace statistics about how many people engage in the bad behavior with messages about what people *should* do instead.
4. Pilot test the campaign with a small audience to check for unintended normalization effects.
5. Measure actual behavior change, not just awareness, to catch backfire effects early.

---

### Lesson 5: Wrap Information Inside a Story (Trojan Horse Story)
Raw product facts are boring; stories carry information in a memorable, shareable way.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Identify the key message or fact you want people to remember (e.g., "our bar makes great drinks").
2. Find or craft a narrative that naturally incorporates that fact without making it the explicit pitch.
3. Ensure the story creates a **curiosity gap**—something intriguing that makes people want to learn more.
4. Test whether the story is shareable in casual conversation (would someone tell this at a party?).
5. Embed the message so that discovering it feels like a reward for engaging with the story, not an advertisement.

---

### Lesson 6: Leverage Practical Value to Drive Sharing
People share useful, practical information that saves others time, money, or effort.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Create content that offers clear, actionable utility (e.g., tips, secrets, how-tos, deals).
2. Frame titles and headlines around the benefit (e.g., "10 tips for using your phone more effectively").
3. Ensure the value is immediately recognizable without requiring deep context.
4. Package deals and discounts in a way that highlights the savings clearly.
5. Ask: "Would someone share this to help a friend?" If not, refine the practical value.

---

### Lesson 7: Use Strong and Weak Ties Strategically in Social Networks
Different types of social ties provide different benefits. Strong ties care deeply; weak ties provide access to novel information.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Map your social network to distinguish **strong ties** (close friends, family) from **weak ties** (acquaintances, distant colleagues).
2. Use strong ties when you need trust, emotional support, or deep collaboration.
3. Use weak ties when you need new information, job opportunities, or introductions to new networks.
4. Recognize that network structure (who is connected to whom) shapes how ideas and influence spread.
5. Design campaigns or communications to reach both tie types for maximum spread.

## 3. Key Concepts and Definitions

| Concept | Definition |
|----------|------------|
| **Social Proof** | The psychological phenomenon where people look to others' behavior to guide their own actions, especially under uncertainty. |
| **Observability** | The degree to which a behavior or action can be seen by others. Higher observability increases social influence. |
| **Private vs. Public** | Private behaviors are hidden and hard to imitate; public behaviors are visible and easily copied. Making the private public drives contagion. |
| **Practical Value** | The usefulness or utility of information; content that saves time, money, or effort is more likely to be shared. |
| **Rule of 100** | A framing heuristic: for prices below 100, percentage discounts are more persuasive; for prices above 100, absolute dollar discounts are more persuasive. |
| **Trojan Horse Story** | A narrative that carries a hidden message or information inside an engaging story, making the message memorable and shareable. |
| **Curiosity Gap** | The space between what people know and what they want to know; stories that open a curiosity gap encourage engagement. |
| **Social Networks** | Structures composed of **nodes** (people) and **ties** (connections). The pattern of ties shapes how information spreads. |
| **Strong Ties** | Close, frequent relationships (e.g., best friends, family) that provide emotional support and trust. |
| **Weak Ties** | More distant, less frequent relationships (e.g., acquaintances) that provide access to novel information and broader networks. |

## 5. Best Practices

1. **Maximize observability** for any behavior or product you want to spread. If people cannot see it, they cannot imitate it.
2. **Make the private public** through visual cues, branding, shareable receipts, or public displays of participation.
3. **Use the Rule of 100** when communicating discounts to ensure the frame (percentage vs. dollar amount) matches the price level.
4. **Avoid negative social proof** in public health or behavior-change campaigns; focus on positive alternatives instead.
5. **Embed messages in stories** rather than stating facts directly. Stories are more memorable, social, and persuasive.
6. **Create curiosity gaps** in your stories to draw people in and make them want to learn more.
7. **Share practical value**—content that helps others saves time, money, or effort is inherently more viral.
8. **Leverage both strong and weak ties** in your network strategy: strong ties for trust, weak ties for novel information and reach.
9. **Design for network structure**—understand that who is connected to whom affects how far and fast an idea spreads.
10. **Test message framing** with real audiences to confirm which version (percentage vs. dollar) performs better for your specific product.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

1. **Ignoring observability:** Assuming people will adopt a behavior just because it is beneficial, without making it visible to others.
2. **Highlighting negative peer behavior:** Campaigns that mention how many people engage in unwanted behavior inadvertently normalize it and can backfire.
3. **Using the wrong discount frame:** Applying percentage discounts to high-price items (or dollar discounts to low-price items), making the deal feel smaller than it is.
4. **Being too factual or promotional:** Sounding like a walking advertisement ("Did you know Subway has five subs under five grams of fat?") instead of sharing an engaging story.
5. **Forgetting the curiosity gap:** Telling the whole message upfront in a story instead of leaving something intriguing that draws the audience in.
6. **Relying only on strong ties:** Assuming close contacts are the only valuable connections, while missing the novel information and broader reach available through weak ties.
7. **Treating all network ties the same:** Failing to recognize that different connection patterns (who knows whom) create different spreading dynamics.
8. **Neglecting practical value:** Creating content that is entertaining but not useful; people share useful content to help others, not just to be amused.
9. **Assuming private behaviors will spread on their own:** Donation choices, health behaviors, and other personal decisions often remain private unless mechanisms are built to make them visible.
10. **Overcomplicating the story:** burying the key message so deeply in narrative that the audience never extracts the intended takeaway.
