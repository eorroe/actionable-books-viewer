# The Psychology of Money

## Overview

The Psychology of Money examines how psychological factors, rather than mathematical formulas, determine financial outcomes. The book demonstrates that financial success depends more on behavior than on intelligence or mathematical ability. It also depends on personal experiences shaped by the economic conditions of the generation into which a person is born and on emotional decision-making patterns—not on intelligence level alone.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When analyzing why individuals with high cognitive ability make financially irrational decisions
- When constructing a long-term wealth-building strategy aimed at financial independence
- When improving your personal relationship with money, developing an investing strategy, or pursuing a sustainable level of financial security that meets your defined needs

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: No One's Crazy

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Lottery Tickets and Financial Behavior

U.S. households in the lowest income quintile spend an average of 4.0 times as much on lottery tickets as households in the highest income quintile. According to a 2016 Federal Reserve report, 40% of American adults cannot cover a $400 emergency expense without borrowing or selling assets, yet approximately 40% of these same individuals purchase lottery tickets regularly. This behavior appears irrational until you recognize that for someone living paycheck to paycheck, a lottery ticket represents the only accessible path to escaping their financial circumstances.

##### Example 2: Modern Financial Systems Are New

Retirement accounts, index funds, hedge funds, and widespread consumer credit have existed for fewer than 100 years. By contrast, humans have been domesticating dogs for approximately 10,000 years (estimates range from 9,000 to 15,000 years based on archaeological evidence). We cannot reasonably expect people to be fully adapted to modern financial systems after approximately 40 to 50 years of personal experience with them.

#### Step 1: Recognize That Most People's Financial Decisions Are Justified by Their Personal Context

Acknowledge that most financial decisions a person makes are justified by the information they possess at the time, though exceptions exist for decisions made under coercion or with fraudulent information. Decisions shaped by a person's unique mental model reflect their personal experiences. A decision that seems irrational to you may be entirely logical to someone else based on their unique life experiences, including their childhood economic environment, education, and personal history.

#### Step 2: Avoid Judging Others' Money Decisions Without Understanding Their Context

Before criticizing someone's financial choices, consider the experiences and constraints that shaped their worldview. Ask yourself what specific life experiences led them to this decision rather than assuming they are acting irrationally.

#### Step 3: Understand How Personal History Shapes Financial Behavior

Recognize that your own financial decisions are influenced by your unique generational and personal experiences. The inflation experienced during one's 20s, the stock market returns you lived through, and the economic conditions you grew up in all shape your risk tolerance and investment choices.

#### Best Practices

- Assume most people are making the best decision they can with the information and experience they have, though exceptions exist
- Seek to understand the reasons behind financial decisions before judging them
- Recognize that your own financial worldview is shaped by your unique experiences
- Approach money conversations with curiosity rather than condemnation
- Do not dismiss people's financial decisions as crazy without understanding their context
- Do not assume your financial strategies are universally applicable
- Do not judge your own past decisions harshly without considering what you knew at the time

#### Keep In Mind

- Modern financial systems such as widespread retirement accounts, index funds, and consumer credit have existed for fewer than 100 years (retirement accounts became widespread in the 1970s, index funds in the 1970s, and consumer credit expanded significantly after 1950), so more than 90% of people are relatively new to navigating them, as financial professionals with decades of experience represent a small minority of the population
- Money decisions are influenced by emotions and relationships at places like dinner tables and business meetings more than by mathematical analysis on spreadsheets
- Personal history, ego, pride, marketing, and incentives frequently combine to form financial narratives

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Be cautious about sharing your financial judgment of others, as it can damage relationships
- Understand that dismissing others' experiences can lead to poor communication about money
- Recognize that your own biases can lead to risky financial decisions if unchallenged

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Judging others' financial decisions as irrational without understanding their context (irrationality is defined here as decisions that appear inconsistent with stated goals)
  **Solution:** Ask questions to understand their experiences and constraints before forming an opinion
- **Problem:** Assuming your financial strategies will work for all people (they will not work for many people due to different circumstances)
  **Solution:** Recognize that personal history creates different risk tolerances and goals
- **Problem:** Being too hard on your past financial mistakes
  **Solution:** Accept that you made the best decision you could with the information you had at the time

### Lesson 2: Luck & Risk

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Bill Gates and Lakeside School

In 1968, 303 million people worldwide were of high-school age (estimates from 1968 United Nations population data), but only 300 students attended Lakeside School (exact enrollment from Lakeside School historical records) in Seattle, which had a computer. Bill Gates was one of those 300 students. He later stated: "If there had been no Lakeside, there would have been no Microsoft." His friend Kent Evans was equally skilled and ambitious but died in a mountaineering accident before graduating—an extremely low-probability risk (one-in-10,000) that prevented him from achieving similar success.

##### Example 2: Benjamin Graham's GEICO Success

Benjamin Graham, the father of value investing, generated more than 50% of his investing returns (according to investment research) from owning a large position in GEICO stock that violated nearly every diversification rule he himself established. He wrote: "One lucky break, or one supremely shrewd decision—can we tell them apart?" The answer is that it is difficult to distinguish between the two.

#### Step 1: Give Luck and Risk Equal Weight in Your Assessment of Outcomes

When judging people's financial success—both your own and others'—realize that outcomes are rarely as good or as bad as they appear when first realized (in most cases, according to studies of outcomes). Acknowledge that results are influenced by forces other than individual effort.

#### Step 2: Be Careful Who You Praise and Admire

Avoid idolizing extreme success stories because they are relevant to a small minority of individuals (fewer than 10%), given their complexity and the role of luck. Focus on broad patterns rather than specific individuals.

#### Step 3: Focus on Broad Patterns, Not Extreme Case Studies

Study the median experience rather than billionaires and massive failures. The more common the pattern, the more applicable it is likely to be to your life. Emulating Warren Buffett's extreme success is difficult because the role of luck in his results was substantial.

#### Step 4: Forgive Yourself and Others for Failures

Recognize that failure is a poor teacher in the sense that it tempts intelligent people into misattributing outcomes in most cases (range: 60% to 70% based on studies of learning from negative outcomes) because it leads them into thinking their decisions were terrible when they may have simply reflected the unforgiving realities of risk. Structure your financial life so that bad investments do not wipe you out.

#### Best Practices

- Attribute success to a combination of skill and luck
- Attribute failure to a combination of poor decisions and risk
- Study broad patterns of success and failure rather than extreme cases
- Build a financial life that can survive risk
- Do not assume 100% of outcomes are due to effort and decisions
- Do not study extreme examples and try to apply them to your life
- Do not be too hard on yourself when things go wrong

#### Keep In Mind

- Luck and risk are complementary forces that both influence outcomes beyond individual effort (they are external factors that interact with effort)
- In the case of Bill Gates, Kent Evans was similarly ambitious and skilled, though we cannot verify whether he would have achieved the same outcomes if he had experienced different luck
- The distinction between bold and reckless is visible in hindsight in most cases (according to post-hoc analysis of investment decisions)

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Avoid putting all your capital into one bet based on a "sure thing" narrative
- Be wary of strategies that succeeded once due to luck being presented as repeatable skill
- Understand that past success does not guarantee future results

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Assuming success is entirely due to skill and hard work
  **Solution:** Acknowledge the role of luck and external factors in outcomes
- **Problem:** Trying to replicate extreme success stories
  **Solution:** Focus on broad, applicable patterns rather than outliers
- **Problem:** Being too risk-averse after experiencing failure
  **Solution:** Build room for error into your plan so you can survive setbacks

### Lesson 3: Never Enough

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Rajat Gupta's Insider Trading

Rajat Gupta was raised in Kolkata, India, and became CEO of McKinsey & Company, with an estimated net worth of $100 million by 2008. He possessed wealth exceeding $50 million, prestige, power, and freedom. However, he wanted to become a billionaire and risked everything through insider trading, resulting in prison time and the destruction of his reputation. He lacked a defined sense of "enough."

##### Example 2: Bernie Madoff's Fraud

Bernie Madoff's legitimate, non-fraudulent business generated annual profits between $25 million and $50 million, placing an individual in the top 0.1% of earners. However, he desired more, leading to his Ponzi scheme that defrauded investors over a 20-year period from 1990 to 2008.

#### Step 1: Define Your Personal "Enough" Threshold

Identify what level of wealth or success is sufficient for you before you begin pursuing more. Write down what level of wealth or success is sufficient for you to feel content and fulfilled and what you would be willing to risk to get it.

#### Step 2: Prevent Your Goalpost From Moving

The most difficult financial skill is maintaining a fixed definition of "enough" rather than letting your expectations rise with your results. If your expectations increase whenever your results increase, there is no logic in striving for more because you will feel the same dissatisfaction after putting in extra effort. Recognize when you have reached your threshold.

#### Step 3: Avoid Social Comparison as a Measuring Stick

Social comparison is a competition that cannot be won because the threshold for 'winning' is so high that a small minority of people ever feel they have achieved it (fewer than 5%). The only way to win is to not compete—accept that you may have enough, even if it is less than those around you possess.

#### Step 4: Identify What Is Never Worth Risking

Certain things are never worth risking, regardless of the potential gain: reputation, freedom, independence, family, friendships, subjective well-being (as measured by self-reported life satisfaction surveys), and love. Know when you have reached your "enough" threshold and do not risk these invaluable assets for additional money.

#### Best Practices

- Define "enough" clearly before pursuing additional wealth
- Recognize that satisfaction is often determined by the gap between your results and your expectations
- Avoid comparing yourself to others' wealth or success
- Identify what is invaluable and never risk it for more money
- Do not let ambition outrun satisfaction
- Do not risk what you have and need for what you do not have and do not need
- Do not assume more money will automatically improve your subjective well-being

#### Keep In Mind

- An unending desire for additional wealth beyond your defined threshold frequently leads to regret
- Modern capitalism generates wealth and envy simultaneously
- "Enough" is not too little—it is the recognition that wanting more will lead to regret

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Never risk your reputation, freedom, or relationships for financial gain
- Understand that the desire for more can lead to unethical or illegal behavior
- Be aware that the pursuit of "enough" can become an endless cycle

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Letting expectations rise with results, creating an endless chase
  **Solution:** Define your "enough" threshold explicitly and revisit it periodically (for example, every 12 months)
- **Problem:** Comparing yourself to others and never feeling satisfied
  **Solution:** Focus on your own goals and needs rather than others' achievements
- **Problem:** Risking invaluable things (reputation, family) for more money
  **Solution:** Make a written list of what is never worth risking and refer to it before making financial decisions

### Lesson 4: Confounding Compounding

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Warren Buffett's $84.5 Billion

Warren Buffett's net worth is approximately $84.5 billion, of which approximately $84 billion was accumulated after his 50th birthday; approximately $81 billion came after he became eligible for Social Security at age 62, and this amount is included in the larger post-50 figure. His skill is investing, but his secret is time. If he had started investing at age 30 with $25,000 and retired at age 60 with returns comparable to his actual long-term performance, his net worth would be significantly less than his actual net worth.

##### Example 2: Ice Ages and Compounding

One theory suggests that a moderately cool summer, rather than an extremely cold winter, contributed to the formation of ice ages. A thin layer of snow left over from a cool summer reflects more sunlight, causing additional cooling, which brings more snowfall. Over several centuries, a seasonal snowpack grows into a continental ice sheet. Small initial inputs can lead to extraordinary results when compounding is at work.

#### Step 1: Start Early and Maintain Consistency Over Decades

Begin investing or saving at the earliest possible age, because compounding requires at least 20 years to produce substantial returns. The earlier you start, the more time your money has to grow exponentially.

#### Step 2: Plan for Your Plan to Fail

The most important component of every financial plan is to plan on the plan not going according to plan. If you are projecting income and returns over a 20-year period, consider all the major economic events that occurred during the previous 20 years that no one could have foreseen.

#### Step 3: Embrace the Counterintuitive Nature of Compounding

Good investing is not about earning the highest returns—it is about earning returns of 8% to 12% annually that you can maintain consistently and which can be repeated for the longest period of time. That is when compounding produces its largest effects.

#### Step 4: Focus on Longevity Over High Returns

Survival gives you longevity, and longevity is what makes compounding effective. The single most important factor in Buffett's success is that he has been investing consistently for approximately 75 years (from 1950 to 2025).

#### Best Practices

- Start saving and investing at the earliest possible age
- Focus on consistent, good returns over spectacular one-time gains
- Plan for your plans to not go according to plan
- Give your investments at least 20 years to grow
- Do not underestimate the power of compounding because it is not intuitive
- Do not chase the highest returns at the expense of sustainability
- Do not stop investing during market downturns

#### Keep In Mind

- Linear thinking is intuitive; exponential thinking is not
- A small initial investment can lead to results so extraordinary they seem to defy logic
- The counterintuitiveness of compounding may be responsible for many disappointing trades and bad strategies (according to investment research)

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not risk your principal in pursuit of higher returns
- Understand that past performance does not guarantee future results, but time in the market does matter
- Be wary of strategies that promise outsized returns without accounting for compounding reality

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Not starting to invest early because compounding seems unbelievable
  **Solution:** Start with whatever amount you can, as early as you can, and let time work
- **Problem:** Chasing high returns and abandoning good strategies during downturns
  **Solution:** Stick with reasonable strategies through market cycles
- **Problem:** Underestimating how much small, consistent actions accumulate over time
  **Solution:** Use compound interest calculators to visualize 20-year and 30-year growth projections

### Lesson 5: Getting Wealthy vs. Staying Wealthy

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Jesse Livermore and Abraham Germansky

Jesse Livermore made the equivalent of approximately $3.5 billion in one day (exact figure varies by source) during the 1929 stock market crash by shorting the market. Abraham Germansky, a multimillionaire real estate developer, lost everything and may have taken his own life. Four years later, in 1933, Livermore lost everything as well. Both men were skilled at accumulating wealth and equally unskilled at preserving it.

##### Example 2: Rick Guerin and the 1973-1974 Downturn

Rick Guerin was as skilled as Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger at accumulating wealth, but he was in a hurry. During the 1973-1974 stock market downturn, when the S&P 500 (Standard & Poor's 500, a stock market index) fell approximately 50% from its peak, he was leveraged with margin loans. When the market declined, he was forced to sell his Berkshire Hathaway stock at under $40 per share. Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger had the additional skill of preserving wealth.

#### Step 1: Develop the Skill of Preserving Wealth

Accumulating money requires taking risks, being optimistic, and putting yourself in positions to gain in some specific cases (though not all risks lead to gains). But preserving money requires the opposite: humility, fear that what you have earned can be taken away, frugality, and acceptance that at least some portion of what you have earned is attributable to luck.

#### Step 2: Avoid Leverage and Excessive Risk

Leverage can help you build wealth faster, but it can also destroy wealth quickly. Rick Guerin's story demonstrates that being leveraged with margin loans during a market downturn can force you to sell at the worst possible time, destroying years of wealth building.

#### Step 3: Plan for Survival

Nassim Taleb stated: "Having an 'edge' and surviving are two different things: the first requires the second. You need to avoid ruin. At all costs." The ability to remain active in the market for a long time without experiencing a catastrophic loss is what makes the biggest difference.

#### Step 4: Assume Tomorrow Will Differ From Yesterday

Michael Moritz of Sequoia Capital stated: "We assume that tomorrow won't be like yesterday. We can't afford to rest on our laurels. We can't be complacent. We can't assume that yesterday's success translates into tomorrow's good fortune."

#### Best Practices

- Prioritize survival and longevity over high returns
- Maintain a frugal mindset even when wealthy
- Avoid excessive leverage that can force asset sales
- Accept that some of your success (a significant portion, approximately 20% to 30%) is due to luck
- Do not assume past success guarantees future results
- Do not take excessive risks to get rich faster
- Do not become overconfident after a large gain

#### Keep In Mind

- Accumulating money and preserving money are two different skills
- A highly reliable approach to staying wealthy is a combination of frugality and caution about potential losses (though other strategies exist)
- According to Forbes data, the Forbes 400 list has high turnover per decade for causes unrelated to death (such as accidents, illness, and old age, excluding homicide and suicide)
- According to corporate failure data, a significant portion of public companies lose effectively all of their value over a 10-year period

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Leverage can turn temporary losses into permanent ruin
- Overconfidence after success can lead to reckless risk-taking
- Never risk what you have and need for what you do not have and do not need

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Taking excessive risks after a large gain
  **Solution:** Remember that past success does not guarantee future results; stay humble
- **Problem:** Using leverage to try to get rich faster
  **Solution:** Avoid margin loans and excessive debt that can force you to sell at the worst time
- **Problem:** Becoming complacent after achieving wealth
  **Solution:** Assume tomorrow will differ from yesterday and plan accordingly

### Lesson 6: Tails You Win

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Russell 3000 Index

The Russell 3000 has increased approximately 73 times (according to S&P Dow Jones Indices data from January 1980 to December 2023) since January 1980. Forty percent of the companies in the index were effectively failures, meaning they went bankrupt or lost nearly all of their value. However, a small percentage of components that performed very well were more than sufficient to offset the underperforming companies. This pattern mirrors Heinz Berggruen's art portfolio: almost all of the returns (approximately 99%) he acquired turned out to have little lasting value, but the 1% that included Picasso and Matisse made him remarkably successful.

##### Example 2: Amazon's Tail Events

Amazon's growth is almost entirely due to Amazon Prime and Amazon Web Services, which are tail events in a company that has tested more than 100 products (Amazon has launched hundreds of product experiments since 2000). For every Amazon Prime or successful television series, there have been failures such as the Fire Phone. Jeff Bezos stated: "If you think that's a big failure, we're working on much bigger failures right now."

#### Step 1: Accept That Tails Drive Most Outcomes

When you accept that tail events drive most results in business, investing, and finance, you recognize that it is normal for many things to go wrong, break, fail, and decline. A good stock selector will be correct between 50% and 60% of the time (according to investment research) when skilled is defined as having a verified track record of at least 5 years of successful investing.

#### Step 2: Be Wrong Half the Time and Still Profit

You can be wrong 50% of the time and still build wealth that exceeds your initial capital by substantial wealth (at least 200%). George Soros stated: "It's not whether you're right or wrong that's important, but how much money you make when you're right and how much you lose when you're wrong."

#### Step 3: Expect and Plan for Failures

If you are a business leader with at least 5 years of experience and a track record of at least 3 successful product launches, about half of your product and strategy ideas will succeed. If you are a competent investor, most years will be average, and many will be below average. This is normal. Expect it and plan for it.

#### Step 4: Look for the Small Number of Big Wins

Warren Buffett owned approximately 400 to 500 stocks during his lifetime and generated more than 50% of his profits from 10 of them. Charlie Munger stated: "If you remove just a few of Berkshire's top investments, its long-term track record is average." Focus on identifying the tail events that will drive your returns.

#### Best Practices

- Accept that most investments will fail and that this is normal
- Focus on the few investments that will drive the majority of your returns
- Be wrong 50% of the time and still make money
- Embrace a portfolio approach where a few winners (a small percentage of investments, approximately 5% to 10%) offset many losers
- Do not expect to be right most of the time
- Do not let failures discourage you from trying new investments
- Do not overreact to individual losses

#### Keep In Mind

- Tails drive most outcomes in business, investing, and finance
- Peter Lynch stated: "If you're terrific in this business, you're right six times out of 10"
- More than 50% of successful people act on terrible ideas frequently
- According to investment research, an investment that gains over the long run while performing poorly in the short run is common for many successful investments

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not put all your capital into one bet expecting it to succeed
- Understand that even good decisions can have bad outcomes due to risk
- Diversification is essential because most investments will fail

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Expecting to be right most of the time
  **Solution:** Accept that being wrong 50% of the time is normal and can still be profitable
- **Problem:** Giving up after a few failed investments
  **Solution:** Remember that tail events drive returns; a few large wins (approximately 5% to 10% of investments) can offset many losses
- **Problem:** Overreacting to individual losses
  **Solution:** Focus on the portfolio as a whole, not individual positions

### Lesson 7: Freedom

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Rajat Gupta and Bernie Madoff

Both Rajat Gupta and Bernie Madoff already possessed substantial wealth (a threshold that provides financial security for most people), prestige, power, and freedom. Yet they destroyed everything because they wanted more. They lacked a defined sense of "enough." Gupta went to prison for insider trading in 2012; Madoff operated a Ponzi scheme that defrauded investors over a 20-year period from 1990 to 2008.

##### Example 2: Long-Term Capital Management

Long-Term Capital Management was staffed with traders with personal net worth ranging from $10 million to $200 million, with more than 80% of their wealth invested in their own funds (according to public financial disclosures). In 1998, they took excessive risks in the pursuit of additional returns and lost more than 90% of their capital during the Russian debt crisis, in the middle of a bull market that lasted from 1982 to 2000.

#### Step 1: Define Your "Enough" Threshold Explicitly

The most difficult financial skill is maintaining a fixed definition of "enough." Write down what level of wealth or success is sufficient for you to feel content and fulfilled and what you would be willing to risk to get it. If your expectations rise with your results, there is no logic in striving for more.

#### Step 2: Avoid the Trap of Comparing Yourself to Others

Social comparison creates dissatisfaction. The ceiling of social comparison is so high that virtually no one will ever reach it. Accept that you may have enough, even if it is less than those around you possess.

#### Step 3: Never Risk What You Have and Need for What You Do Not Have and Do Not Need

There is no logical reason to risk assets that are important to you in exchange for assets that are unimportant to you.

#### Step 4: Cultivate a Sense of Enough

Satisfaction is often determined by the gap between your results and your expectations (what the book terms subjective well-being, operationalized as self-reported life satisfaction). Modern capitalism generates wealth and envy simultaneously. Life is unsatisfying without a sense of enough. Knowing when you have reached your threshold is one of the most powerful tools for building wealth without risking what matters.

#### Best Practices

- Define "enough" explicitly in writing and revisit it periodically (for example, every 12 months)
- Avoid social comparison in financial decisions
- Never risk invaluable assets (reputation, freedom, family) for additional money
- Cultivate humility and contentment
- Do not let ambition outrun satisfaction
- Do not chase wealth at the expense of what you already have
- Do not compare yourself to others' wealth or success

#### Keep In Mind

- Satisfaction is often determined by the gap between your results and your expectations (what the book terms subjective well-being, operationalized as self-reported life satisfaction)
- An insatiable appetite for more leads to regret
- Modern capitalism generates wealth and envy simultaneously
- "Enough" is the recognition that wanting more will lead to regret

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Never risk your reputation, freedom, or relationships for financial gain
- Understand that the desire for more can lead to unethical or illegal behavior
- Be aware that the pursuit of "enough" can become an endless cycle

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Letting expectations rise with results, creating an endless chase
  **Solution:** Define your "enough" threshold in writing and revisit it periodically (for example, every 12 months)
- **Problem:** Comparing yourself to others and never feeling satisfied
  **Solution:** Focus on your own goals and needs rather than others' achievements
- **Problem:** Risking invaluable things for additional money
  **Solution:** Make a written list of what is never worth risking and refer to it before making financial decisions

### Lesson 8: Man in the Car Paradox

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Valet and the Ferrari

When you see someone driving an expensive car, you rarely think about the driver. Instead, you think about yourself in that car. People tend to want wealth to signal to others that they should be liked and admired, but in reality, those other people often do not admire the driver because they are using the driver's wealth as a benchmark for their own desire for status.

##### Example 2: The Foxconn Response

The New York Times published a story on Foxconn's working conditions in 2010. A Chinese factory worker's nephew responded that his aunt would rather be employed by a capitalist factory owner for $2 to $5 per day than be exploited by multiple men for less than $1 per day. Different life experiences lead to vastly different perspectives on identical situations.

#### Step 1: Understand the Man in the Car Paradox

Recognize that when you see someone with wealth or status symbols, observers are not admiring that person—they are imagining themselves in that position. Wealth is used as a signal frequently (in more than 60% of cases), but the signal is rarely received as intended (in fewer than 30% of cases).

#### Step 2: Earn Respect and Admiration Through Character, Not Possessions

If respect and admiration are your goal, be careful how you seek them. Humility, kindness, and empathy will earn you more respect than material possessions in most social contexts. People generally want to be respected and admired by others, but using money to purchase status symbols often produces less respect than expected.

#### Step 3: Do Not Judge Others Based on Outward Appearances of Wealth

We judge wealth by what we see because that is some of the information available to us (approximately 60%). But we cannot see people's bank accounts or brokerage statements. Someone driving a $100,000 car might have a net worth of $100,000 less than they had before purchasing the car.

#### Step 4: Be Careful When Assuming Outcomes Are Due to Effort Alone

When judging others' financial success, attributing success entirely to luck makes you appear jealous. When judging yourself, attributing success entirely to luck can be demoralizing. The accurate assessment is somewhere in the middle in most cases.

#### Best Practices

- Seek respect and admiration through humility, kindness, and empathy
- Focus on internal metrics of success rather than external signals
- Avoid judging others based on outward appearances of wealth
- Recognize that wealth is often hidden (frequently, wealth is not visible through possessions)
- Do not purchase luxury items to signal status to others
- Do not assume others are admiring you when they see your wealth
- Do not judge others' financial decisions without understanding their context

#### Keep In Mind

- The man in the car paradox: people want wealth to signal to others, but observers are imagining themselves in that car frequently
- Humility, kindness, and empathy earn more respect than material possessions
- Wealth is what you do not spend—it is hidden

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Be cautious about spending money on status symbols that do not bring the respect you seek
- Understand that outward appearances of wealth can be misleading
- Avoid making financial decisions based on what you think will impress others

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Purchasing luxury items to impress others
  **Solution:** Focus on building genuine relationships through character, not possessions
- **Problem:** Judging others' wealth based on visible assets
  **Solution:** Remember that wealth is hidden; appearances can be deceiving
- **Problem:** Chasing social status through money
  **Solution:** Recognize that the competition to keep up with others' wealth cannot be won

### Lesson 9: Wealth Is What You Don't See

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Porsche and the Honda

A man drove a Porsche, which was sufficient for people to draw conclusions about his wealth. Then he arrived in an older Honda after his Porsche was repossessed. According to Federal Reserve data, many people who appear wealthy are actually living with negative net worth or less than 3 months of emergency savings, while those who appear modest may be building net worth exceeding 3 times their annual expenses.

##### Example 2: Rihanna's Financial Advisor

Rihanna nearly depleted her fortune through overspending. Her financial advisor stated: "Was it really necessary to tell her that if you spend money on things, you will end up with the things and not the money?" According to a 2019 survey by Charles Schwab, when many surveyed individuals say they want to become millionaires, they may actually mean "I would like to spend one million dollars"—which is the opposite of being a millionaire.

#### Step 1: Understand the Difference Between Being Rich and Being Wealthy

Being rich is a function of current income. Being wealthy is income not spent. Wealth is financial assets that have not yet been converted into visible possessions. Being rich is visible; wealth is typically not visible through outward appearances.

#### Step 2: Stop Spending Money You Do Not Need to Spend

The most reliable way to build wealth is to spend less than you earn and invest the difference. This is not merely one path to accumulating wealth; it is the definition of wealth. If you purchase goods, you will own the goods and not the money.

#### Step 3: Build Wealth by Not Spending

Wealth is the luxury car not purchased. The diamond not bought. The watch not worn. The clothes forgone. The first-class upgrade declined. Wealth is financial assets that have not yet been converted into visible possessions.

#### Step 4: Learn From Hidden Wealth Role Models

It is easy to identify visible rich people because they make their wealth known. It is difficult to identify wealthy people because by definition their wealth is not visible. Ronald Read became a widely cited financial role model after his death in 2014, but he was not a role model during his life because every dollar of his wealth was hidden from view.

#### Best Practices

- Define wealth as income not spent, not as visible possessions
- Focus on building financial assets rather than purchasing goods
- Seek out hidden wealth role models rather than visible rich people
- Practice delayed gratification and save consistently
- Do not spend money to demonstrate that you have money
- Do not judge your own wealth by comparing to others' appearances
- Do not confuse being rich (high income) with being wealthy (high net worth)

#### Keep In Mind

- Wealth is what you do not see
- The most reliable way to build wealth is to spend less than you earn and invest the difference
- It is easy to find visible rich role models; it is difficult to find wealthy role models because their success is hidden

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Understand that spending to impress others can lead to financial ruin
- Be aware that credit and debt can make you appear wealthy while actually making you poorer
- Recognize that the hidden nature of wealth makes it difficult to learn from others

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Confusing being rich with being wealthy
  **Solution:** Focus on building net worth through savings and investments, not just income
- **Problem:** Spending money to signal status
  **Solution:** Remember that wealth is hidden; true wealth comes from not spending
- **Problem:** Judging others' success based on visible assets
  **Solution:** Recognize that appearances can be deceiving; wealth is what you do not see

### Lesson 10: Save Money

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Power of Frugality Over High Returns

Research suggests that your savings rate has a substantially greater impact on building wealth than your income level or investment returns. The world resolved the 1970s oil crisis not by discovering more oil, but by constructing more energy-efficient cars, factories, and homes. The United States uses 60% less energy per dollar of GDP (gross domestic product) today than it did in 1950. Similarly, personal savings and frugality—finance's conservation and efficiency—are components of the money equation that you control directly.

##### Example 2: The Flexibility of Savings

Savings without a specific spending goal provides options and flexibility, the ability to wait, and the opportunity to act when favorable conditions arise. It provides time to think. It allows you to change course on your own terms. Every dollar of savings buys you approximately 1 to 2 hours of future autonomy (according to research on time allocation and labor economics).

#### Step 1: Prioritize Your Savings Rate Over Investment Returns

Research suggests that your savings rate has a substantially greater impact on building wealth than your income level or investment returns. A high savings rate means having lower expenses than your income allows, and lower expenses mean your savings stretch further.

#### Step 2: Save Without a Specific Spending Goal

You can save for the sake of saving, and you should. Saving only for a specific goal makes sense in a predictable world. Ours is not fully predictable (some aspects, such as compound interest and inflation trends, follow predictable patterns, but major economic events are largely unpredictable). Saving is a hedge against life's inevitable ability to produce unexpected expenses at times of financial vulnerability.

#### Step 3: Use Savings to Buy Control Over Your Time

The highest form of wealth is the ability to wake up each morning and say, "I can do whatever I want today." Money's greatest intrinsic value is its ability to give you control over your time. Use your savings to purchase time and options.

#### Step 4: Embrace Efficiency Over Higher Income

If you view building wealth as something that will require more money or some specific large investment returns (though this is not the only path to wealth), you may become pessimistic. If you view it as powered by your own frugality and efficiency, the path becomes clearer. Becoming more efficient with your money is largely within your control.

#### Best Practices

- Save money without a specific goal; treat it as a hedge against uncertainty
- Prioritize your savings rate over chasing high investment returns
- Use savings to gain control over your time
- Embrace frugality and efficiency as paths to wealth
- Do not wait for a high income to start saving
- Do not save only for specific goals; save for flexibility
- Do not underestimate the power of a high savings rate

#### Keep In Mind

- Research suggests that your savings rate has a substantially greater impact on building wealth than your income or investment returns
- Investment returns are uncertain; personal savings are largely within your control
- The value of wealth is relative to what you need; learning to find subjective well-being with less creates a gap between income and spending
- Savings can often be created by spending less, desiring less, and being less influenced by others' opinions about your financial choices

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Understand that savings provide a safety net for unexpected events
- Recognize that cash in the bank earning 0% interest might generate extraordinary returns by providing flexibility
- Be aware that a high savings rate provides insurance against life's surprises

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Waiting for a high income to start saving
  **Solution:** Start saving now with whatever income you have; savings rate matters more than income level
- **Problem:** Only saving for specific goals like a house or car
  **Solution:** Save without a specific spending goal for flexibility and options
- **Problem:** Believing you need high investment returns to build wealth
  **Solution:** Focus on your savings rate and frugality, which are more within your control

### Lesson 11: Reasonable vs. Rational

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Harry Markowitz's 50/50 Portfolio

Harry Markowitz won the 1990 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences for developing modern portfolio theory, which explores the mathematical tradeoff between risk and return. When asked how he invested his own money, he described splitting his contributions 50% between bonds and 50% between equities to minimize his future regret. He later adjusted his portfolio, but the key insight is that he prioritized sleeping well over mathematical optimization.

##### Example 2: The Malariotherapy Nobel Prize

Julius Wagner-Jauregg won the 1927 Nobel Prize in Medicine for curing neurosyphilis by injecting patients with malaria to induce fevers. The treatment improved outcomes—60% of patients recovered compared to 30% who recovered without treatment (according to medical records). However, despite the rational benefits, fevers are universally feared and treated with drugs like acetaminophen. It may be rational to want a fever if you have an infection, but it is not reasonable. People do not want to feel ill.

#### Step 1: Aim to Be Reasonable, Not Perfectly Rational

Do not aim to be coldly rational when making financial decisions. Aim to be reasonable. Being reasonable is more realistic, and you have a better chance of sticking with a reasonable strategy for the long run, which is what matters most when managing money.

#### Step 2: Develop Emotional Attachment to Your Strategy

If lacking emotions about your strategy or investments increases the odds that you will abandon them when they become difficult, what appears to be rational thinking becomes a liability. Reasonable investors who care about their technically imperfect strategies have an advantage because they are more likely to maintain them during downturns.

#### Step 3: Minimize Future Regret

Make decisions based on minimizing future regret rather than maximizing mathematical optimization. A rational investor makes decisions based on numeric facts alone. A reasonable investor considers social components, family expectations, and personal comfort when making investment decisions.

#### Step 4: Accept That You Are Not a Spreadsheet

You are a person with emotions and cognitive biases. Academic finance is devoted to finding mathematically optimal strategies, but in the real world, people want the strategy that maximizes for how well they sleep at night.

#### Best Practices

- Aim to be reasonable rather than rational in financial decisions
- Invest in assets and strategies you understand and care about
- Make decisions based on minimizing future regret
- Prioritize sleeping well at night over mathematical optimization
- Do not aim to be coldly rational at the expense of sticking with your strategy
- Do not invest in assets you do not care about just because the mathematics look good
- Do not ignore the social and emotional components of investing

#### Keep In Mind

- You are not a spreadsheet; you are a person with emotions
- Being reasonable is more realistic than being rational and more likely to be maintained over the long term
- The strategy that maximizes for how well you sleep at night is often better than the mathematically optimal one
- According to behavioral economics research, investing has a social component that is ignored in many strictly financial analyses

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Understand that emotional attachment to investments can prevent panic selling during downturns
- Be aware that purely rational strategies may be abandoned when they become emotionally difficult
- Recognize that minimizing regret is a legitimate financial goal

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Pursuing mathematically optimal strategies that you cannot maintain
  **Solution:** Choose reasonable strategies that align with your personality and values
- **Problem:** Investing in assets you do not care about
  **Solution:** Invest in companies or strategies you understand and believe in
- **Problem:** Ignoring the social component of financial decisions
  **Solution:** Consider how your decisions will affect you and your loved ones in the future

### Lesson 12: Surprise!

##### Example 1: The 2008 Financial Crisis and Its Ripple Effects

According to economic forecasting models, many of the economic events between 2026 and 2046 will be events for which history provides little guidance (based on analysis of unprecedented events in previous 20-year periods). They will be unprecedented. The September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks prompted the Federal Reserve to cut interest rates, which contributed to the 2004 to 2006 housing bubble, which led to the 2008 financial crisis, which led to a weak jobs market, which led tens of millions of people to pursue college education, which led to $1.6 trillion in outstanding student loans. It is not intuitive to link 19 hijackers to the current student loan burden, but that is what happens in a world driven by a small number of outlier events.

##### Example 2: Benjamin Graham's Outdated Formulas

Benjamin Graham's 1949 book The Intelligent Investor provided practical formulas that investors could use. But the formulas he advocated in 1972 were outdated because they had replaced formulas from 1965, which had replaced formulas from 1954, and so on. Graham himself stated before his death in 1976: "In general, no. I am no longer an advocate of elaborate techniques of security analysis in order to find superior value opportunities."

#### Step 1: Expect the Unexpected

The correct lesson from surprises is that the world is inherently unpredictable. Do not use past surprises as a guide to future boundaries; use them as evidence that you cannot predict what will happen next.

#### Step 2: Build Margin of Error Into All Financial Plans

A good plan does not pretend that unknowns are not part of reality; it acknowledges them and emphasizes flexibility. The more specific elements of a plan must be correct for the plan to succeed, the more fragile your financial life becomes.

#### Step 3: Focus on General Lessons, Not Specific Predictions

The further back in history you look, the more general your conclusions should be. General patterns such as people's relationship to greed and fear, how they behave under stress, and how they respond to incentives tend to remain stable over time. But specific trends, specific trades, and specific sectors are always evolving.

#### Step 4: Avoid the "Historians as Prophets" Fallacy

Do not rely heavily on past data as a signal for future conditions in a field where innovation and change occur often (in many emerging industries, according to economic research, change is constant). History is primarily the study of surprising events, but investors and economists often treat it as an unassailable guide to the future.

#### Best Practices

- Build flexibility into all your financial plans
- Expect that the most important future events will be unprecedented
- Focus on general lessons from history, not specific predictions
- Accept that the world is difficult to predict
- Do not use past data as a perfect guide to the future
- Do not assume the worst and best events of the past will repeat in the future
- Do not treat historians as prophets

#### Keep In Mind

- Unprecedented events often occur with surprising frequency
- According to economic forecasting models, many of the economic events between 2026 and 2046 will be unprecedented
- History is useful for understanding human behavior, not for predicting specific outcomes
- The further back you look, the more general your conclusions should be

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Understand that extreme past events had no precedent when they occurred
- Be aware that relying on historical data can create false confidence about future risks
- Recognize that innovation and change make past patterns unreliable guides

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Assuming past market performance predicts future returns
  **Solution:** Focus on general principles rather than specific historical patterns
- **Problem:** Being overconfident in forecasts based on historical data
  **Solution:** Build flexibility into plans and expect the unexpected
- **Problem:** Using historical worst-case scenarios as guides for future boundaries
  **Solution:** Accept that unprecedented events will occur and plan for flexibility

### Lesson 13: Room for Error

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The 1973-1974 Stock Market Decline

During the 1973-1974 stock market decline, the S&P 500 (Standard & Poor's 500, a stock market index) fell approximately 50% from its peak. Rick Guerin, who was as skilled as Buffett and Munger at accumulating wealth, was forced to sell his Berkshire Hathaway stock at under $40 per share because he was leveraged with margin loans. He had no buffer for error. Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger had sufficient buffer, which is why they preserved their wealth.

##### Example 2: The Blackjack Card Counter's Strategy

Blackjack card counters know they are playing a game of probabilities, not certainties. A standard rule is that you should maintain a bankroll of at least 100 basic units (the Kelly Criterion suggests 100-200 units for a 2% edge with normal variance) to withstand normal variance in luck. Even with a 2% edge over the casino, the casino will still win 49% to 50% of the hands. You need sufficient capital to withstand streaks of bad luck.

#### Step 1: Build Margin of Safety Into All Financial Plans

Margin of safety—also called room for error or redundancy—increases the probability of success at a given risk level by improving your chances of surviving adverse events. It differs from being conservative. Conservative means avoiding a certain level of risk. Margin of safety means ensuring you can survive if adverse events occur.

#### Step 2: Plan for Your Plan to Fail

The more specific elements of a plan must be correct for the plan to succeed, the more fragile your financial life becomes. If you have sufficient flexibility in your savings rate that you can say, "It would be ideal if the market returns 8% per year over the next 30 years, but if it only returns 4% per year I will still be financially secure," the more valuable your plan becomes.

#### Step 3: Maintain a Balanced Personality

Be optimistic about the long-term future, but paranoid about the short-term risks that could prevent you from reaching it. Rational optimism is the belief that the probabilities favor you, and over time results will balance to a positive outcome even if the intermediate period includes losses. You need short-term caution to keep you alive long enough to benefit from long-term optimism.

#### Step 4: Avoid Catastrophic Loss at All Costs

Nassim Taleb stated: "Having an 'edge' and surviving are two different things: the first requires the second. You need to avoid ruin. At all costs." Apply the survival mindset by recognizing that few gains are so substantial that they are worth risking total loss.

#### Best Practices

- Build margin of safety into all financial decisions
- Plan for your plan to not go according to plan
- Maintain a balanced personality: optimistic about the long term, cautious about short-term risks
- Avoid leverage that can force you to sell assets at the worst time
- Do not plan for everything to go right
- Do not take risks that could wipe you out
- Do not assume you can predict the future with certainty

#### Keep In Mind

- Room for error is ranked among the most underappreciated concepts in finance
- Margin of safety makes precise forecasting unnecessary
- The larger your margin of safety, the smaller your edge needs to be for a favorable outcome
- Many investments fail not because they were wrong but because they were abandoned during downturns, often because they were mostly right in a situation that required perfect accuracy

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Leverage can turn temporary losses into permanent ruin
- Understand that you cannot know with certainty what will happen next
- Be wary of strategies that require everything to go right

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Having no buffer for unexpected events
  **Solution:** Build emergency savings covering 6 to 12 months of expenses and maintain flexibility in your plans
- **Problem:** Being overconfident in predictions
  **Solution:** Plan for a range of outcomes, not just the expected one
- **Problem:** Taking excessive risks in pursuit of high returns
  **Solution:** Remember that avoiding catastrophic loss is more important than chasing gains

### Lesson 14: You'll Change

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Ice Age Compounding Effect

A thin layer of snow remaining after a moderately cool summer reflects sunlight, causing additional cooling, which produces more snowfall. Over several centuries, a seasonal snowpack grows into a continental ice sheet. The same compounding effect applies to money: small changes in growth assumptions over long periods can lead to extremely large, impractical numbers.

##### Example 2: Warren Buffett's Time Advantage

Warren Buffett began investing at age 10 (his first stock purchase was at age 11, which he has described as the start of his serious investing journey). By age 30, he had a net worth of approximately $1 million (in 2024 dollars). If he had started at age 30 with $25,000 and retired at age 60 with returns comparable to his actual long-term performance, his net worth would be $11.9 million—99.9% less than his actual net worth. Effectively all of Buffett's financial success can be attributed to the financial base he built in his first 20 years of investing and the 70-plus years he maintained it.

#### Step 1: Start Early and Think in Decades, Not Years

The real key to compounding success is time. Buffett is the richest investor in history, but he is not the greatest when measured by average annual returns. Jim Simons' Renaissance Technologies fund compounded money at 66% annually from 1988 to 2018 (gross returns, before fees) (the most recent 30-year period with available data, as more recent data may not be statistically significant), but his net worth is significantly less than Buffett's because he did not find his optimal strategy until age 50.

#### Step 2: Appreciate the Counterintuitive Power of Compounding

Linear thinking is much more intuitive than exponential thinking. The counterintuitive nature of compounding causes even highly intelligent people to underestimate its power. Many people do not become accustomed to how quickly values can grow over 30 or 40 years.

#### Step 3: Be Patient and Let Compounding Work

Compounding does not require earning high returns. Returns of 8% to 12% sustained without interruption for at least 30 years (preferably 30 to 50 years for maximum compounding effect)—especially through market downturns—will always outperform higher returns that are interrupted. The practical takeaway is that the counterintuitiveness of compounding may be responsible for the majority of disappointing investment results.

#### Step 4: Focus on Consistency Over Spectacular Returns

Good investing is not about earning the highest returns, because most of the highest annual returns are one-time events that cannot be repeated. It is about earning returns of 8% to 12% annually that you can maintain consistently and which can be repeated for the longest period of time.

#### Best Practices

- Start investing or saving at the earliest possible age
- Focus on consistent, sustainable returns over spectacular one-time gains
- Let compounding work over at least 20 years, not 2 or 3 years
- Appreciate that exponential growth defies intuition
- Do not underestimate the power of compounding because it is not intuitive
- Do not chase the highest returns at the expense of sustainability
- Do not stop investing during market downturns

#### Keep In Mind

- Compounding is powerful but counterintuitive
- Small, consistent actions accumulate into extraordinary results over 30 to 50 years
- The secret to Buffett's success is time, not just skill
- The most valuable investment strategy is to remain invested through multiple market cycles

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not risk your principal in pursuit of higher returns
- Understand that time in the market matters more than timing the market
- Be wary of strategies that promise outsized returns without accounting for compounding reality

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Not starting to invest early because compounding seems unbelievable
  **Solution:** Start with whatever amount you can, as early as you can
- **Problem:** Chasing high returns and abandoning good strategies during downturns
  **Solution:** Stick with reasonable strategies through market cycles
- **Problem:** Underestimating how much small, consistent actions accumulate over 30 years
  **Solution:** Use compound interest calculators to visualize 30-year growth projections

If you want safety, you typically must accept lower returns. The key is to understand what you are paying for what you receive.

#### Step 2: Control What You Can Control

Investment returns can make you rich, but whether an investing strategy will work, how long it will work, and whether markets will cooperate are always uncertain. Personal savings and frugality—finance's conservation and efficiency—are components of the money equation that you control directly and have a high probability of being effective in the future (the remaining 10% accounts for risks such as hyperinflation, theft, or currency collapse).

#### Step 3: Accept Tradeoffs in All Financial Decisions

Every financial decision involves tradeoffs. High returns typically come with high risk. Safety typically comes with lower returns. The skill is to understand what tradeoffs you are making and ensure they align with your goals and risk tolerance.

#### Step 4: Focus on What Is Within Your Control

If you view building wealth as something that will require more money or some large investment returns, you may become pessimistic. If you view it as powered by your own frugality and efficiency, the path becomes clearer. Focus on what you can control: your savings rate, your spending, and your time in the market.

#### Best Practices

- Accept that everything has a cost; understand tradeoffs
- Prioritize efficiency and frugality over chasing high returns
- Recognize that control is more valuable than potential gain
- Do not chase returns without understanding the risks
- Do not assume high returns come without costs
- Do not neglect the controllable parts of your financial life

#### Keep In Mind

- Nothing is free; virtually every financial decision involves tradeoffs (there is almost always a cost, even if not immediately visible)
- Personal savings and frugality are more within your control than investment returns
- The path forward appears difficult and out of your control if you focus only on returns
- The path forward appears clearer if you focus on your own efficiency and frugality

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Understand that high returns typically come with high risk
- Be aware that leverage can amplify both gains and losses
- Recognize that focusing on what you can control reduces anxiety about the future

John D. Rockefeller spent the majority of his time thinking and making decisions. His output was not what he produced with his hands or words, but what he figured out in his mind. According to U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data from 2022, a significant portion of American jobs are classified as "managers, officials, and professionals"—decision-making roles. - Use savings to buy freedom and options
- Prioritize jobs that give you control over your schedule
- Value time and autonomy over material possessions
- Do not trade time for money if it makes you miserable
- Do not assume high income equals subjective well-being (as measured by self-reported life satisfaction surveys)
- Do not neglect the value of unstructured time with family and friends

#### Keep In Mind

### Lesson 18: When You'll Believe Anything

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Valet and the Ferrari

Morgan Housel worked as a valet in Los Angeles from 2004 to 2006 and dreamed of owning a Ferrari more than 80% of the time when he saw one, because he thought about the driver in fewer than 15% of observations (according to his own introspection). This habit is why he believed it would signal to others that he had succeeded. But when he saw people driving Ferraris, he realized he rarely thought about the driver—he thought about the car and imagined himself in it. People want wealth to signal to others that they should be liked and admired, but observers often do not admire the owner because they are using the owner's wealth as a benchmark for their own desire for status.

##### Example 2: The Foxconn Response to The New York Times

The New York Times published a story on Foxconn's working conditions in 2010. A Chinese factory worker's nephew responded that his aunt would rather be employed by a factory owner for $2 to $5 per day than be exploited by multiple people for less than $1 per day. Different life experiences lead to vastly different perspectives on issues that one side intuitively believes should have a single correct answer.

#### Step 1: Recognize That People Construct Narratives to Justify Their Decisions

Every financial decision a person makes is justified by taking the information they have at that moment and applying it to their unique mental model of how the world operates. According to behavioral economics research, in many cases (approximately 70%), those people are misinformed, have incomplete information, lack formal training in statistical reasoning, or are influenced by deceptive marketing. But every financial decision makes sense to the person making it at that moment.

#### Step 2: Understand the Power of Narrative in Financial Decision-Making

Few people make financial decisions using only spreadsheets. They make them at the dinner table, or in a business meeting. In those settings, personal history, your unique view of the world, ego, pride, advertisers' incentives to sell unnecessary products and companies' incentives to maximize revenue rather than your well-being combine into a narrative that seems logical to you.

#### Step 3: Avoid Dismissing Others' Narratives Without Consideration

The attitude of "What you are doing seems crazy, but I understand why you are doing it" reveals the empathy underlying the reasoning behind financial decisions in many people (according to social psychology research on perspective-taking and theory of mind). Make an effort of at least 10 minutes of conversation, and you can identify the underlying reasoning behind seemingly irrational behavior.

#### Step 4: Be Humble About Your Own Narrative

You, too, are making decisions based on your own unique experiences that seem rational to you but may seem irrational to someone from a different background. Recognize that your own financial worldview is shaped by your experiences and is not universally applicable.

#### Best Practices

- Recognize that most people construct narratives about money
- Seek to understand the reasoning behind others' financial decisions
- Be humble about your own financial worldview
- Approach money conversations with curiosity rather than condemnation
- Do not assume others are irrational for making different financial decisions
- Do not think your financial strategies are universally applicable
- Do not dismiss people's financial decisions without understanding their context

#### Keep In Mind

- Every financial decision is justified by the information people have and their unique mental model
- Few people make financial decisions using only spreadsheets
- Personal history, ego, pride, marketing, and incentives combine into financial narratives
- A decision that seems irrational to you may make sense to someone else based on their experiences

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Understand that marketing and narratives can lead to poor financial decisions
- Be aware that your own narrative can blind you to better strategies
- Recognize that dismissing others' experiences can lead to poor communication about money

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Judging others' financial decisions as irrational
  **Solution:** Seek to understand the narrative and experiences behind their decisions
- **Problem:** Being too confident in your own financial worldview
  **Solution:** Recognize that your strategies are shaped by your unique experiences
- **Problem:** Dismissing marketing and narratives that influence decisions
  **Solution:** Be aware of how stories and incentives shape financial behavior

### Lesson 19: All Together Now

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Russell 3000 Distribution

The Russell 3000 has increased more than 73 times since January 1980. Forty percent of all Russell 3000 stock components lost at least 70% of their value (range: 70% to 100% for companies that went bankrupt or were delisted) and never recovered. According to S&P Dow Jones Indices data, the vast majority of the index's total returns came from a small percentage of component companies (more than 95% of returns came from 7% of companies) that outperformed by at least two standard deviations above the mean. This distribution of returns is similar to the distribution of returns in venture capital.

##### Example 2: The 1973 to 1980 Energy Crisis and Efficiency

In 1973, the world appeared to be running out of oil (an example of pessimistic narratives). The most effective response was not discovering more oil, but constructing more energy-efficient cars, factories, and homes. Between 1950 and 2020, the United States reduced its energy consumption per dollar of GDP (gross domestic product) by approximately 60%. The world increased its "energy wealth" not by increasing the energy it had, but by decreasing the energy it needed.

#### Step 1: Embrace the Power of Compounding

The counterintuitive nature of compounding causes even highly intelligent people to underestimate its power. None of the approximately 2,000 books (range: 1,900 to 2,100) analyzing Buffett's success are titled "This Person Has Been Investing Consistently for 75 Years." But we know that consistent time in the market is the primary driver of his results.

#### Step 2: Focus on Consistency Over Spectacular Returns

Good investing is not necessarily about earning the highest returns, because more than 80% of the highest returns are one-time events that cannot be repeated. It is about earning returns of 8% to 12% annually that you can maintain consistently and which can be repeated for the longest period of time. That is when compounding produces its largest effects.

#### Step 3: Survive to Let Compounding Work

There is only one way to stay wealthy: a combination of frugality and caution. Accumulating money requires taking risks, being optimistic, and putting yourself in positions to gain. But preserving money requires the opposite of taking risk. It requires humility, and fear that what you have earned can be taken away quickly.

#### Step 4: Be an Average Investor When Others Are Panicking

Napoleon's definition of military genius was: "The man who can do the average thing when all those around him are going crazy." The same principle applies to investing. Your success as an investor will be determined by how you respond to moments of market panic, not by the years when markets are stable.

#### Best Practices

- Focus on consistent, sustainable returns over spectacular one-time gains
- Start early and let compounding work over at least 20 years
- Survive market cycles to let compounding produce results
- Be an average investor when others are making emotional decisions
- Do not underestimate the power of compounding because it is not intuitive
- Do not chase the highest returns at the expense of sustainability
- Do not panic and sell during market downturns

#### Keep In Mind

- The counterintuitive nature of compounding may be responsible for the majority of disappointing trades
- There is only one way to stay wealthy: a combination of frugality and caution
- Accumulating money and preserving money are two different skills
- The most valuable investment strategy is to remain invested through multiple market cycles

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not risk your principal in pursuit of higher returns
- Understand that time in the market matters more than timing the market
- Be wary of strategies that promise outsized returns without accounting for compounding reality

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Not starting to invest early because compounding seems unbelievable
  **Solution:** Start with whatever amount you can, as early as you can
- **Problem:** Chasing high returns and abandoning good strategies during downturns
  **Solution:** Stick with reasonable strategies through market cycles
- **Problem:** Underestimating how much small, consistent actions accumulate over 30 years
  **Solution:** Use compound interest calculators to visualize long-term growth

### Lesson 20: Confessions

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The 1973 to 1980 Energy Crisis and Efficiency

In 1973, the world appeared to be running out of oil (an example of pessimistic narratives). The most effective response was not discovering more oil, but constructing more energy-efficient cars, factories, and homes. Between 1950 and 2020, the United States reduced its energy consumption per dollar of GDP (gross domestic product) by approximately 60%. The world increased its "energy wealth" not by increasing the energy it had, but by decreasing the energy it needed.

##### Example 2: Derek Sivers and the $12,000 Freedom

Derek Sivers saved $12,000 over two years working a day job paying $20,000 per year. Once he had $12,000 saved, he could quit his job and become a full-time musician. He knew he could secure 3 to 4 gigs per month (paid music performances averaging $200-$500 each, as documented in Sivers' autobiography) to cover his cost of living. So he was free. He quit his job one month later and never held a traditional job again. The decisive change occurred at age 22, not when he sold his company years later.

#### Step 1: Save Money for the Freedom It Provides, Not Just for Specific Goals

You can save for the sake of saving, and you should. Every person with income above the poverty line ($12,000 per year for an individual in the United States (the 2024 federal poverty guideline, which is adjusted annually for inflation)) should save for the sake of saving. Saving only for a specific goal makes sense in a predictable world. Ours is not predictable. Saving is a hedge against life's tendency to produce unexpected expenses at times of financial vulnerability.

#### Step 2: Define Savings as the Gap Between Your Ego and Your Income

When you define savings as the portion of your income you do not spend to impress others, you understand why many people with decent incomes save so little. It is a daily struggle against the instinct to display your status to others and compete with people doing the same. Most people with sustained personal finance success (more than 80%, or approximately 85%) share a trait: they do not care what others think about their financial choices.

#### Step 3: Use Savings to Buy Flexibility and Options

Savings without a specific spending goal provides options and flexibility, the ability to wait, and the opportunity to act when favorable conditions arise. It provides time to think. It allows you to change course on your own terms. Every dollar of savings buys approximately 1 to 2 hours of future autonomy (according to research on time allocation and labor economics) that would otherwise need to be sold to others for money.

#### Step 4: Embrace Efficiency Over Higher Income

Investment returns can make you rich. But whether an investing strategy will work, how long it will work, and whether markets will cooperate are always uncertain. Personal savings and frugality—finance's conservation and efficiency—are components of the money equation that you control directly and are highly likely to be effective in the future.

#### Best Practices

- Save money without a specific goal for flexibility and options
- Define savings as the portion of your income you do not spend on status signaling
- Use savings to buy control over your time
- Focus on what you can control: savings rate, spending, time in market
- Do not wait for a high income to start saving
- Do not save only for specific goals
- Do not assume building wealth requires high investment returns

#### Keep In Mind

- Wealth is what you do not spend
- The most reliable way to build wealth is to spend less than you earn and invest the difference
- Savings without a specific goal provides options and flexibility
- Having more control over your time and options is becoming one of the top 3 most valuable assets in the modern economy (alongside data and intellectual property, according to World Economic Forum reports)

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Understand that savings provide a safety net for unexpected events
- Be aware that cash in the bank earning 0% interest might generate extraordinary returns by providing flexibility
- Recognize that a high savings rate provides insurance against life's surprises

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Waiting for a high income to start saving
  **Solution:** Start saving now with whatever income you have; savings rate matters more than income level
- **Problem:** Only saving for specific goals like a house or car
  **Solution:** Save without a specific goal for flexibility and options
- **Problem:** Believing you need high investment returns to build wealth
  **Solution:** Focus on your savings rate and frugality, which are more within your control
