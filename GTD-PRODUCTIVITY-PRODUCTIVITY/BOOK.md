# Getting Things Done

## Overview

Getting Things Done by David Allen is a productivity method designed to help knowledge workers (professionals who determine their own tasks) achieve productivity by capturing open loops (items requiring action that have not been captured) into external capture systems (physical in-baskets, digital notes, voice recorders, or email inboxes) and making next-action decisions at the moment of capture. The methodology is designed to transform unprocessed thoughts and commitments into organized workflow using GTD tools (Projects lists, Next Actions lists, calendars, Waiting For lists, Someday/Maybe lists, and reference filing systems) through a five-stage process: collecting, processing, organizing, reviewing, and doing. Rather than relying on traditional to-do list and calendar-only time-management models, the book provides tools and behaviors that enable workers to maintain a state where no open loop triggers anxiety, and consistently make effective choices about what to do when choosing actions.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

  - When you have more than 25 active open loops
  - When you struggle to maintain the ability to identify the next physical action for any open loop within 60 seconds amid more than 3 interruptions per hour
  - When traditional to-do list and calendar-only systems may prove inadequate for professional work involving creative or analytical tasks
  - When you want to achieve a state where all open loops are captured and processed within professional, personal, health, financial, and relational domains
  - When you need a systematic method to manage projects, from single-action tasks to multi-step professional initiatives
  - When you want to improve your ability to make decisions using the Four-Criteria Model about what to do when choosing actions

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: The Mind Like Water Principle — Achieve Productivity With Reduced Mental Stress

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Karate "Mind Like Water" Simile

Imagine throwing a pebble into a still pond. The water responds in direct proportion to the pebble's force and mass, then returns to calm. This is the ideal state for responding to inputs.

##### Example 2: The Rowers' "Swing"

Elite rowers describe a state called 'swing' (analogy), where the boat carries them and gravity does much of the work (analogy). They are not so much swinging as being swung (analogy). Trying too hard can reduce performance; being relaxed and present can produce strong results.

#### Step 1: Identify Your Open Loops

Any item requiring action, reference, or decision that has not been captured in a trusted system (a system you check at least once daily and update within 24 hours of new inputs) that is recurring in your thoughts more than three times in a week—items requiring action, reference, or decision that you have explicitly or implicitly accepted responsibility for.

#### Step 2: Capture All Relevant Items Outside Your Head

Your mind is for having ideas, not for holding them. Capture all work-related items requiring action, reference, or decision into collection buckets (physical in-basket, electronic notes, voice recorder, calendar, and other tools). This frees working memory (analogy for limited mental capacity) so your brain can focus rather than relying on your brain to remind you.

#### Step 3: Clarify and Decide on Next Actions

For each item collected, determine: (a) Is it actionable? (actionable = requires a physical next step) (b) If yes, what is the very next physical, visible action required? If no, decide whether it is trash, reference material, or something to incubate for later.

#### Step 4: Organize Results into Trusted Systems

Place time-specific actions on your calendar and all other action reminders on context-based Next Actions lists.

#### Step 5: Conduct a Weekly Review

Every Friday afternoon, review and update all open loops in your Projects list, Next Actions lists, Waiting For list, and Someday/Maybe list. This helps ensure your brain stays clear and that no item is missed.

#### Step 6: Choose Actions Using the Four-Criteria Model

When choosing actions, apply these four criteria in order: (1) Context — where you are and what tools you have available; (2) Time Available — how much time you have before your next commitment; (3) Energy Available — how much mental or physical energy you have; (4) Priority — which action moves closest to project completion.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Capture all work-related items requiring action, reference, or decision into an external system—never rely on your memory
- ✅ Process your in-basket every 24-48 hours—do not let it pile up
- ✅ Make a next-action decision within 60 seconds of capture
- ✅ Keep your filing system able to file any document within 60 seconds—if filing takes longer than 60 seconds, adoption drops significantly
- ✅ Maintain no more than three to five collection buckets, with at least one bucket for each context (work, home, mobile)
- ✅ Use your calendar for time-specific actions only—never for to-do lists. Time-specific actions are actions tied to a calendar date or time window. Day-specific actions are actions tied to a particular day but not a specific time.
- ✅ Trust your system by reviewing it every 7 days—the Weekly Review is the key habit for maintaining control
- ✅ Reframe projects for which you cannot name the next physical action within 60 seconds by asking 'What's the very next physical action?'

#### Keep In Mind

 - Stress is commonly reported as caused by commitments that have been captured but not clarified into next actions, not by having more work than can be tracked
 - Your short-term memory functions like working memory (analogy for limited mental capacity)—it becomes overloaded by more than seven incomplete items held for more than 24 hours
 - The mind may not distinguish between action with a deadline within 24 hours and an unresolved open loop; it will remind you of incomplete items during sleep, focused work, or personal time
 - The GTD system's goal is not to complete all actions, but to experience no mental resistance or regret about your selections


#### Security & Safety Notes

- Never store sensitive reference materials (PII, financial data, or proprietary information) in unencrypted plain-text note systems or physical in-baskets left unattended
 - When delegating actions to others via email or shared systems, confirm receipt and expected completion date, and log the item on your Waiting For list
- Avoid the temptation to use your in-basket as a storage system; items left there unresolved create mental resistance or distraction comparable to unresolved items held only in memory

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Keeping things only in your head instead of capturing them in a trusted external system.
  **Solution:** Immediately write down or record any new commitment, idea, or task the moment it enters your awareness.

- **Problem:** Using daily to-do lists that must be rewritten and become demoralizing.
  **Solution:** Use a calendar only for time-specific and day-specific hard landscape items; store all other actions on context-based Next Actions lists.

- **Problem:** Failing to define the next physical action for projects, leaving them lacking a defined next action, preventing forward progress.
  **Solution:** For every open project, always identify and record the very next physical, visible action step required to move it forward.

 - **Problem:** Overloading your collection buckets without processing them, turning in-baskets into more than 10 unprocessed items in the in-basket.
   **Solution:** Empty your in-baskets, email, voicemail, and physical notes regularly as part of your Weekly Review.

 - **Problem:** Trying to manage priorities without a current Next Actions list and without knowing your available actions.
   **Solution:** In the GTD framework, priorities are inherent rather than assigned. What you must manage is your actions, by making choices aligned with context, time, energy, and priority.

### Lesson 2: The Five Stages of Mastering Workflow — The Horizontal Action Management System

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Police Radar Scan

Your attention system continuously monitors your environment, landing on 200-300 items each day. Horizontal action management keeps track of all items across professional and personal domains.

##### Example 2: The Project as Open Loop

A project is any outcome you have committed to achieving that requires two or more distinct physical actions. Without a Projects list, the project remains in working memory (analogy for limited mental capacity), consuming cognitive resources during periods of rest, sleep, or focus on other tasks.

#### Step 1: Collect

Gather every open loop, task, idea, and reference item into collection buckets. This includes items from professional projects requiring 10+ actions to personal tasks requiring 1-2 actions. All captured items must be stored in physical or digital in-baskets, notebooks, voice recorders, or email inboxes.

#### Step 2: Process

For each item in your collection buckets, ask: "What is it?" and "Is it actionable? (actionable = requires a physical next step)" If actionable, determine the project it belongs to and the next physical action required. If not actionable, decide whether it is trash, reference material, or something to incubate for later.

#### Step 3: Organize

Place the results of your processing into these specific categories: Projects list, Next Actions lists organized by context, Calendar for time-specific actions, Waiting For list, Someday/Maybe list, Reference filing system, and Trash.

#### Step 4: Review

Review your full inventory of open loops at least once per week during the Weekly Review. Check your calendar each workday morning to see the hard landscape of time-specific commitments. Review your Projects, Waiting For, and Someday/Maybe lists at least weekly or when list exceeds 10 items to keep them from consuming attention. The most important habit for maintaining the GTD system is the Weekly Review.

#### Step 5: Do

Make action choices using the Four-Criteria Model. Trust that your system is complete when all open loops have been captured and processed within the past 7 days, so you can move from hoping it's the right choice to trusting it's the right choice.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Capture all work-related items requiring action, reference, or decision into collection buckets, then empty them every 24-48 hours
- ✅ Make the 'next action' decision for every item in your collection buckets during processing—do not leave items vague
- ✅ If an action will take less than two minutes, do it within 60 seconds of identifying it
- ✅ Delegate actions to the appropriate person (person with required skills, authority, and availability) who is the right one to do them (person who can complete the action most efficiently given context, time, and energy constraints)
- ✅ Organize action reminders by context (At Computer, At Home, Calls, Errands, etc.) to enable rapid selection
- ✅ Never put non-time-specific to-do items on your calendar—it must remain protected space reserved for hard landscape items only. Time-specific actions are actions tied to a calendar date or time window. Day-specific actions are actions tied to a particular day but not a specific time.
- ✅ Maintain a single A-Z general-reference filing system—do not create multiple filing systems
- ✅ Keep file drawers less than three-quarters full to eliminate unconscious resistance to filing
- ✅ Use an automatic labeler to create clean, professional file folder labels that make filing fast and attractive
- ✅ Review your Someday/Maybe list during your Weekly Review to surface possible new projects

#### Keep In Mind

 - The quality of your workflow management is only as good as the weakest link in the five-stage chain
 - Most people have major leaks in their collection process—they collect things but do not process them, or process them but do not organize the results
 - The in-basket should be emptied regularly, but emptying it does not mean finishing everything—it means deciding what everything is and organizing the results
 - Horizontal management maintains coherence across all activities; vertical management (project planning) fleshes out the details of individual projects
- You do not manage time, information, or priorities—you manage actions

#### Security & Safety Notes

 - Keep sensitive reference materials in secure, access-controlled filing systems
 - When delegating actions to others via email or shared systems, confirm receipt and expected completion date, and log the item on your Waiting For list

#### Common Pitfalls

 - **Problem:** Having too many collection buckets, making it impossible to process them consistently.
   **Solution:** Have as many as you need to capture items in every context, but as few as possible.

 - **Problem:** Leaving things in the in-basket because there is no effective system downstream to handle them.
   **Solution:** Implement the full five-stage system—collection, processing, organizing, reviewing, doing—before expecting to empty your in-basket with integrity.

- **Problem:** Using daily to-do lists that must be rewritten and become demoralizing.
  **Solution:** Store all non-time-specific actions on context-based Next Actions lists. Only time-specific actions and day-specific information belong on the calendar.

 - **Problem:** Failing to define the next physical action for projects, leaving them stuck in your mind, preventing forward progress.
  **Solution:** For every project, ensure the next action is clear. If it is unclear, do more planning.

### Lesson 3: The Natural Planning Model — Five Phases of Project Planning

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Planning a Dinner Out

The last time you planned to go out to dinner, your mind went through five natural phases: (1) Purpose — satisfying hunger or socializing with friends; (2) Vision/Outcome — sitting at a sidewalk table at the Bistro Cafe; (3) Brainstorming — "What time should we go?" "Is it open tonight?" "Will it be crowded?"; (4) Organizing — "First check if the restaurant is open, then call the Andersons, then get dressed"; (5) Next Action — "Call Suzanne's to see if it's open, and make the reservation."

##### Example 2: The Reactive Planning Model vs. Natural Planning

Most organizations use a reactive model: action first, then organization, then creativity, then vision. The natural planning model reverses this order and produces better results with less stress.

#### Step 1: Define Purpose

Ask 'Why are we doing this?' Clarifying purpose defines success, creates decision-making criteria, aligns resources, motivates, clarifies focus, and expands creative options. If you are not sure why you are doing something, you can never do enough of it.

#### Step 2: Envision Success

Create a clear, vivid picture of what successful completion of the project would look, sound, and feel like. View the project from beyond the completion date. Envision 'WILD SUCCESS' and suspend all 'Yeah, but...' objections. Capture the features, aspects, and qualities you imagine in place.

#### Step 3: Brainstorm

Generate ideas without judging, challenging, evaluating, or criticizing. Go for quantity, not quality. Put analysis and organization in the background. Capture every idea and many ideas.

#### Step 4: Organize

Identify the significant pieces and sort them by components, sequences, and/or priorities. Detail to the required degree.

#### Step 5: Identify Next Actions

For each component of the project, determine the very next physical action required to move it forward. Ask: 'What's the next action, and who's got it?' If the next action is not yours, clarify whose it is and track it on a Waiting For list.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Always begin project planning by clarifying purpose—do not skip to action or organization
- ✅ Envision wild success before asking "How do we get there?"
- ✅ Brainstorm freely without judgment before organizing or evaluating ideas
 - ✅ Use back-of-the-envelope planning for most projects—informal, natural planning is often more effective than formal models
- ✅ Keep the next action decision front and center—if the project is still on your mind, there is more planning to do
- ✅ Do not assign "good" or "bad" labels to ideas during brainstorming—park critical thinking for the organizing phase
- ✅ If you need more clarity, shift up the natural planning scale (from action to organization to brainstorming to vision to purpose)
- ✅ If you need more to be happening, shift down the natural planning scale

#### Keep In Mind

 - Natural planning is not normal—most people are trained to plan unnaturally
 - Your brain is already a planning machine—you plan naturally every time you decide to go out to dinner or run an errand
 - You do not need elaborate tools or formal meetings for most project planning—a few minutes of focused thinking is often sufficient
- If greater clarity is needed, pull back up the planning scale; if more action is needed, move down the scale
- A project plan identifies the smaller outcomes, which can then be naturally planned down to next actions

#### Security & Safety Notes

- When planning projects involving sensitive data, ensure that project support materials are stored in appropriate access-controlled systems
- Clarify purpose and principles at the outset of any major project to prevent scope creep and unwanted side effects

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Jumping straight to action or "What's a good idea?" before clarifying purpose, vision, or gathering enough ideas.
  **Solution:** Follow the natural planning sequence—purpose, vision, brainstorming, organizing, next actions.

 - **Problem:** Using unnatural planning models that create resistance, stress, and mediocre results.
  **Solution:** Leverage the way your brain naturally plans—generate ideas first, organize them second, and decide on next actions third.

- **Problem:** Believing you need formal tools and meetings for every project plan.
  **Solution:** Most projects need only a project name and a next action. About 15 percent may need some brainstorming notes. Only about 5 percent require full formal planning.

- **Problem:** Procrastinating on projects because you have not defined what the next physical action is.
  **Solution:** Ask "What's the next action?" If you do not know the answer, you have more planning to do at a prior level of the natural planning model.

### Lesson 4: Horizontal and Vertical Action Management

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Horizontal Control

Your psyche scans your environment like police radar, landing on dozens of items every day. Horizontal action management keeps track of all items across professional and personal domains.

##### Example 2: Vertical Control

Your inner radar lands on your next vacation—where and when you'll go, what you'll do, how to prepare. Or you and your boss need to make decisions about a new departmental reorganization. Vertical management focuses on a single endeavor and fleshes out the ideas, details, priorities, and sequences required to handle it.

#### Step 1: Practice Horizontal Management

Maintain coherence across all your activities using the GTD five-stage workflow: Collect all open loops into trusted buckets, process them to determine meaning and next actions, organize the results into trusted lists and files, review your system regularly, and choose actions in the moment using the four-criteria model.

#### Step 2: Practice Vertical Management

For individual projects and topics, apply the Natural Planning Model: define purpose and principles, envision success, brainstorm ideas, organize components and sequences, and identify next actions. Use back-of-the-envelope planning, mind-mapping, or formal project tools as appropriate.

#### Step 3: Integrate Both Levels

Use horizontal management to keep your overall life and work under control, and vertical management to get specific projects moving. The goal for both is the same: get things off your mind and get things done.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Use horizontal management to prevent things from falling through the cracks across all tracked commitments
- ✅ Use vertical management to clarify and drive forward individual projects when they need deeper thinking
- ✅ Keep project support materials in separate, easily accessible files or binders for current projects
- ✅ Do not try to do all five stages of workflow management simultaneously—separate collection, processing, organizing, and reviewing into distinct time blocks
 - ✅ Getting current on mundane 'stuff' unleashes creative energy

#### Keep In Mind

- Horizontal and vertical action management are complementary, not competing systems
 - Most people fail at 'getting organized' because they try to process, organize, and review all at once
 - Getting your in-basket and physical workspace under control is like taking off baggy clothing before swimming—it makes everything else easier

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Ensure that vertical project planning for sensitive projects (projects involving confidential data, regulated information, or competitive strategy) includes confidentiality and access controls
- When using shared project support materials, label clearly sensitive from non-sensitive information

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Trying to plan all current projects in detail by identifying the next action for each component before implementing horizontal workflow management.
  **Solution:** Get your current "stuff" under control first using horizontal management; this will free up the mental energy needed for effective vertical planning.

- **Problem:** Letting project details and support materials pile up on your desk instead of organizing them into accessible files.
  **Solution:** Store project support materials in standing file folders or trays within reach for current projects, and in your general-reference filing system for lower-priority projects.

### Lesson 5: The Six-Level Model for Reviewing Your Work

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Altitude Framework

Like a pilot at different altitudes (analogy), you can review your work from six different perspectives

##### Example 2: Misaligned Thinking

A manager complains about too many meetings. When asked 'What is the purpose of the meetings?' the manager cannot answer. Without clarity at the purpose level, decisions at the action level become misaligned and ineffective.

#### Step 1: Review from the Runway (Current Actions)

Review your full inventory of actions. Based on GTD research, many knowledge workers have 300 to 500 hours of actions in their open loop inventory if all external commitments paused.

#### Step 2: Review from 10,000 Feet (Current Projects)

Examine 30 to 100 current projects (typically 30-40 for individual contributors, 70-100 for managers). These are short-term outcomes achievable within 3-12 months.

#### Step 3: Review from 20,000 Feet (Areas of Responsibility)

Identify 10 to 15 areas (typically including professional responsibilities, health, family, finances, and personal development) where you want results and standards. These are domains where you have accepted responsibility for outcomes.

#### Step 4: Review from 30,000 Feet (One- to Two-Year Goals)

Consider what you want to experience in the areas 12-24 months from now. Meeting these outcomes may require changes in emphasis and new areas of responsibility.

#### Step 5: Review from 40,000 Feet (Three- to Five-Year Vision)

Project 36-60 months into the future. Consider questions such as: What organization strategies will be available? What environmental trends will affect your work? What career or life transitions do you anticipate? How will technology and market trends evolve?

#### Step 6: Review from 50,000+ Feet (Life)

Clarify your fundamental purpose (the single reason that, if fulfilled, would make all other goals unnecessary) and the main reason that supersedes all others. This is the foundational description for both your work and your life, from which all goals, visions, objectives, projects, and actions derive.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Use the six-level model as a framework for evaluating your inventory of projects and commitments
- ✅ Review from higher altitudes when you need greater clarity; review from lower altitudes when you need more action
- ✅ The bottom-up approach is frequently more effective
- ✅ List and review your areas of responsibility to generate a more comprehensive framework for evaluating projects
- ✅ Notice when things feel unclear at one level—shift up or down the model to find the missing piece of planning

#### Keep In Mind

- "Setting priorities" in the traditional sense does not provide a practical framework for most day-to-day decisions
- Mastering the flow of your work at all six levels provides a holistic way to get things done and feel good about it
- The altitude analogies are chosen for convenience—they are not based on empirical research or universal principles. Important conversations (conversations with high impact on outcomes) may not fit exactly to one level or another.
- Many knowledge workers are so engaged in day-to-day commitments that their ability to focus on longer-term goals is significantly reduced; getting current on routine items frees creative capacity for higher-level thinking

#### Security & Safety Notes

- When reviewing work at the 40,000- and 50,000-foot levels, ensure that long-term strategic plans involving sensitive information (information whose unauthorized disclosure could cause harm) are documented in secure systems (systems with access controls and encryption)
- Clarify purpose and principles at the start of any significant project (project requiring more than 10 actions or 3+ months to complete) to prevent uncontrolled expansion of project boundaries and unintended consequences

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Trying to work from the top down (life purpose → goals → actions) when you are very overwhelmed by daily commitments can limit your ability to focus effectively. Use a bottom-up approach—get current on your runway-level actions first, then gradually broaden your horizons.
  **Solution:** Use a bottom-up approach—get current on your runway-level actions first, then gradually broaden your horizons.

- **Problem:** Focusing only on big-picture goals and values while ignoring the mundane details that must be managed to achieve them.
  **Solution:** The GTD system bridges both levels—manage routine details through horizontal workflow management while maintaining vertical focus on outcomes and purpose.
