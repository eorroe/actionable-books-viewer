# Getting Things Done

## Overview

Getting Things Done by David Allen is a productivity method designed to help knowledge workers achieve productivity by capturing open loops (incompletes) into external capture systems and making front-end decisions. The methodology transforms mental clutter into organized, actionable workflow through a five-stage process of collecting, processing, organizing, reviewing, and doing. Rather than relying on traditional time-management models, the book provides tools and behaviors that enable workers to maintain relaxed control and stress-free productivity, achieve a mind-like-water state (analogy), and consistently make good choices about what to do at any given moment.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

 - When you feel overwhelmed by the volume
 - When you struggle to maintain mental clarity amid constant interruptions
 - When traditional time-management systems have proven inadequate for modern knowledge work
 - When you want to achieve relaxed control and stress-free productivity across all areas of your life
 - When you need a systematic method to manage projects, from small personal goals to large professional initiatives
 - When you want to improve your ability to make intuitive, trusted decisions about what to do at any point in time

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: The Mind Like Water Principle — Achieve Stress-Free Productivity

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Karate "Mind Like Water" Simile

Imagine throwing a pebble into a still pond. The water responds appropriately to the force and mass of the input, then returns to calm. This is the ideal state for responding to the demands of work and life.

##### Example 2: The Rowers' "Swing"

World-class rowers describe a frictionless state called "swing", where the boat carries them and gravity does most of the work. They are not so much swinging as being swung. Trying too hard sabotages performance; being relaxed and present produces the best results.

#### Step 1: Identify Your Open Loops

Anything that is not where it should be, the way it should be, and everything that commands your attention—big or small, personal or professional, urgent or minor—that you feel any responsibility to change or complete.

#### Step 2: Capture Everything Outside Your Head

Your mind is for having ideas, not for holding them. Capture everything into trusted collection buckets (physical in-basket, electronic notes, voice recorder, calendar, etc.). This empties your RAM so your brain can focus rather than relying on your brain to remind you.

#### Step 3: Clarify and Decide on Next Actions

For each item collected, determine: (a) Is it actionable? (actionable = requires a physical next step) (b) If yes, what is the very next physical, visible action required? If no, decide whether it is trash, reference material, or something to incubate for later.

#### Step 4: Organize Results into Trusted Systems

Place time-specific actions on your calendar and all other action reminders on context-based Next Actions lists. Track projects on a Projects list, actions on Next Actions lists organized by context, delegated items on a Waiting For list, and future ideas on a Someday/Maybe list.

#### Step 5: Conduct a Weekly Review

Once a week, review and update all your open loops. This ensures your brain remains clear and that nothing falls through the cracks.

#### Step 6: Choose Actions Using the Four-Criteria Model

At any given moment, apply these four criteria in order: (1) Context — where am I and what tools do I have available? (2) Time Available — how much time do I have before my next commitment? (3) Energy Available — how much mental or physical energy do I have? (4) Priority — which action yields the highest payoff?

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Capture everything into an external trusted system—never rely on your memory
- ✅ Process your in-basket regularly—do not let it pile up
- ✅ Make front-end decisions about next actions when items first appear on your radar
- ✅ Keep your filing system fast, functional, and fun—if it takes more than one minute to file something, you will resist using it
- ✅ Maintain as few collection buckets as possible, but enough to capture items in every context
- ✅ Use your calendar for time-specific and day-specific actions only—never for to-do lists
- ✅ Trust your system by reviewing it consistently—the Weekly Review is the master key to relaxed control
- ✅ Reframe overwhelming projects by asking "What's the very next physical action?"

#### Keep In Mind

- Stress is caused by inappropriately managed commitments, not by having too much to do
- Your short-term memory functions like RAM on a computer—it becomes overloaded by too many incomplete items
- The mind cannot tell the difference between urgent demand for action and an unresolved open loop; it will remind you of incomplete items at inappropriate times
- The goal of the GTD system is not to complete everything, but to feel good about your choices
- Most people experience their best week just before vacation—not because of the vacation itself, but because they cleaned up, clarified, and renegotiated all their agreements with themselves and others

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Never store sensitive reference materials in unencrypted plain-text note systems or physical in-baskets left unattended
- When delegating actions to others, maintain a "Waiting For" list with an agreed completion date and accountability reminders to ensure no item is missed
- Avoid the temptation to use your in-basket as a storage system; items left there unresolved create the same mental drag as items kept only in your head

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Keeping things only in your head instead of capturing them in a trusted external system.
  **Solution:** Immediately write down or record any new commitment, idea, or task the moment it enters your awareness.

- **Problem:** Using daily to-do lists that must be rewritten when items are not completed, demoralizing and wasting time.
  **Solution:** Use a calendar only for time-specific and day-specific hard landscape items; store all other actions on context-based Next Actions lists.

- **Problem:** Failing to define the next physical action for projects, leaving them lacking a defined next action, preventing forward progress.
  **Solution:** For every open project, always identify and record the very next physical, visible action step required to move it forward.

- **Problem:** Overloading your collection buckets without processing them, turning in-baskets into "amorphous blobs of undoability."
  **Solution:** Empty your in-baskets, email, voicemail, and physical notes regularly as part of your Weekly Review.

- **Problem:** Trying to manage priorities in the abstract without knowing what your actual actions are.
  **Solution:** Priorities are not something you manage—you have them. What you must manage is your actions, by making good choices in the moment based on context, time, energy, and priority.

### Lesson 2: The Five Stages of Mastering Workflow — The Horizontal Action Management System

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Police Radar Scan

Your psyche is constantly scanning, like police radar, landing on hundreds of items that invite or demand your attention every 24-hour period. Horizontal action management keeps track of all of these across your whole life and work.

##### Example 2: The Project as Open Loop

A project is any desired result that requires more than one action step. Without a Projects list, the project slips back into RAM, consuming mental energy even when you are not actively working on it.

#### Step 1: Collect

Gather everything into collection buckets. This includes everything, from major strategic initiatives to the smallest task. All of it must be captured into physical or digital in-baskets, note-taking devices, voice recorders, or email inboxes.

#### Step 2: Process

For each item in your collection buckets, ask: "What is it?" and "Is it actionable? (actionable = requires a physical next step)" If actionable, determine the project it belongs to and the next physical action required. If not actionable, decide whether it is trash, reference material, or something to incubate for later.

#### Step 3: Organize

Place the results of your processing into these specific categories: Projects list, Next Actions lists organized by context, Calendar for time-specific actions, Waiting For list, Someday/Maybe list, Reference filing system, and Trash.

#### Step 4: Review

Review your complete inventory of open loops on a regular basis. Check your calendar daily to see the hard landscape of time-specific commitments. Review your Projects, Waiting For, and Someday/Maybe lists as often as needed to keep them off your mind. The critical success factor is the Weekly Review.

#### Step 5: Do

Make intuitive action choices in the moment. Trust that your system is complete so you can move from hoping it's the right choice to trusting it's the right choice.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Capture everything into as few collection buckets as possible, then empty them regularly
- ✅ Make the "next action" decision for every item during processing—do not leave items vague
- ✅ If an action will take less than two minutes, do it immediately when you identify it
- ✅ Delegate actions to the appropriate person who is the right one to do them when you are not the person who can complete the action most efficiently given context, time, and energy constraints
- ✅ Organize action reminders by context (At Computer, At Home, Calls, Errands, etc.) to enable rapid selection
- ✅ Never put non-time-specific to-do items on your calendar—it must remain sacred territory reserved for hard landscape items only
- ✅ Maintain a single A-Z general-reference filing system—do not create multiple filing systems
- ✅ Keep file drawers less than three-quarters full to eliminate unconscious resistance to filing
- ✅ Use an automatic labeler to create clean, professional file folder labels that make filing fast and attractive
- ✅ Review your Someday/Maybe list during your Weekly Review to surface possible new projects

#### Keep In Mind

- The quality of your workflow management is only as good as the weakest link in the five-stage chain
- Most people have major leaks in their collection process—they collect things but do not process them, or process them but do not organize the results
- The in-basket should be emptied regularly, but emptying it does not mean finishing everything—it means deciding what everything is and organizing the results
- Horizontal management maintains all activities captured and fleshes out the details; vertical management (project planning) fleshes out the ideas of individual projects
- You do not manage time, information, or priorities—you manage actions

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Keep sensitive information in secure systems
- When delegating actions to others, maintain a "Waiting For" list with an expected completion date and accountability reminders to ensure nothing falls through the cracks

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Having too many collection buckets, making it impossible to process them consistently.
  **Solution:** Minimize the number of collection zones. Have as many as you need to capture items in every context, but as few as possible.

- **Problem:** Leaving things in the in-basket because there is no effective system downstream.
  **Solution:** Implement the full five-stage system—collection, processing, organizing, reviewing, doing—before expecting to empty your in-basket with integrity.

- **Problem:** Using daily to-do lists that must be rewritten and become demoralizing.
  **Solution:** Store all non-time-specific actions on context-based Next Actions lists. Only time-specific actions and day-specific information belong on the calendar.

- **Problem:** Failing to define the next physical action for projects, leaving them stuck in your mind, preventing forward progress.
  **Solution:** For every project, ensure the next action is clear. If it is unclear, do more planning.

### Lesson 3: The Natural Planning Model — Five Phases of Project Planning

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Planning a Dinner Out

The last time you planned to go out to dinner, your mind went through five natural phases: (1) Purpose — satisfying hunger or socializing with friends; (2) Vision/Outcome — sitting at a sidewalk table at the Bistro Cafe; (3) Brainstorming — "What time should we go?" "Is it open tonight?" "Will it be crowded?"; (4) Organizing — "First check if the restaurant is open, then call the Andersons, then get dressed"; (5) Next Action — "Call Suzanne's to see if it's open, and make the reservation."

##### Example 2: The Reactive Planning Model vs. Natural Planning

Most organizations use a reactive planning model: action first ("Work harder!"), then organization ("Let's get organized!"), then creativity ("Let's brainstorm!"), then vision ("So, what are we really trying to do here?"). The natural planning model reverses this order and produces better results with less stress.

#### Step 1: Define Purpose

Ask "Why are we doing this?" Clarifying purpose defines success, creates decision-making criteria, aligns resources, motivates, clarifies focus, and expands creative options. If you are not sure why you are doing something, you can never do enough of it.

#### Step 2: Envision Success

Create a clear, vivid picture of what successful completion of the project would look, sound, and feel like. View the project from beyond the completion date. Envision "WILD SUCCESS" and suspend all "Yeah, but..." objections. Capture the features, aspects, and qualities you imagine in place.

#### Step 3: Brainstorm

Generate ideas without judging, challenging, evaluating, or criticizing. Go for quantity, not quality. Put analysis and organization in the background. Capture every idea and many ideas.

#### Step 4: Organize

Identify the significant pieces requiring the required degree of organization and sort them by components, sequences, and/or priorities. Detail to sufficient detail to identify the next action for each component.

#### Step 5: Identify Next Actions

For each component of the project, determine the very next physical action required to move it forward. Ask: "What's the next action, and who's got it?" If the next action is not yours, clarify whose it is and track it on a Waiting For list.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Always begin project planning by clarifying purpose—do not skip to action or organization
- ✅ Envision wild success before asking "How do we get there?"
- ✅ Brainstorm freely without judgment before organizing or evaluating ideas
- ✅ Use back-of-the-envelope planning for most projects—informal, natural planning is often more effective than formal models
- ✅ Keep the next action decision front and center—if the project is still on your mind, there is more planning to do
- ✅ Do not assign "good" or "bad" labels to ideas during brainstorming—park critical thinking for the organizing phase
- ✅ If you need more clarity, shift up the natural planning scale (from action to organization to brainstorming to vision to purpose)
- ✅ If you need more to be happening, shift down the natural planning scale

#### Keep In Mind

- Natural planning is not normal—most people are trained to plan unnaturally
- Your brain is already a planning machine—you plan naturally every time you decide to go out to dinner or run an errand
- You do not need elaborate tools or formal meetings for most project planning—a few minutes of focused thinking is often sufficient
- If greater clarity is needed, pull back up the planning scale; if more action is needed, move down the scale
- A project plan identifies the smaller outcomes, which can then be naturally planned down to next actions

#### Security & Safety Notes

- When planning projects involving sensitive data, ensure that project support materials are stored in appropriate access-controlled systems
- Clarify purpose and principles at the outset of any major project to prevent scope creep and unwanted side effects

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Jumping straight to action or "What's a good idea?" before clarifying purpose, vision, or gathering enough ideas.
  **Solution:** Follow the natural planning sequence—purpose, vision, brainstorming, organizing, next actions.

- **Problem:** Using unnatural planning models that create resistance, stress, and mediocre results.
  **Solution:** Leverage the way your brain naturally plans—generate ideas first, organize them second, and decide on next actions third.

- **Problem:** Believing you need formal tools and meetings for every project plan.
  **Solution:** Most projects need only a project name and a next action. About 15 percent may need some brainstorming notes. Only about 5 percent require full formal planning.

- **Problem:** Procrastinating on projects because you have not defined what the next physical action is.
  **Solution:** Ask "What's the next action?" If you do not know the answer, you have more planning to do at a prior level of the natural planning model.

### Lesson 4: Horizontal and Vertical Action Management

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Horizontal Control

Your psyche scans your environment, landing on dozens of items every day—the drugstore, the housekeeper, your aunt Martha, the strategic plan, lunch, a wilting plant, an upset customer. Horizontal management keeps track of all of these so you can shift focus quickly and easily.

##### Example 2: Vertical Control

Your inner radar lands on where and when you'll go, what you'll do, how to prepare. Or you and your boss need to make decisions about a new departmental reorganization. Vertical management focuses on a single endeavor requiring focused attention and fleshes out the ideas required to handle it.

#### Step 1: Practice Horizontal Management

Maintain coherence across all your activities using the GTD five-stage workflow: Collect all open loops into trusted buckets, process them to determine meaning and next actions, organize the results into trusted lists and files, review your system regularly, and choose actions in the moment using the four-criteria model.

#### Step 2: Practice Vertical Management

For individual projects and topics, apply the Natural Planning Model: define purpose and principles, envision success, brainstorm ideas, organize components and sequences, and identify next actions. Use back-of-the-envelope planning for simple projects, mind-mapping for medium complexity projects, or formal project tools for complex projects, as appropriate.

#### Step 3: Integrate Both Levels

Use horizontal management to keep your overall life and work under control, and vertical management to get specific projects moving. The goal for both is the same: get things off your mind and get things done.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Use horizontal management to prevent things from falling through the cracks across all tracked commitments
- ✅ Use vertical management to clarify and drive forward individual projects when they need deeper thinking
- ✅ Keep project support materials in separate, easily accessible files or binders for current projects
- ✅ Do not try to do all five stages of workflow management simultaneously—separate collection, processing, organizing, and reviewing into distinct time blocks
- ✅ Getting current on mundane "stuff" unleashes creative energy

#### Keep In Mind

- Horizontal and vertical action management are complementary, not competing systems
- Most people fail at "getting organized" because they try to do it all at once
- Getting your in-basket and physical workspace under control is like taking off baggy clothing before swimming—it makes everything else easier

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Ensure that vertical project planning for sensitive initiatives includes appropriate confidentiality and access controls
- When using shared project support materials, clearly label confidential from non-confidential information

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Trying to plan all projects in detail by identifying the next action for each component before implementing horizontal workflow management.
  **Solution:** Get your current "stuff" under control first using horizontal management; this will free up the mental energy needed for effective vertical planning.

- **Problem:** Letting project details and support materials pile up on your desk instead of organizing them into accessible files.
  **Solution:** Store project support materials in standing file folders or trays within easy reach for active projects, and in your general-reference filing system for lower-priority projects.

### Lesson 5: The Six-Level Model for Reviewing Your Work

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Altitude Framework

Just as a pilot operates at different altitudes,

##### Example 2: Misaligned Thinking

A manager complains about too many meetings. When asked "What is the purpose of the meetings?" he cannot answer. Without clarity at the purpose level, decisions at the action level become misaligned and ineffective.

#### Step 1: Review from the Runway (Current Actions)

Look at your complete inventory—all the phone calls, emails, errands, and tasks. Most people have 300 to 500 hours' worth of actions if they stopped the world right now.

#### Step 2: Review from 10,000 Feet (Current Projects)

Examine your 30 to 100 current projects. These are the relatively short-term outcomes you want to achieve—setting up a home computer, organizing a sales conference, moving to a new headquarters, getting a dentist.

#### Step 3: Review from 20,000 Feet (Areas of Responsibility)

Identify 10 to 15 key areas in which you want to achieve results and maintain standards—strategic planning, administrative support, staff development, market research, customer service, health, family, finances, home environment, spirituality, recreation, etc.

#### Step 4: Review from 30,000 Feet (One- to Two-Year Goals)

Consider what you want to be experiencing in the various areas one to two years from now. Meeting these goals may require shifts in emphasis and new areas of responsibility.

#### Step 5: Review from 40,000 Feet (Three- to Five-Year Vision)

Project three to five years into the future. Consider organization strategies, environmental trends, career and life-transition circumstances, technology, globalization, market trends, and competition.

#### Step 6: Review from 50,000+ Feet (Life)

Clarify your core purpose, answering the question 'Why?', and the primary reason that supersedes all others for both your company or your own life. This is your ultimate job description from which all goals, visions, objectives, projects, and actions derive.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Use the six-level model as a framework for evaluating your inventory of projects and commitments
- ✅ Review from higher altitudes when you need greater clarity; review from lower altitudes when you need more action
- ✅ Do not try to clarify fundamental purpose before getting your current actions under control—a bottom-up approach is usually more effective
- ✅ List and review your areas of responsibility to generate a more comprehensive framework for evaluating projects
- ✅ Notice when things feel unclear at one level—shift up or down the model to find the missing piece of planning

#### Keep In Mind

- "Setting priorities" in the traditional sense does not provide a practical framework for most day-to-day decisions
- Mastering the flow of your work at all six levels provides a holistic way to complete actions and feel good about it
- The altitude analogies are chosen for convenience—in real life, important conversations may not fit exactly to one horizon or another
- Most people are so embroiled in day-to-day commitments that their ability to focus on the larger horizon is seriously impaired; getting current on mundane "stuff" unlocks creative energy

#### Security & Safety Notes

- When reviewing work at the 40,000- and 50,000-foot levels, ensure that long-term strategic plans involving sensitive information are documented in secure systems
- Clarify purpose and principles at the start of any significant project to prevent uncontrolled expansion and unintended consequences

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Trying to work from the top down (life purpose → goals → actions) when you are too overwhelmed by day-to-day commitments to focus effectively.
  **Solution:** Use a bottom-up approach—get current on your runway-level actions first, then gradually broaden your horizons.

- **Problem:** Focusing only on big-picture goals and values while ignoring the mundane details that must be managed to achieve them.
  **Solution:** The GTD system bridges both levels—manage the details through horizontal workflow management while maintaining vertical focus on outcomes and purpose.
