# Chapter 12: CULTURE

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Let Culture Emerge Naturally, Don't Force It
**Context:** Culture is not something you create artificially with mission statements, policies, or events. It is the byproduct of consistent behavior over time.

**Examples:**
- "Instant cultures are artificial cultures. They're big bangs made of mission statements, declarations, and rules. They are obvious, ugly, and plastic. Artificial culture is paint. Real culture is patina."
- "You don't create a culture. It happens. This is why new companies don't have a culture. Culture is the byproduct of consistent behavior. If you encourage people to share, then sharing will be built into your culture. If you reward trust, then trust will be built in. If you treat customers right, then treating customers right becomes your culture."
- "Culture isn't a foosball table or trust falls. It isn't policy. It isn't the Christmas party or the company picnic. Those are objects and events, not culture. And it's not a slogan, either. Culture is action, not words."

**Steps:**
1. Stop trying to manufacture culture with rules, slogans, or events.
2. Focus on consistent daily behaviors you want to encourage.
3. Reward the behaviors you want repeated (e.g., sharing, trust, customer care).
4. Be patient—allow time for culture to develop naturally, like a fine scotch.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Consistently reinforce the actions you want to define your company.
- ✅ Reward trust, collaboration, and customer care in everyday operations.
- ✅ Be patient and give culture time to develop on its own.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Culture is action, not words or perks.
- Artificial culture is "paint"; real culture is "patina" that develops over time.
- New companies naturally lack culture—this is expected, not a problem to fix immediately.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Creating fake culture through mission statements, rules, or events.
  **Solution:** Focus on daily behaviors and let culture emerge as their byproduct.
- **Problem:** Trying to rush culture development.
  **Solution:** Be patient; real culture takes time and cannot be installed.

**Key Terms:**
- Patina: The natural, earned character of a culture that develops over time, as opposed to artificial paint.
- Byproduct of consistent behavior: Culture forms as a natural result of repeated actions and rewards, not through deliberate creation.

### Lesson 2: Treat Decisions as Temporary, Not Permanent
**Context:** Don't create problems you don't have. Optimize for the present and change decisions when circumstances change. The ability to pivot is a key advantage of being small.

**Examples:**
- "Don't make up problems you don't have yet. It's not a problem until it's a real problem. Most of the things you worry about never happen anyway."
- "Besides, the decisions you make today don't need to last forever. It's easy to shoot down good ideas, interesting policies, or worthwhile experiments by assuming that whatever you decide now needs to work for years on end. It's just not so, especially for a small business. If circumstances change, your decisions can change. Decisions are temporary."
- "At this stage, it's silly to worry about whether or not your concept will scale from five to five thousand people. Getting a product or service off the ground is hard enough without inventing even more obstacles. Optimize for now and worry about the future later."
- "The ability to change course is one of the big advantages of being small. Compared with larger competitors, you're way more capable of making quick, sweeping changes."

**Steps:**
1. Resist the urge to plan for distant hypothetical scenarios.
2. Make decisions based on current circumstances, not future scaling fears.
3. Revisit and revise decisions when conditions change.
4. Focus resources on getting the product/service off the ground.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Optimize for the present moment, not hypothetical futures.
- ✅ Embrace your ability to change course quickly as a competitive advantage.
- ✅ Avoid inventing obstacles before they exist.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Small businesses have a major advantage: they can make quick, sweeping changes.
- Worrying about scaling problems wastes energy, time, and money on issues that may never arise.
- Decisions should serve current needs, not distant hypothetical ones.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Killing good ideas because they don't seem scalable for a distant future.
  **Solution:** Evaluate ideas on their current merit and be ready to change them later if needed.
- **Problem:** Spending time planning for problems that may never materialize.
  **Solution:** Focus on real, present problems and trust that you can handle future ones when they arrive.

**Key Terms:**
- Temporary decisions: Decisions made for current circumstances that can be changed when conditions change.
- Scaling obsession: Prematurely worrying about growth from small to large scale before establishing a solid foundation.

### Lesson 3: Focus on the Environment, Not on Hiring "Rock Stars"
**Context:** Stop posting ads for "rock stars" or "ninjas." Instead, focus on creating an environment where regular people can do great work.

**Examples:**
- "A lot of companies post help-wanted ads seeking 'rock stars' or 'ninjas.' Lame. Unless your workplace is filled with groupies and throwing stars, these words have nothing to do with your business."
- "Instead of thinking about how you can land a roomful of rock stars, think about the room instead. We're all capable of bad, average, and great work. The environment has a lot more to do with great work than most people realize."
- "But there's a ton of untapped potential trapped under lame policies, poor direction, and stifling bureaucracies. Cut the crap and you'll find that people are waiting to do great work. They just need to be given the chance."
- "This isn't about casual Fridays or bring-your-dog-to-work day. Rockstar environments develop out of trust, autonomy, and responsibility. They're a result of giving people the privacy, workspace, and tools they deserve. Great environments show respect for the people who do the work and how they do it."

**Steps:**
1. Stop using "rock star" or "ninja" language in hiring and internal communications.
2. Audit your current work environment for policies, direction, or bureaucracy that stifle potential.
3. Remove obstacles that prevent people from doing great work.
4. Build trust, grant autonomy, and assign responsibility.
5. Provide the privacy, workspace, and tools people need to succeed.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Evaluate and improve the work environment rather than chasing superstars.
- ✅ Trust employees with autonomy and responsibility.
- ✅ Provide proper workspace, privacy, and tools.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Everyone has the capacity for bad, average, and great work—environment determines which emerges.
- Casual perks like casual Fridays are not the same as a rockstar environment.
- Untapped potential is often trapped by poor policies and stifling bureaucracy.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Wasting energy trying to hire "perfect" rock star employees while ignoring the environment.
  **Solution:** Create an environment where ordinary people can produce extraordinary work.
- **Problem:** Confusing superficial perks (dog-at-work day) with genuine trust and autonomy.
  **Solution:** Focus on trust, autonomy, responsibility, and proper resources—not gimmicks.

**Key Terms:**
- Rockstar environment: A workplace built on trust, autonomy, and responsibility that enables great work.
- Stifling bureaucracy: Excessive rules, policies, and red tape that prevent employees from doing their best work.

### Lesson 4: Treat Employees Like Adults, Not Children
**Context:** Micromanagement, constant approval requirements, and surveillance create a culture of nonthinkers. Trust your employees and treat them with respect.

**Examples:**
- "When you treat people like children, you get children's work. Yet that's exactly how a lot of companies and managers treat their employees. Employees need to ask permission before they can do anything. They need to get approval for every tiny expenditure."
- "When everything constantly needs approval, you create a culture of nonthinkers. You create a boss-versus-worker relationship that screams, 'I don't trust you.'"
- "What do you gain if you ban employees from, say, visiting a social-networking site or watching YouTube while at work? You gain nothing. That time doesn't magically convert to work."
- "People need diversions. It helps disrupt the monotony of the workday. A little YouTube or Facebook time never hurt anyone."
- "Then there's all the money and time you spend policing this stuff. How much does it cost to set up surveillance software? How much time do IT employees waste on monitoring other employees instead of working on a project that's actually valuable? How much time do you waste writing rule books that never get read?"

**Steps:**
1. Eliminate unnecessary approval processes for minor decisions.
2. Stop micromanaging employee internet usage and minor diversions.
3. Trust employees to manage their own time and priorities.
4. Allow reasonable diversions to prevent burnout.
5. Remove surveillance and policing mechanisms that signal distrust.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Give adults autonomy to make decisions without constant approval.
- ✅ Allow reasonable diversions (social media, etc.) during the workday.
- ✅ Trust employees with their time and output.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Treating adults like children produces childish work.
- Constant approval requirements create "nonthinkers," not engaged employees.
- Banning distractions does not magically convert time to productive work.
- Surveillance software and rule books are expensive wastes of time and money.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Implementing strict approval processes and surveillance out of distrust.
  **Solution:** Treat employees as adults and give them autonomy—the cost of distrust is far higher than the risk of occasional misuse.
- **Problem:** Banning personal internet use at work.
  **Solution:** Accept that people need diversions; time saved from policing is better spent on valuable work.

**Key Terms:**
- Culture of nonthinkers: A workplace where employees stop thinking for themselves because every decision requires approval.
- Boss-versus-worker relationship: An adversarial dynamic created when management shows it doesn't trust employees.

### Lesson 5: Send People Home at 5—Value Quality Hours Over Quantity
**Context:** Don't expect employees to work fourteen-hour days or have no life outside work. People with lives outside the office are more productive and efficient during work hours.

**Examples:**
- "The dream employee for a lot of companies is a twenty-something with as little of a life as possible outside of work—someone who'll be fine working fourteen-hour days and sleeping under his desk. But packing a room full of these burn-the-midnight-oil types isn't as great as it seems."
- "It lets you get away with lousy execution. It perpetuates myths like 'This is the only way we can compete against the big guys.' You don't need more hours; you need better hours."
- "When people have something to do at home, they get down to business. They get their work done at the office because they have somewhere else to be. They find ways to be more efficient because they have to. They need to pick up the kids or get to choir practice. So they use their time wisely."
- "As the saying goes, 'If you want something done, ask the busiest person you know.' You want busy people. People who have a life outside of work. People who care about more than one thing."

**Steps:**
1. Set reasonable work hours and encourage employees to leave on time.
2. Stop glorifying long hours and late nights.
3. Hire people who have full lives outside of work.
4. Focus on output and efficiency, not hours logged.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Value productivity and output over face time and hours worked.
- ✅ Hire people with rich lives outside of work.
- ✅ Set clear boundaries between work and personal time.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Long hours often hide lousy execution and poor planning.
- People with outside commitments are forced to be more efficient.
- Busy people with diverse interests tend to be more productive and engaged.
- Expecting the job to be someone's entire life leads to burnout and turnover.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Building a culture of overwork and glorifying long hours.
  **Solution:** Prioritize quality hours and efficiency; send people home at 5 and measure results, not hours.
- **Problem:** Hiring employees with no life outside work.
  **Solution:** Seek candidates with diverse interests—they bring energy and efficiency to the job.

**Key Terms:**
- Better hours: Productivity measured by output and efficiency, not by time spent at the office.
- Burn-the-midnight-oil types: Employees who work excessively long hours, often masking poor execution.

### Lesson 6: Don't Create Policies from One-Time Incidents
**Context:** Avoid the urge to create a new policy every time something goes wrong. Policies are overreactions to isolated incidents and are the building blocks of bureaucracy.

**Examples:**
- "The second something goes wrong, the natural tendency is to create a policy. 'Someone's wearing shorts!? We need a dress code!' No, you don't. You just need to tell John not to wear shorts again."
- "Policies are organizational scar tissue. They are codified overreactions to situations that are unlikely to happen again. They are collective punishment for the misdeeds of an individual."
- "This is how bureaucracies are born. No one sets out to create a bureaucracy. They sneak up on companies slowly. They are created one policy—one scar—at a time."
- "So don't scar on the first cut. Don't create a policy because one person did something wrong once. Policies are only meant for situations that come up over and over again."

**Steps:**
1. When an incident occurs, address it directly with the individual involved.
2. Ask: Is this a one-time event or a recurring pattern?
3. Only create a policy if the situation happens repeatedly.
4. Regularly audit existing policies and remove ones that are no longer necessary.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Address individual problems with individuals, not with blanket policies.
- ✅ Resist the urge to over-regulate based on single incidents.
- ✅ Regularly review and prune policies to prevent bureaucratic creep.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Bureaucracies don't start with grand plans—they accumulate one policy at a time.
- Policies are collective punishment for individual misdeeds.
- One bad apple does not justify a rule for the entire organization.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Creating a policy after a single incident to prevent recurrence.
  **Solution:** Handle the situation case-by-case until it becomes a genuine recurring pattern.
- **Problem:** Letting small policies accumulate into a bloated bureaucracy.
  **Solution:** Regularly audit and remove unnecessary policies before they become "scar tissue."

**Key Terms:**
- Organizational scar tissue: Policies that are overreactions to past incidents, often causing more harm than good.
- Bureaucratic creep: The gradual accumulation of rules and policies that slows down an organization.

### Lesson 7: Communicate Naturally—Sound Like Yourself
**Context:** Avoid corporate-speak, formal announcements, artificial friendliness, and legalese. Write and speak conversationally, as if talking to one specific person.

**Examples:**
- "What is it with businesspeople trying to sound big? The stiff language, the formal announcements, the artificial friendliness, the legalese, etc. You read this stuff and it sounds like a robot wrote it. These companies talk at you, not to you."
- "This mask of professionalism is a joke. We all know this. Yet small companies still try to emulate it. They think sounding big makes them appear bigger and more 'professional.' But it really just makes them sound ridiculous."
- "Plus, you sacrifice one of a small company's greatest assets: the ability to communicate simply and directly, without running every last word through a legal-and-PR-department sieve."
- "There's nothing wrong with sounding your own size. Being honest about who you are is smart business, too. Language is often your first impression—why start it off with a lie?"
- "Talk to customers the way you would to friends. Explain things as if you were sitting next to them. Avoid jargon or any sort of corporate-speak. Stay away from buzzwords when normal words will do just fine."
- "Don't talk about 'monetization' or being 'transparent;' talk about making money and being honest. Don't use seven words when four will do."
- "And don't force your employees to end e-mails with legalese like 'This e-mail message is for the sole use of the intended recipient(s) and may contain confidential and privileged information.' That's like ending all your company e-mails with a signature that says, 'We don't trust you and we're ready to prove it in court.'"
- "Write to be read, don't write just to write. Whenever you write something, read it out loud. Does it sound the way it would if you were actually talking to someone? If not, how can you make it more conversational?"
- "And when you're writing, don't think about all the people who may read your words. Think of one person. Then write for that one person. Writing for a mob leads to generalities and awkwardness."

**Steps:**
1. Write and speak conversationally, as if talking to a friend.
2. Replace jargon and buzzwords with plain, direct language.
3. Read your writing out loud to test for natural flow.
4. Imagine writing to one specific person rather than a faceless audience.
5. Remove unnecessary legalese and legal warnings from communications.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Talk to customers like you would to friends.
- ✅ Use simple, direct language without corporate filters.
- ✅ Read writing out loud to ensure it sounds natural.
- ✅ Write for one person, not a mob.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Small companies have an advantage: they can communicate simply and directly.
- Sounding "big" or "professional" often makes you sound ridiculous.
- Language is your first impression—start with honesty, not a mask.
- Buzzwords and legalese signal distrust and alienate people.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Using corporate-speak, buzzwords, and legalese to sound professional.
  **Solution:** Write and speak naturally; be honest about who you are and what you mean.
- **Problem:** Writing for a vague audience, resulting in generic and awkward content.
  **Solution:** Write for one specific person to hit the mark more accurately.

**Key Terms:**
- Corporate-speak: Artificial, formal, or overly professional language that sounds inauthentic.
- Legal-and-PR-department sieve: Over-filtering of natural language through legal and public relations concerns.
- Monetization/Transparency: Buzzwords that should be replaced with plain language like "making money" and "being honest."

### Lesson 8: Eliminate "Four-Letter Words" from Business Communication
**Context:** Avoid absolute language like "need," "must," "can't," "easy," "just," "only," and "fast." These words create black-and-white situations, introduce tension, and torpedo productive discussions.

**Examples:**
- "There are four-letter words you should never use in business. They're not fuck or shit. They're need, must, can't, easy, just, only, and fast."
- "These words get in the way of healthy communication. They are red flags that introduce animosity, torpedo good discussions, and cause projects to be late."
- "When you use these four-letter words, you create a black-and-white situation. But the truth is rarely black and white. So people get upset and problems ensue. Tension and conflict are injected unnecessarily."
- "Here's what's wrong with some of them:
  - Need: Very few things actually need to get done. Instead of saying 'need,' you're better off saying 'maybe' or 'What do you think about this?' or 'How does this sound?' or 'Do you think we could get away with that?'
  - Can't: When you say 'can't,' you probably can. Sometimes there are even opposing can'ts: 'We can't launch it like that, because it's not quite right' versus 'We can't spend any more time on this because we have to launch.' Both of those statements can't be true. Or wait a minute, can they?
  - Easy: Easy is a word that's used to describe other people's jobs. 'That should be easy for you to do, right?' But notice how rarely people describe their own tasks as easy. For you, it's 'Let me look into it'—but for others, it's 'Get it done.'"
- "These four-letter words often pop up during debates (and also watch out for their cousins: everyone, no one, always, and never). Once uttered, they make it tough to find a solution. They box you into a corner by pitting two absolutes against each other. That's when head-butting occurs."
- "And these words are especially dangerous when you string them together: 'We need to add this feature now. We can't launch without this feature. Everyone wants it. It's only one little thing so it will be easy. You should be able to get it in there fast!' Only thirty-six words, but a hundred assumptions. That's a recipe for disaster."

**Steps:**
1. Audit your vocabulary and identify instances of absolute language.
2. Replace "need" with softer alternatives: "maybe," "What do you think?" or "How does this sound?"
3. Reframe "can't" by exploring alternatives: ask "What if we could?" instead.
4. Stop calling others' work "easy"—describe your own tasks the same way.
5. Watch for cousins: "everyone," "no one," "always," and "never."
6. Never string these words together in a single statement.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Replace absolute language with collaborative, open-ended questions.
- ✅ Recognize that few things truly "need" to be done.
- ✅ Avoid describing others' work as "easy" when you wouldn't describe your own that way.
- ✅ Watch for words that box people into absolutes during debates.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Truth is rarely black and white, but these words create false binaries.
- Absolute language injects unnecessary tension and conflict.
- Combining multiple absolute words in one sentence is a recipe for disaster.
- These words are especially dangerous in debates, pitting absolutes against each other.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Using "need," "must," and "can't" to shut down discussion.
  **Solution:** Replace absolutes with questions and alternatives to keep dialogue open.
- **Problem:** Dismissing others' work as "easy" while treating your own with more nuance.
  **Solution:** Apply the same standard of complexity to all work—if yours isn't "easy," neither is theirs.

**Key Terms:**
- Four-letter words: Need, must, can't, easy, just, only, fast—absolute language that creates conflict.
- Cousins: Everyone, no one, always, never—related absolute terms to watch for in debates.
- Absolute language: Words that create false black-and-white situations and shut down collaboration.

### Lesson 9: Eliminate "ASAP" from Your Vocabulary
**Context:** Stop using "ASAP" (as soon as possible) because it makes everything appear high priority, which means nothing is truly prioritized. Reserve emergency language for true emergencies only.

**Examples:**
- "Stop saying ASAP. We get it. It's implied. Everyone wants things done as soon as they can be done."
- "When you turn into one of these people who adds ASAP to the end of every request, you're saying everything is high priority. And when everything is high priority, nothing is. (Funny how everything is a top priority until you actually have to prioritize things.)"
- "ASAP is inflationary. It devalues any request that doesn't say ASAP. Before you know it, the only way to get anything done is by putting the ASAP sticker on it."
- "Most things just don't warrant that kind of hysteria. If a task doesn't get done this very instant, nobody is going to die. Nobody's going to lose their job. It won't cost the company a ton of money. What it will do is create artificial stress, which leads to burnout and worse."
- "So reserve your use of emergency language for true emergencies. The kind where there are direct, measurable consequences to inaction. For everything else, chill out."

**Steps:**
1. Audit your communications and remove "ASAP" from routine requests.
2. Identify true emergencies—those with direct, measurable consequences to inaction.
3. Use clear, realistic timelines instead of urgency markers.
4. Reserve emergency language only for genuine crises.
5. Assess whether a task truly has life-or-death consequences before marking it urgent.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Use clear deadlines instead of "ASAP."
- ✅ Reserve emergency language for true emergencies with measurable consequences.
- ✅ Assess the actual cost of delay before labeling something urgent.
- ✅ Avoid creating artificial stress through inflated urgency.

**Keep In Mind:**
- When everything is high priority, nothing is.
- ASAP is inflationary—it devalues all requests over time.
- Most tasks do not have life-or-death consequences.
- Artificial urgency creates stress, burnout, and worse outcomes.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Defaulting to "ASAP" on every request.
  **Solution:** Use specific, realistic timelines and reserve "ASAP" for true emergencies.
- **Problem:** Creating a culture where urgency is meaningless because everything is urgent.
  **Solution:** Prioritize genuinely and communicate realistic expectations.

**Key Terms:**
- ASAP: A destructive urgency marker that devalues all requests when overused.
- Inflationary urgency: When constant use of emergency language reduces its meaning and effectiveness.
- True emergency: A situation with direct, measurable consequences to inaction, warranting genuine urgency.
