# Chapter 7: COMPETITORS

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Don't Copy — Understand Instead
**Context:** Copying in business is a formula for failure because it skips the understanding layer. Imitation may be helpful when learning, but in business, it produces a "faux finish" with no substance, no understanding, and nothing to base future decisions on. A copycat is always behind, never leading.

**Examples:**
- An art student replicating a museum painting is learning; a business copycat repurposing someone else's work without understanding the layers beneath is failing.
- "You just repurpose the last layer instead of understanding all the layers underneath."
- "The copy is a faux finish. It delivers no substance, no understanding, and nothing to base future decisions on."
- "You're always in a passive position. You never lead; you always follow."

**Steps:**
1. Before replicating anything, ask: "Do I understand why this works the way it does?"
2. Trace the decision layers beneath the surface — not just the visible output.
3. Use others' work for influence, not theft. If someone else is doing the bulk of the work, you are copying.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Be influenced, but don't steal.
- ✅ Focus on understanding the "why" behind a successful product or approach.
- ✅ Invest in discovering your own voice rather than replicating another's.

**Keep In Mind:**
- So much of the work an original creator puts into something is invisible and buried beneath the surface.
- A copycat can never keep up with the original because the copy is born already behind the times.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Mistaking copy-paste replication for learning or progress.
  **Solution:** Pause and interrogate why something works before adopting it; build from first principles.

**Key Terms:**
- **Faux finish:** A surface-level copy that lacks the substance and depth of the original.
- **Layers underneath:** The foundational reasoning, decisions, and context that produce the visible result.

### Lesson 2: Decommoditize Your Product
**Context:** When you are successful, people will try to copy you. The best defense is to make yourself — your unique thinking, values, and personality — part of your product or service. When you inject what is uniquely yours into what you sell, competitors cannot replicate it.

**Examples:**
- Zappos.com: A pair of sneakers is the same as any other retailer, but Zappos injects CEO Tony Hsieh's obsession with customer service — no scripts, call center in the same building as HQ, all employees spending four weeks answering phones and working in the warehouse.
- Polyface Farm: Joel Salatin's farm sells a way of thinking, not just chickens — grass-fed, no antibiotics, no shipping, open to visitors anytime. Customers drive 150 miles for "clean" meat.
- "Competitors can never copy the you in your product."

**Steps:**
1. Identify what is uniquely yours — your beliefs, obsessions, values, or personality.
2. Pour that uniqueness into every touchpoint: how you sell, how you support, how you explain, and how you deliver.
3. Make your product or service an expression of your distinctive perspective, not just a commodity.
4. Communicate that story clearly so customers understand what they are really buying.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Pour yourself into your product and everything around it.
- ✅ Make your service or product something no one else can offer because it is tied to who you are.
- ✅ Let customers experience your uniqueness through every interaction.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Decommoditization means your product becomes irreplaceable not because of features, but because of the person/values behind it.
- Competitors can copy your code, your features, or your pricing — but they cannot copy you.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Treating your product as a commodity and competing only on price or features.
  **Solution:** Anchor your offering in a personal or organizational value that is hard to replicate.

**Key Terms:**
- **Decommoditize:** To transform a generic, interchangeable product into something unique and irreplaceable by injecting personal identity, values, or philosophy.
- **Commodity:** A product or service that is indistinguishable from competitors' offerings, competing only on price.

### Lesson 3: Pick a Fight
**Context:** If you believe a competitor is wrong or bad, say so openly. Positioning yourself as the anti-[competitor] is a powerful way to differentiate yourself and attract followers. Having a clear enemy gives you a compelling story, ignites passion, and makes people take sides.

**Examples:**
- Dunkin' Donuts positions itself as the anti-Starbucks, mocking "Fritalian" terms and running taste-test campaigns with DunkinBeatStarbucks.com.
- Audi takes on "old luxury" brands (Rolls-Royce, Mercedes) and Lexus, mocking BMW drivers who use rearview mirrors to adjust hair.
- Apple's Mac vs. PC ads; 7UP as "the Uncola"; Under Armour as Nike for a new generation.
- Dyson's Airblade frames the entire hand-dryer industry as a failure.
- I Can't Believe It's Not Butter puts its enemy in the product name.

**Steps:**
1. Identify a competitor or industry practice you genuinely believe is failing customers.
2. Position your product as the alternative — the anti-[X].
3. Use humor, taste tests, comparisons, or direct comparisons in your messaging.
4. Lean into the conflict — people get stoked by it and take sides.
5. Communicate your stand consistently across marketing, product, and brand.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Be the anti-______ — fill in the blank with a real competitor or outdated industry norm.
- ✅ Let your messaging reflect genuine conviction; authenticity resonates.
- ✅ Use conflict as a storytelling device — it gives people a reason to care.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Having an enemy gives you a great story to tell customers.
- "Taking a stand always stands out."
- People get stoked by conflict, take sides, and passions are ignited — which drives attention and loyalty.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Picking a fight with a competitor you don't genuinely believe is worse, coming across as inauthentic.
  **Solution:** Choose targets based on real customer pain points you can credibly solve better.

**Key Terms:**
- **Anti-[competitor] positioning:** A branding strategy where you define yourself in opposition to a dominant competitor or industry norm.
- **Differentiation through conflict:** Using opposition to a competitor as the primary way to stand out in the market.

### Lesson 4: Underdo Your Competition
**Context:** The conventional "one-up" arms-race mindset — more features, more spending, more employees — is a dead end. It drains money, time, and drive, and forces you into a defensive, reactive posture. Instead, solve fewer problems better. Do less than your competitors and focus on simplicity.

**Examples:**
- Fixed-gear bicycles: While major brands pushed high-tech mountain bikes with suspension, disc brakes, and 21 gears, fixed-gear bikes boomed with just one gear and often no brakes — simpler, lighter, cheaper, less maintenance.
- The Flip camcorder: Deliberately omits big screen, photo ability, tapes/discs, menus, settings, video light, viewfinder, special effects, headphone jack, lens cap, memory card, and optical zoom. Wins fans because it only does a few simple things well.

**Steps:**
1. Identify the complex, expensive, or over-engineered problems your competitors are obsessed with solving.
2. Choose to solve only the simple, core problems really well.
3. Deliberately leave out features, leaving the "hairy, difficult, nasty problems" to the competition.
4. Market your simplicity as a virtue — highlight what you don't do as aggressively as competitors flaunt what they do.
5. Keep the product lightweight, easy to use, and accessible to people who would never use a complex alternative.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Practice "one-downing" instead of one-upping.
- ✅ Don't shy away from the fact that your product does less — sell it proudly.
- ✅ Focus on simplicity, ease of use, and accessibility over feature bloat.

**Keep In Mind:**
- "When you get suckered into an arms race, you wind up in a never-ending battle that costs you massive amounts of money, time, and drive."
- Defensive companies can't think ahead; they can only think behind. They don't lead; they follow.
- The Flip "goes places a bigger camera would never go and gets used by people who would never use a fancier camera."

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Feeling pressured to match every competitor feature out of fear of losing market share.
  **Solution:** Ruthlessly prioritize the core use case and let the feature checklist be your guide for what to exclude, not include.

**Key Terms:**
- **One-downing:** The strategy of deliberately doing less than competitors to win through simplicity rather than feature parity.
- **Arms race:** A competitive cycle where each player continuously adds more features, spending, or scale in response to rivals, leading to diminishing returns.
- **Underdoing:** Choosing to solve fewer problems better rather than trying to outdo competitors on every dimension.

### Lesson 5: Stop Obsessing Over the Competition — Focus on Yourself
**Context:** Worrying about competitors turns into an obsession, creates stress and anxiety, and dilutes your own vision. The competitive landscape is constantly changing and largely out of your control. Your energy is better spent improving your own product, vision, and execution. Building "the iPod killer" or "the next Pokemon" means you are letting the competition set the rules — and you cannot beat someone who is making the rules.

**Examples:**
- "What are they doing right now? Where are they going next? How should we react?" — this mindset leads to reaction rather than vision.
- "If you're planning to build 'the iPod killer' or 'the next Pokemon,' you're already dead. You're allowing the competition to set the parameters."
- "You wind up offering your competitor's products with a different coat of paint."

**Steps:**
1. Recognize when you are obsessing over competitors' moves.
2. Redirect that energy inward: focus on your own product, your own customers, and your own vision.
3. Redefine the rules of the game rather than trying to play by someone else's.
4. Ask: "If I merely replicate competitors, what is the point of my existence?"
5. Accept that it is better to go down fighting for what you believe in than to win by imitating others.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Focus on what is going on "in here" rather than "out there."
- ✅ Redefine the rules rather than trying to out-execute on someone else's terms.
- ✅ Guard your vision against dilution by competitors' ideas.

**Keep In Mind:**
- "Focus on competitors too much and you wind up diluting your own vision."
- "Your chances of coming up with something fresh go way down when you keep feeding your brain other people's ideas."
- You become reactionary instead of visionary when you live in competitors' shadows.
- Their profits and costs are theirs; yours are yours. It is not a win-or-lose battle.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Constantly analyzing every competitor move and letting it drive product decisions.
  **Solution:** Set strict boundaries on competitor research and redirect the majority of strategic thinking toward your own customers and roadmap.
- **Problem:** Defining success as "beating" a specific competitor.
  **Solution:** Measure success by your own progress, values, and customer impact — not by relative standing against another company.

**Key Terms:**
- **Reactionary:** Operating in response to competitors' moves rather than driving your own vision forward.
- **Visionary:** Leading by defining new rules and possibilities rather than following existing market frameworks.
- **Competitive landscape:** The ever-changing ecosystem of rivals and alternatives in a market.
