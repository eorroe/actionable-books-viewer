# Chapter 10: HIRING

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Do It Yourself First
**Context:** Before hiring anyone for a role, try doing the work yourself. This gives you the knowledge to write realistic job descriptions, ask relevant interview questions, and supervise effectively.

**Examples:**
- At 37signals, one of the founders spent a whole summer setting up servers before hiring a system administrator.
- For the first three years, one of the founders handled all customer support before hiring a dedicated support person.
- You may feel out of your element or feel like you "suck" at the work—that's normal and a signal to learn first before hiring.

**Steps:**
1. Attempt to perform the job yourself before posting any opening.
2. Observe what "a job well done" looks like.
3. Determine whether the work should be done full-time, part-time, outsourced, or kept in-house (keeping it in-house is preferable if possible).
4. Write the job description based on your firsthand experience.
5. Use your experience to formulate interview questions and know how to evaluate candidates.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Learn your way out of feeling inadequate rather than hiring your way out of it.
- ✅ Want to be intimately involved in all aspects of your business to avoid putting your fate solely in others' hands.
- ✅ Be a better manager because you've done the work yourself and know when to criticize and when to support.

**Keep In Mind:**
- The initial time you invest learning the job will be repaid many times over by the wisdom you gain.
- Hands-on knowledge protects you from dark unknowns where you depend entirely on others.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Hiring before understanding the work leads to unrealistic job descriptions and poor hiring decisions.
  **Solution:** Spend enough time doing the work yourself to know what excellence looks like and what the role truly requires.

**Key Terms:**
- Realistic job description: A job description based on firsthand knowledge of the work.
- Hands-on supervision: Managing people based on your own experience doing their job.

### Lesson 2: Hire When It Hurts
**Context:** Don't hire for pleasure or growth theater—hire only when there is genuine pain caused by having too much work.

**Examples:**
- When 37signals felt the weight of unmanageable workload, quality slipping, and tasks they physically couldn't handle anymore, they knew it was time to hire.
- If you lose someone, don't replace them immediately—see how long you can get by without that position and whether the work was truly necessary.

**Steps:**
1. Before hiring, ask: "What if we don't hire anyone?"
2. Evaluate whether the extra work is truly necessary or if it can be solved with a slice of software, a change of practice, or simply not doing it.
3. If someone leaves, see how long you can operate without replacing them—you may discover you don't need as many people as you thought.
4. Hire only when there is more work than you can handle for a sustained period and quality is slipping.
5. Recognize that the right time to hire is when you're hurting, not earlier.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Challenge every hiring request by asking if the work is truly necessary.
- ✅ Look for software or process solutions before adding headcount.
- ✅ Wait until you genuinely can't handle the workload anymore.

**Keep In Mind:**
- The right time to hire is when there's more work than you can handle for a sustained period, quality is slipping, and you physically can't do it anymore.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Hiring too early because it feels like growth, creating unnecessary overhead.
  **Solution:** Apply the "pain test"—if it doesn't hurt enough to make you unable to do quality work, don't hire yet.

**Key Terms:**
- Sustained period: A continuous timeframe where workload exceeds capacity, indicating real need.
- Growth theater: Hiring to give the appearance of growth without a genuine operational need.

### Lesson 3: Pass on Great People
**Context:** Resist the temptation to hire talented people when you don't have a real need for them. Inventing roles to attract great people causes more harm than good.

**Examples:**
- Some companies hear about someone great and invent a position or title just to lure them in, where they sit parked doing work that isn't important.
- Having more people than you need leads to inventing work to keep everyone busy, which creates artificial projects, real costs, and complexity.
- It's worse to have talented people on staff with nothing meaningful to do than to miss out on a "great catch."

**Steps:**
1. Before hiring, confirm there is a genuine, current need for the role.
2. If there is no real work for someone, decline to hire them—even if they are exceptional.
3. Resist the urge to create roles or invent work just to justify a hire.
4. Trust that when you have a real need, you will find the right person—there is plenty of talent out there.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Greatness doesn't matter if there's no meaningful work for someone to do.
- ✅ Avoid the fear of missing out on talent—focus on current needs, not potential future needs.
- ✅ Protect your company from artificial work and complexity.

**Keep In Mind:**
- "Great has nothing to do with it. If you don't need someone, you don't need someone."
- There is plenty of talent out there; when a real need arises, you will find someone who fits.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Hiring great people without meaningful work, leading to busywork, artificial projects, and organizational complexity.
  **Solution:** Pass on hires when there is no genuine need, regardless of how talented the candidate is.

**Key Terms:**
- Artificial work: Made-up tasks created to keep overstaffed employees busy rather than serving real business needs.
- Artificial projects: Projects created from artificial work that generate real costs without real value.

### Lesson 4: Avoid the "Strangers at a Cocktail Party" Problem
**Context:** Rapidly hiring many people creates a culture of strangers where politeness replaces honest debate, and bad ideas go unchallenged.

**Examples:**
- A cocktail party full of strangers produces dull, stiff conversation with small talk about the weather, sports, or TV shows—no serious debate or controversy.
- A small, intimate dinner among old friends generates genuinely interesting conversations and heated debates where you leave feeling you actually got something out of it.
- Rapid hiring leads to everyone being unfailingly polite, avoiding conflict or drama, and no one saying "This idea sucks."
- Appeasement leads to products that don't offend anyone but also don't make anyone fall in love.

**Steps:**
1. Hire slowly—only bring on new people when you truly need them and can integrate them well.
2. Build an environment where people feel safe enough to be honest when things get tough.
3. Cultivate relationships where you know how far you can push someone and what people really mean when they say something.
4. Prioritize depth of relationships over breadth of headcount.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Hire slowly to avoid winding up at a cocktail party of strangers.
- ✅ Create a culture where honest debate and criticism are safe and expected.
- ✅ Encourage people to challenge ideas rather than appease.

**Keep In Mind:**
- You need an environment where everyone feels safe enough to be honest, even when things get tough.
- Appeasement leads to mediocrity—products that don't offend anyone but don't inspire anyone either.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Rapid hiring creates a culture of strangers who avoid conflict, leading to unchallenged bad ideas and mediocre output.
  **Solution:** Hire slowly, prioritize cultural integration, and explicitly build a culture of honest debate.

**Key Terms:**
- Cocktail party problem: The organizational dysfunction caused by rapid hiring, resulting in polite but unproductive cultures where honest debate is avoided.

### Lesson 5: Resumes Are Ridiculous
**Context:** Resumes are unreliable, easily gamed, and filled with meaningless jargon. They are a poor signal of whether a candidate truly cares about your company.

**Examples:**
- Resumes are filled with "action verbs" that don't mean anything and list job titles and responsibilities that are vaguely accurate at best.
- Anyone can create a decent-enough resume and shotgun it out to hundreds of companies—this is just spam.
- If someone sends a resume to three hundred companies, that's a huge red flag that they haven't researched your company or know what's different about it.
- A cover letter reveals someone's actual voice and shows whether it's in tune with your company—no one can churn out hundreds of personalized letters.

**Steps:**
1. Focus on cover letters rather than resumes.
2. Evaluate the first paragraph—if it "sucks," the second has to work that much harder.
3. Look for a hook in the first three paragraphs—if there's none, there's likely no real match.
4. Trust your gut reaction to the cover letter.
5. If your gut says there's a chance at a real match, move to the interview stage.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Use cover letters as your primary filter—they reveal voice, communication skills, and genuine interest.
- ✅ Treat generic, mass-applied resumes as a red flag.
- ✅ Trust your instinct about whether someone's communication style fits your company.

**Keep In Mind:**
- You want a specific candidate who cares specifically about your company, your products, your customers, and your job—not just any job.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Hiring based on resumes leads to selecting candidates who are good at resume writing but not necessarily good at the job or invested in your company.
  **Solution:** Prioritize cover letters as a better test of genuine interest and communication ability.

**Key Terms:**
- Cover letter: A personalized communication from a candidate that reveals their actual voice and specific interest in your company.
- Shotgunning: Mass-applying to hundreds of jobs without research, a sign of low genuine interest.

### Lesson 6: Years of Experience Are Overrated
**Context:** Requiring a specific number of years of experience is a poor hiring criterion. After six months to a year, the curve flattens and individual qualities matter more.

**Examples:**
- Job ads that say "Five years of experience required" give you a number but tell you nothing about actual capability.
- There's surprisingly little difference between a candidate with six months of experience and one with six years.
- The real difference comes from an individual's dedication, personality, and intelligence—not how long they've been doing it.
- It's murky and unverifiable what "five years of experience" actually means—someone could have dabbled for a few weekends years ago.

**Steps:**
1. Require only a baseline level of experience (six months to a year) to ensure candidates understand the domain and tools.
2. After that baseline, de-emphasize years of experience in your evaluation.
3. Focus on how well someone has been doing their work, not how long they've been doing it.
4. Evaluate dedication, personality, and intelligence instead of years on a resume.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Use a reasonable baseline (6 months to 1 year) to filter out complete beginners.
- ✅ Judge candidates by the quality of their work and their personal attributes rather than tenure.
- ✅ Question how you would even verify years-of-experience claims.

**Keep In Mind:**
- "How long someone's been doing it is overrated. What matters is how well they've been doing it."

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Over-reliance on years of experience filters out talented candidates who may have grown faster or learned more intensely in less time.
  **Solution:** Replace years-of-experience requirements with evaluations of actual capability, dedication, and fit.

**Key Terms:**
- Experience curve flattening: The phenomenon where capability growth slows significantly after the first year, making additional years less meaningful as a predictor of performance.

### Lesson 7: Forget About Formal Education
**Context:** Educational credentials—degrees, GPA, specific schools—are poor predictors of ability and can exclude excellent candidates.

**Examples:**
- 90% of CEOs currently heading the top 500 American companies did not receive undergraduate degrees from Ivy League colleges.
- More CEOs received their undergraduate degrees from the University of Wisconsin than from Harvard (the most heavily represented Ivy school, with nine CEOs).
- There are plenty of intelligent people who don't excel in the classroom.
- Too much time in academia can be harmful—people unlearn bad habits like making documents longer to matter more, using stiff formal tone, or prioritizing format over content.
- The pool of great candidates is far bigger than just people with college degrees and stellar GPAs.

**Steps:**
1. Remove educational requirements from your job postings unless absolutely necessary.
2. Consider candidates who are dropouts, had low GPAs, attended community college, or only finished high school.
3. Don't assume someone from a "top" school will automatically be better than someone without formal credentials.
4. Evaluate based on actual skills, work, and thinking rather than academic pedigree.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Consider dropouts, low-GPA candidates, community college students, and high school graduates.
- ✅ Avoid filtering based on Ivy League schools or specific degree requirements.
- ✅ Recognize that academia can teach bad habits that hurt business performance.

**Keep In Mind:**
- "I have never let my schooling interfere with my education." —Mark Twain
- Many skills useful in academia aren't worth much outside of it.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Using educational requirements as a lazy filter excludes excellent candidates who learn differently or don't fit the academic mold.
  **Solution:** Focus on actual ability, portfolio, and demonstrated work rather than credentials.

**Key Terms::
- Academic pedigree: A candidate's educational background, including school, degree, and GPA—an overrated hiring filter.
- Ivy League filter: The practice of only considering candidates from elite schools, which excludes a large pool of capable people.

### Lesson 8: Everybody Works
**Context:** On a small team, every person must be doing actual work. Avoid hiring delegators who tell others what to do instead of producing themselves.

**Examples:**
- Delegators love telling others what to do and are dead weight for a small team.
- Delegators clog the pipes for others by coming up with busywork.
- When delegators run out of work to assign, they make up more regardless of whether it needs to be done.
- Meetings are a delegator's best friend—they get to seem important while pulling everyone else away from real work.

**Steps:**
1. During hiring, evaluate whether a candidate is a doer or a delegator.
2. Avoid people who love telling others what to do rather than doing work themselves.
3. Ensure every person you hire will be producing directly, not just managing others' output.
4. Be wary of candidates who seem eager to pull people into meetings as their primary mode of contribution.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Hire people who do work, not those who delegate work.
- ✅ In a small team, everyone must be above the work—no one can be above it.
- ✅ Avoid meetings as a primary output—meetings should be the exception, not the rule.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Delegators create busywork that clogs the pipes for productive team members.
- In small teams, you can't afford overhead in the form of people who manage rather than produce.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Hiring delegators who appear senior but don't contribute real work, draining productivity from the rest of the team.
  **Solution:** Explicitly look for people who produce and avoid those whose primary value seems to be assigning tasks to others.

**Key Terms:**
- Delegator: A person who prefers telling others what to do rather than doing work themselves—dead weight for small teams.
- Busywork: Made-up tasks created by delegators to justify their presence and keep others occupied without adding real value.

### Lesson 9: Hire Managers of One
**Context:** Look for people who are self-directed—they set their own goals, execute them, and don't need daily check-ins or heavy direction.

**Examples:**
- Managers of one come up with their own goals and execute them without needing heavy direction.
- They do what a manager would do—set the tone, assign items, determine what needs to get done—but they do it for themselves.
- They free you from oversight and set their own direction.
- When left alone, they surprise you with how much they've gotten done.
- Look at their backgrounds—they have set the tone for how they've worked at other jobs, run something on their own, or launched a kind of project.
- You want someone capable of building something from scratch and seeing it through.

**Steps:**
1. Evaluate whether candidates have historically been self-directed in their careers.
2. Look for evidence they have run something on their own or launched a project independently.
3. Ask about how they've set goals and managed their own work in previous roles.
4. Prioritize candidates who don't need daily check-ins or heavy direction.
5. Give autonomy to people who have demonstrated they can handle it.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Look for people who have built something from scratch and seen it through.
- ✅ Hire people who set their own direction and surprise you with results when left alone.
- ✅ Managers of one free the rest of your team to work more and manage less.

**Keep In Mind:**
- You want someone capable of building something from scratch and seeing it through.
- Managers of one don't need hand-holding or supervision—they deliver results independently.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Hiring people who need constant direction and oversight, which creates management overhead and slows the team down.
  **Solution:** Look for a track record of self-direction and independent execution—prioritize managers of one.

**Key Terms:**
- Manager of one: A self-directed employee who sets their own goals, executes without heavy oversight, and acts as their own manager.
- Self-direction: The ability to set goals and execute them without needing daily check-ins or constant supervision.

### Lesson 10: Hire Great Writers
**Context:** When deciding among final candidates, hire the best writer regardless of role. Writing ability is a proxy for clear thinking and strong communication.

**Examples:**
- Great writers know how to communicate and make things easy to understand.
- They can put themselves in someone else's shoes.
- They know what to omit.
- Writing is today's currency for good ideas—look at how much communication happens via email, text, instant messaging, and blogging rather than phone calls.
- These qualities—communication, empathy, clarity, and editing—are valuable in any role: marketer, salesperson, designer, programmer, or otherwise.

**Steps:**
1. Among your top candidates, compare their writing samples or writing ability.
2. Choose the best writer, regardless of their specific role or title.
3. Evaluate whether their writing demonstrates clear thinking, empathy, and the ability to make complex things simple.
4. Trust that good writing will pay off in every aspect of their work.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ When in doubt between candidates, hire the best writer.
- ✅ Clear writing signals clear thinking.
- ✅ Great communicators make everything easier for the team and customers.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Being a good writer is about more than writing—it's about clear thinking, communication, empathy, and knowing what to omit.
- "Writing is today's currency for good ideas."

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Hiring a technically skilled candidate who can't communicate clearly, leading to misunderstandings, poor documentation, and team friction.
  **Solution:** Make writing ability a decisive factor—prioritize the best writer when candidates are otherwise comparable.

**Key Terms:**
- Clear writing: Writing that demonstrates clear thinking and makes complex ideas easy to understand.
- Writing as currency: The idea that strong writing skills are essential for sharing and executing good ideas in modern business.

### Lesson 11: The Best Are Everywhere (Hire Remotely)
**Context:** Don't limit your hiring to your geographic location. Technology makes remote collaboration easy, and the best talent is distributed globally.

**Examples:**
- 37signals is headquartered in Chicago, but more than half their team lives elsewhere—in Spain, Canada, Idaho, Oklahoma, and more.
- Had they limited their search to Chicago, they would have missed out on half their great people.
- Geography doesn't matter anymore—hire the best talent regardless of where it is.

**Steps:**
1. Open your hiring search to candidates anywhere, not just your local area.
2. Establish at least 2-4 hours of real-time overlap between team members in different time zones.
3. If there's no natural overlap, someone may need to shift hours slightly so they're available at the same time.
4. Meet in person at least every few months to review progress, plan for the future, and reconnect on a personal level.
5. Make sure the whole team gets together a few times a year.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Hire the best talent regardless of geography.
- ✅ Maintain 2-4 hours of real-time overlap daily for remote teams.
- ✅ Meet in person every few months to strengthen relationships and align on goals.
- ✅ Prefer not having complete overlap—alone time boosts productivity.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Technology has made it easy to bring distributed teams together online.
- You don't need eight hours of overlap—2-4 hours is plenty, and incomplete overlap gives people valuable alone time.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Limiting hiring to a single city or country severely restricts your talent pool and may cause you to miss out on your best candidates.
  **Solution:** Cast a global net, establish reasonable overlap windows, and schedule regular in-person gatherings.

**Key Terms:**
- Real-time overlap: The daily window when remote team members are simultaneously working and available for live collaboration.
- Distributed team: A team whose members work from different geographic locations, connected through technology.

### Lesson 12: Test-Drive Employees
**Context:** Interviews alone aren't enough to evaluate whether someone will actually perform well. The best way to assess a candidate is to see them work.

**Examples:**
- Some people sound like pros in interviews but don't work like pros.
- Hire candidates for a mini-project of just 20-40 hours to see how they make decisions, if you get along, what questions they ask, and what kind of work they produce.
- You can make up a fake project for the test-drive.
- BMW built a simulated assembly line where candidates get 90 minutes to perform a variety of work-related tasks.
- Cessna uses a role-playing exercise simulating the day of an executive—candidates work through memos, deal with phony irate customers, and handle other problems. Cessna has hired over 100 people using this simulation.
- When you get into a real work environment, the truth comes out.

**Steps:**
1. After initial screening, offer candidates a paid mini-project lasting 20-40 hours (or longer).
2. Observe how they make decisions, what questions they ask, and how they interact with the team.
3. Judge them by their actions rather than just their words.
4. If needed, create a simulated or fake project that mirrors real work.
5. Use simulations or role-playing exercises that mimic actual job tasks.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Evaluate current work ability, not past claims on a resume.
- ✅ Pay for test projects—compensate candidates for their time.
- ✅ Use realistic simulations that mirror actual job responsibilities.
- ✅ Judge by actions, not words.

**Keep In Mind:**
- "When you get into a real work environment, the truth comes out."
- It's one thing to look at a portfolio or conduct an interview—it's another to actually work with someone.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Hiring based on interviews and resumes alone, only to discover the candidate doesn't perform well in actual work.
  **Solution:** Always test-drive candidates with real or simulated work before making a final hiring decision.

**Key Terms:**
- Test-drive: A short-term, paid work engagement where a candidate performs actual or simulated tasks to demonstrate their abilities.
- Mini-project: A small-scale work assignment (20-40 hours) used to evaluate a candidate's real-world performance before a full hire.
- Simulation: A structured exercise that mimics real job tasks or scenarios to assess candidate capability.
