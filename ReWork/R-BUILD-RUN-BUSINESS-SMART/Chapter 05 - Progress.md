# Chapter 5: PROGRESS

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Embrace Constraints
**Context:** Constraints are advantages in disguise. Limited resources force creativity and eliminate waste. Instead of complaining about lack of time, money, people, or experience, use what you have to innovate.

**Examples:**
- Prisoners making weapons from soap or spoons
- Shakespeare writing sonnets within strict structural rules (14-line poems in iambic pentameter with specific rhyme schemes)
- Haiku and limericks with strict rules that produce creative results
- Hemingway and Carver using simple, clear language for maximum impact
- "The Price Is Right" game show using one simple formula ("How much does this item cost?") for 30+ years
- Southwest Airlines flying only Boeing 737s, allowing any crew to work any flight and all parts fitting all planes

**Steps:**
1. Stop complaining about insufficient resources
2. Identify what you do have
3. Force yourself to make do with existing assets
4. Look for creative workarounds

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ View constraints as advantages, not disadvantages
- ✅ Use limited resources to eliminate waste and force creativity
- ✅ Accept that less is often better

**Keep In Mind:**
- Constraints force you to be creative with what you have
- There's no room for waste when resources are limited
- You'll be amazed at what you can make with just a little creativity

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Complaining about insufficient time, money, people, or experience
  **Solution:** Reframe the constraint as an opportunity and focus on what you do have

**Key Terms:**
- **Constraints:** Limitations in resources (time, money, people, experience) that force creative problem-solving
- **Advantages in disguise:** The concept that limitations can lead to better outcomes than unlimited resources

### Lesson 2: Build Half a Product, Not a Half-Assed Product
**Context:** Trying to do too many things at once results in a mediocre product. You have limited time, resources, ability, and focus. Sacrifice some ideas for the greater good and focus on excellence in fewer areas.

**Examples:**
- Directors cut good scenes to make a great movie
- Musicians drop good tracks to make a great album
- Writers eliminate good pages to make a great book
- The authors cut their book from 57,000 words to about 27,000 words between the next-to-last and final drafts

**Steps:**
1. Identify all the great ideas you want to include
2. Cut your ambition in half
3. Remove anything that's merely "good" to focus on what's great
4. Remember you can always add things back later

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Focus on doing one thing well rather than ten things poorly
- ✅ Sacrifice some "darlings" for the greater good
- ✅ Cut features to a minimum to prevent bloat
- ✅ Start chopping—getting to great starts by cutting out stuff that's merely good

**Keep In Mind:**
- Most great ideas won't seem all that great once you get perspective
- Great ideas can always be done later
- You're better off with a kick-ass half than a half-assed whole

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Trying to implement too many features at once, resulting in a bloated, mediocre product
  **Solution:** Ruthlessly cut features and focus only on what's essential; remember you can always add back later

**Key Terms:**
- **Half-assed product:** A product that tries to do too much and ends up doing nothing well
- **Kick-ass half:** A focused product that excels at its core function

### Lesson 3: Start at the Epicenter
**Context:** When starting anything new, focus on the stuff you HAVE to do first, not what you could do or want to do. Identify the core element that defines your business and focus all energy on making it the best it can be.

**Examples:**
- Hot dog stand example: The hot dog is the epicenter; everything else (condiments, cart, name, decoration) is secondary
- If you took away the hot dogs, it's not a hot dog stand

**Steps:**
1. List everything you could do, want to do, and have to do
2. Identify the stuff you HAVE to do
3. Ask: "If I took this away, would what I'm selling still exist?"
4. The element that can't be removed is your epicenter
5. Focus all your energy on making the epicenter the best it can be
6. Build everything else around that foundation

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Start with what you MUST do, not what you COULD or WANT to do
- ✅ Identify the irreducible core of your offering
- ✅ Focus energy on making the epicenter excellent first

**Keep In Mind:**
- Everything else you do depends on the epicenter foundation
- You can take away many things, but the epicenter must remain
- If you can continue without it, it's not the epicenter

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Getting distracted by secondary features and details before perfecting the core offering
  **Solution:** Use the "if I took this away" test to identify and focus on the epicenter first

**Key Terms:**
- **Epicenter:** The core, irreducible element of your offering that cannot be removed without destroying the business
- **Irreducible core:** The essential component that defines what you're selling

### Lesson 4: Ignore the Details Early On
**Context:** Details matter, but focusing on them too early leads to disagreement, meetings, delays, and wasted time on decisions that will change anyway. Nail the basics first and worry about specifics later.

**Examples:**
- Architects don't worry about which tiles go in the shower or which brand of dishwasher to install until after the floor plan is finalized
- Using a big, thick Sharpie instead of a fine-point pen for initial sketches to avoid getting lost in details
- Walt Stanchfield encouraging Disney animators to "forget the detail" at first because detail doesn't buy anything in early stages

**Steps:**
1. Sketch big shapes, lines, and boxes first (use thick markers)
2. Avoid fine-resolution tools that encourage premature detail
3. Focus only on the big picture in the beginning
4. Wait until you start building to recognize which details matter most
5. Pay attention to details only after the basics are nailed down

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Use low-resolution tools (like Sharpies) in early design phases
- ✅ Nail the basics before worrying about specifics
- ✅ Delay decisions that can change later
- ✅ Recognize that you often can't identify important details until after you start building

**Keep In Mind:**
- Getting infatuated with details early leads to wasted time on decisions that will change
- You feel what's missing only after you start building
- Detail just doesn't buy you anything in the early stages

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Wasting time on minor details that will change anyway, causing delays and disagreement
  **Solution:** Use thick markers and focus only on big shapes; wait until after the foundation is set to drill into specifics

**Key Terms:**
- **Low-resolution sketching:** Using broad strokes and big shapes to focus on the big picture without getting lost in details
- **Big picture thinking:** Focusing on overall structure and direction before fine-tuning specifics

### Lesson 5: Making the Call is Making Progress
**Context:** Decisions are progress. Postponing decisions creates piles of unresolved problems. Get into the rhythm of making choices—each decision is a brick in your foundation. Don't wait for the perfect solution; decide and move forward.

**Examples:**
- 37signals avoided creating an affiliate program because the "perfect" solution seemed too complicated (automate payments, mail checks, figure out foreign tax laws)
- Breakthrough came when they asked: "What can we easily do right now that's good enough?"
- Solution: Pay affiliates in credit instead of cash initially, then eventually implement a cash payment system

**Steps:**
1. Swap "Let's think about it" for "Let's decide on it"
2. Don't wait for the perfect solution—good enough is often better
3. Make decisions quickly to build momentum
4. Accept that you can correct mistakes later
5. Build on "Done" rather than "We'll decide later"

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Get into the rhythm of making decision after decision
- ✅ Build momentum by making choices quickly
- ✅ Accept that good enough now is better than perfect later
- ✅ Remember you can correct decisions later if needed

**Keep In Mind:**
- Decisions are bricks in your foundation
- Long projects zap morale
- You're as likely to make a great call today as you are tomorrow
- You don't have to live with a decision forever

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Postponing decisions hoping for a perfect answer that will never come
  **Solution:** Ask "What can we easily do right now that's good enough?" and commit to deciding

**Key Terms:**
- **Decision pile-up:** The accumulation of unresolved decisions that lead to ignored, hastened, or discarded problems
- **Good enough:** A pragmatic approach where a functional solution now beats a perfect solution later

### Lesson 6: Be a Curator
**Context:** A great museum isn't made by putting everything in one room. What makes it great is what's NOT on the walls. Constantly look for things to remove, simplify, and streamline. Stick to what's truly essential.

**Examples:**
- Zingerman's deli curates its shelves by personally trying products and only carrying what they believe is great quality
- The owner of Zingerman's describes Pasolivo Olive Oil not based on packaging or marketing, but on actual taste and quality

**Steps:**
1. Constantly evaluate what you're offering or doing
2. Look for things to remove, simplify, and streamline
3. Pare things down until you're left with only the most important stuff
4. Repeat the process—keep curating
5. You can always add stuff back in later if needed

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Be a curator—make conscious decisions about what stays and what goes
- ✅ Focus on quality over quantity
- ✅ Pare down repeatedly until only the essential remains
- ✅ Personal vetting is crucial—know your products/services deeply

**Keep In Mind:**
- There's a lot more stuff off the walls than on the walls
- The best is a sub-sub-subset of all possibilities
- It's the stuff you leave out that matters
- You can always add stuff back in later

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Hoarding every idea, feature, or product without curating, resulting in a warehouse rather than a museum
  **Solution:** Actively look for things to remove and focus only on what's truly essential

**Key Terms:**
- **Curator:** Someone who makes conscious decisions about what should stay and what should go
- **Sub-sub-subset:** The very best selection from a much larger pool of possibilities

### Lesson 7: Throw Less at the Problem
**Context:** When things aren't working, the natural inclination is to throw more at the problem (more people, time, money). This makes the problem bigger. The right approach is the opposite: cut back. Doing less often produces better results.

**Examples:**
- Gordon Ramsay's Kitchen Nightmares: failing restaurants offer 30+ dishes; Ramsay trims to around 10 and polishes what's left
- Improving the current menu doesn't come first—trimming it down does

**Steps:**
1. When something isn't working, resist adding more resources
2. Cut back instead
3. Trim your offerings or approach
4. Polish what remains
5. Make tough calls to sort out what truly matters

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ When projects struggle, subtract rather than add
- ✅ Trim menus, features, or scope first before trying to improve
- ✅ Make tough calls to identify what truly matters
- ✅ Do less—your project won't suffer as much as you fear

**Keep In Mind:**
- Adding more people, time, and money just makes the problem bigger
- Trimming down comes before improving what's left
- You'll be forced to make tough calls that clarify priorities
- If you start pushing back deadlines and increasing budgets, you'll never stop

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Trying to fix a failing project by throwing more resources at it
  **Solution:** Cut back and simplify; trim the scope first, then polish what remains

**Key Terms:**
- **Trim the menu:** Reducing offerings to focus on quality rather than quantity
- **Scope reduction:** Intentionally cutting features or requirements to improve overall quality

### Lesson 8: Focus on What Won't Change
**Context:** Many companies chase the next big thing, trends, and technology. This is a fool's path. Build your business around things that people will want today and ten years from now. Fashion fades away; permanent features never go out of style.

**Examples:**
- Amazon focuses on fast/free shipping, great selection, friendly return policies, and affordable prices—things always in demand
- Japanese automakers focus on reliability, affordability, and practicality—desired 30 years ago, today, and 30 years from now
- 37signals focuses on speed, simplicity, ease of use, and clarity—timeless desires

**Steps:**
1. Identify what people will always want, regardless of trends
2. Focus your business on these permanent features
3. Build your core around timeless principles rather than temporary fads
4. Invest in things that won't change

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Focus on substance, not fashion
- ✅ Build around permanent features rather than trends
- ✅ Invest in things people will want today and ten years from now
- ✅ Avoid chasing the next big thing

**Keep In Mind:**
- Fashion fades away
- People won't wake up in ten years wishing software was harder to use
- People won't wish applications were slower
- Permanent features never go out of style

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Chasing trends and the "next big thing" instead of focusing on timeless value
  **Solution:** Build your core around things that people will want today and in the future

**Key Terms:**
- **Permanent features:** Aspects of a product or service that remain valuable over long periods
- **Timeless desires:** Fundamental human wants and needs that don't change with technology or trends

### Lesson 9: Tone Is in Your Fingers
**Context:** Fancy tools and gear don't make you better—the skill comes from you. People obsess over tools, software tricks, scaling issues, and office space instead of what really matters: getting customers and making money. Use what you have or can afford cheaply, then go.

**Examples:**
- Guitarists: Eddie Van Halen sounds like Eddie Van Halen on any guitar; the tone comes from the player, not the gear
- Golf: Give Tiger Woods cheap clubs and he'll still beat you
- Designers using fancy typefaces and Photoshop filters without anything to say
- Amateur photographers debating film vs. digital instead of focusing on what makes a photograph great
- Business people obsessing over tools, fancy office space, and lavish furniture

**Steps:**
1. Stop obsessing over tools, equipment, and software
2. Use whatever you already have or can afford cheaply
3. Focus on developing your skills and what you have to say
4. Prioritize getting customers and making money over gear

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Use what you've got or can afford cheaply
- ✅ Focus on the content, not the equipment
- ✅ Develop your skills rather than buying shortcuts
- ✅ Prioritize substance over style

**Keep In Mind:**
- Your tone is in your fingers, not your gear
- People use equipment as a crutch to avoid putting in the work
- You don't need the best gear in the world to be good or to get started
- What really matters is how to actually get customers and make money

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Obsessing over tools, fancy equipment, and software instead of focusing on execution and value
  **Solution:** Start with what you have; focus on developing your skills and delivering real value

**Key Terms:**
- **Tone:** The quality of your work that comes from your skill and experience, not your tools
- **Gear obsession:** The tendency to focus on equipment and tools rather than execution and results

### Lesson 10: Sell Your By-Products
**Context:** When you make something, you always make something else. Everything has a by-product. Observant and creative business minds spot these by-products and turn waste into profit.

**Examples:**
- Lumber industry selling sawdust, chips, and shredded wood (by-products) for profit in synthetic fireplace logs, concrete, mulch, particleboard, fuel, etc.
- 37signals' book "Getting Real" was a by-product of building a company and software; made over $1 million directly and another $1 million indirectly
- Wilco filmed their album creation process and released it as a documentary "I Am Trying to Break Your Heart"
- Henry Ford turned wood scraps from Model T production into charcoal briquets, creating Kingsford Charcoal
- The book "Rework" itself is a by-product

**Steps:**
1. Recognize that everything you do produces by-products
2. Identify what waste or by-products your business creates
3. Ask: "What haven't we thought about that we could sell?"
4. Repackage by-products into sellable products or services
5. Look for opportunities in what others consider waste

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Look for by-products in everything you do
- ✅ Consider what knowledge, content, or materials you're already creating
- ✅ Repurpose waste into revenue streams
- ✅ Think creatively about unexpected products you could offer

**Keep In Mind:**
- You can't make just one thing—everything has a by-product
- Software companies don't usually think about writing books, but they could
- There's probably something you haven't thought about that you could sell
- By-products can be more valuable than the core product

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Overlooking valuable by-products because you're too focused on the main product
  **Solution:** Regularly audit your processes for waste and by-products that could be turned into opportunities

**Key Terms:**
- **By-product:** Something produced in the process of making something else, often overlooked as waste
- **Waste-to-wealth:** The practice of identifying value in by-products and turning them into profitable products

### Lesson 11: Launch Now
**Context:** Your product or service is finished a lot sooner than you think. Once it does what it needs to do, get it out there. Don't hold everything up because of a few leftovers. You can do them later, and doing them later may mean doing them better.

**Examples:**
- 37signals launched Basecamp without billing capability—they knew they had a 30-day gap to figure it out
- They used the pre-launch time to solve more urgent problems that mattered on day one
- Camper shoes opened a San Francisco store before construction was finished, calling it "Walk in Progress"
- Crate and Barrel opened their first store using crates and barrels as displays instead of waiting for fancy fixtures

**Steps:**
1. Ask: "If I had to launch in two weeks, what would I cut out?"
2. Build only the necessities needed for launch
3. Put off anything you don't need for launch
4. Launch while you have motivation and momentum
5. Iterate and improve after launch
6. Build luxuries later

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Launch as soon as your product does what it needs to do
- ✅ Don't let a list of leftovers prevent launch
- ✅ Impose artificial deadlines to gain clarity
- ✅ Use iterations—stop imagining what's going to work and find out for real
- ✅ Build necessities now, worry about luxuries later

**Keep In Mind:**
- Just because you have a list of things to do doesn't mean it's not done
- Imposing a deadline forces you to focus and realize what you don't need
- Long projects zap morale
- Day 30 can wait—focus on day one needs

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Waiting too long to launch because everything isn't perfect
  **Solution:** Impose a two-week deadline; focus only on launch necessities; launch while motivated

**Key Terms:**
- **Launch now:** The principle of releasing a product as soon as it fulfills its core function rather than waiting for perfection
- **Iteration:** The process of improving a product through repeated cycles after launch rather than trying to perfect it beforehand
- **Day one vs. day thirty:** The distinction between immediate launch necessities and features that can wait
