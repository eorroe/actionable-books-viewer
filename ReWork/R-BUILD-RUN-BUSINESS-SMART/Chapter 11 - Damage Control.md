# Chapter 11: DAMAGE CONTROL

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Own Your Bad News
**Context:** When something goes wrong, you must control the narrative yourself instead of letting rumors, hearsay, and false information spread. People respect honesty and transparency during a crisis.

**Examples:**
- Exxon Valdez (1989): Exxon waited weeks to respond, held news briefings in a remote town, and created a PR disaster where the public believed the company was hiding something or didn't care.
- Ashland Oil spill near Pittsburgh: Chairman John Hall went to the scene immediately, pledged cleanup, visited news bureaus, and shifted the narrative within a day from "evil company" to "good company trying to clean up."

**Steps:**
1. Ensure the message comes from the highest-ranking person available in a forceful way.
2. Spread the message far and wide using whatever megaphone you have.
3. Do not try to sweep the issue under the rug.
4. Refuse to say "no comment."
5. Apologize like a real person would and explain what happened in detail.
6. Honestly show concern for the fate of your customers and then prove it.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Be open, honest, public, and responsive during a crisis.
- ✅ Tell customers about bad news even if they never noticed it initially.
- ✅ Control the story before someone else tells it for you.

**Keep In Mind:**
- There are no more secrets. Someone will call you out online if you don't address it yourself.
- People will respect you more for transparency than for hiding behind spin.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Delaying response to a crisis, allowing rumors to spread.
  **Solution:** Address the issue immediately and take control of the narrative yourself.

**Key Terms:**
- Crisis communication: Managing public and customer information flow when something goes wrong.
- PR disaster: A public relations failure that damages a company's reputation due to poor handling of bad news.

### Lesson 2: Speed Changes Everything
**Context:** Getting back to people quickly is one of the most important things you can do in customer service. Speed defuses bad situations and turns them into good ones.

**Examples:**
- Long hold times ("Your call is very important... average hold time is sixteen minutes") frustrate customers and create antagonistic tones.
- Customers who start support queries with threats or name-calling often shift 180 degrees, become extra polite, and thank you profusely once you answer quickly.
- Craigslist founder Craig Newmark answers support emails within minutes and personally moderates discussion boards.

**Steps:**
1. Answer customer inquiries as quickly as possible.
2. Offer a personal response rather than a canned answer to differentiate yourself.
3. If you don't have a perfect answer, say something anyway (e.g., "Let me do some research and get back to you").

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Do not take antagonistic customer tone personally; they are just trying to be heard.
- ✅ Thoughtful, personal responses show customers you are listening.
- ✅ Even an imperfect immediate response works wonders.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Customers have come to expect slow responses and hollow platitudes.
- Quick, genuine responses create powerful positive contrast.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Delayed responses lead to escalated frustration and negative public posts.
  **Solution:** Prioritize speed over perfection; acknowledge receipt immediately even if you need time to investigate.

**Key Terms:**
- Squeaky wheel: A customer who makes noise (complains loudly) to get attention.
- Canned answers: Generic, impersonal responses that fail to show genuine listening.

### Lesson 3: How to Say You're Sorry
**Context:** There is no perfect apology, but there are many terrible ways to apologize. A good apology accepts responsibility, provides details, and seeks to make things right.

**Examples:**
- Bad: "We're sorry if this upset you." (Conditional "if" phrase avoids accepting blame.)
- Bad: "I'm sorry that you don't feel we lived up to your expectations." (Blaming the customer's feelings.)
- Bad: "We apologize for any inconvenience this may have caused." (Weakens with "may have," minimizes the problem, uses "we" instead of "I," and uses "inconvenience" instead of acknowledging the real severity.)

**Steps:**
1. Accept full responsibility without conditional "if" phrases.
2. Use "I" instead of "we" to show personal accountability.
3. Describe what happened with real details and what you are doing to prevent recurrence.
4. Seek a way to make things right.
5. Judge your apology by asking: "If someone said this to me, would I believe them?"
6. Approach each apology on a case-by-case basis; avoid stock/generic answers.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Match the language and tone to the severity of the situation.
- ✅ Show that the buck stops with you.
- ✅ Be genuinely concerned about your customers' fate.

**Keep In Mind:**
- You cannot apologize your way out of being an "ass" if you haven't earned people's trust beforehand.
- Everything you do before things go wrong matters far more than the actual apology words.
- If you have built rapport, customers will cut you slack and trust your apology.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Using non-apology apologies that sound like apologies but accept no blame.
  **Solution:** Remove conditional language, accept direct responsibility, and commit to concrete fixes.

**Key Terms:**
- Non-apology apology: An apology that sounds like one but doesn't actually accept blame, often using conditional language or minimizing the issue.
- Case-by-case apology: Tailoring each apology to the specific situation rather than using generic stock responses.

### Lesson 4: Put Everyone on the Front Lines
**Context:** Everyone on your team should be connected to customers directly. When feedback passes through too many layers, it gets distorted like a game of Telephone, and the people doing the work never feel the real customer pain or joy.

**Examples:**
- Restaurant industry: Chefs work as waiters for a stretch so kitchen staff can directly experience front-of-house customer interaction.
- Telephone game: The more people in the circle between customers and workers, the more distorted the message becomes.
- Craig Newmark still answers support emails and deletes racist comments personally, showing that even founders can stay connected to customers.

**Steps:**
1. Connect every team member to customers at least a few times throughout the year.
2. Do not shield anyone from direct customer criticism or feedback.
3. Make time for customer interaction even if you think you don't have time.
4. Let team members feel both the hurt of customer problems and the joy of solving them.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Encourage empathy between product creators and customers.
- ✅ Use direct customer interaction as a powerful motivator for fixing problems.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Feeling customer hurt is what really motivates people to fix problems.
- The joy of happy customers or resolved problems is also wildly motivating.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Too many layers between customers and the people doing the work, causing message distortion and lack of empathy.
  **Solution:** Remove intermediaries; have team members hear customer feedback directly and regularly.

**Key Terms:**
- Front-of-house/back-of-house split: The division between customer-facing roles and product-making/support roles in a company.
- Telephone distortion: The phenomenon where a message becomes increasingly distorted as it passes through multiple people.

### Lesson 5: Take a Deep Breath
**Context:** When you introduce a new feature, change a policy, or remove something, knee-jerk negative reactions will pour in. Resist the urge to panic or make rapid changes. Let things simmer before acting.

**Examples:**
- Customers react negatively to change because they are creatures of habit.
- People often respond before giving a change a fair chance, calling minor changes "the worst thing I've ever seen."
- Negative reactions are almost always louder and more passionate than positive ones, so you may only hear negative voices even when the majority is happy.

**Steps:**
1. Resist panic and the urge to make rapid changes in response to initial complaints.
2. Acknowledge that you are listening and understand customer discontent.
3. Let people know you are going to let the change play out for a while before making decisions.
4. Give the change a fair chance to settle.
5. Avoid foolishly backpedaling on a necessary but controversial decision.

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Ride out the first rocky week; things usually settle down.
- ✅ Explain that you are listening and aware of the feedback.
- ✅ Trust that people will adjust and may even grow to prefer the new way.

**Keep In Mind:**
- Passions flare at the beginning of change; this is normal.
- Negative feedback is almost always louder than positive feedback.
- Do not foolishly reverse necessary decisions just because of initial noise.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Backpedaling on a necessary but controversial decision too quickly due to loud initial negative reactions.
  **Solution:** Wait for emotions to settle, gather more data, and recognize that silence from the majority does not mean majority discontent.

**Key Terms:**
- Knee-jerk reaction: An immediate, instinctive response to change before rational consideration.
- Primal response: An emotional, instinctive negative reaction to change before giving it a fair chance.
