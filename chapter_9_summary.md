# Chapter 9: From Good To Great To Built To Last
## Book: Good to Great by Jim Collins

---

## Overview

This final chapter bridges the findings of Jim Collins' two landmark research studies: **Built to Last** (1994) and **Good to Great** (2001). It answers the question of how to transform not just a good company into a great one, but a great company into an **enduring great company**. The chapter synthesizes both frameworks, shows how they interrelate, and closes with a powerful reflection on the deeper meaning of work and greatness.

---

## Key Concepts and Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Good to Great (GtG)** | The framework for transforming an established, merely good company into one that achieves sustained, remarkable performance. |
| **Built to Last (BtL)** | The framework for building an enduring great company that lasts across generations and product cycles. |
| **Prequel** | GtG is described as a "prequel" to BtL — first apply GtG to get great results, then BtL to make them endure. |
| **Core Ideology** | The combination of **core values** (enduring principles) and **core purpose** (reason for being beyond profit). |
| **BHAG (Big Hairy Audacious Goal)** | A huge, daunting, compelling goal that galvanizes an organization forward. |
| **Good BHAG** | A BHAG set with **understanding** (rooted in the three Hedgehog circles), not bravado. |
| **Bad BHAG** | A BHAG set with bravado, without grounding in reality or the Hedgehog Concept. |
| **Preserve the Core / Stimulate Progress** | Holding core values and purpose fixed while adapting strategies, practices, and innovations endlessly. |
| **Hedgehog Concept** | The intersection of three circles: what you can be best at, what drives your economic engine, and what you are deeply passionate about. |
| **Flywheel** | The build-up-to-breakthrough momentum model; enduring greatness is built turn-by-turn, not in one explosive moment. |
| **Clock Building, Not Time Telling** | Build an organization that endures through many leaders and product cycles, rather than building around a single leader or idea. |
| **Genius of AND** | Embrace both extremes simultaneously (e.g., purpose AND profit, continuity AND change). |
| **Level 5 Leadership** | Executive who blends personal humility and professional will. |
| **First Who, Then What** | Getting the right people on the bus before deciding where to drive it. |

### Lesson 1: Apply Good to Great First, Then Apply Built to Last

**Overview:**  
Good to Great is the **prequel** to Built to Last. A company must first achieve sustained great results using the Good to Great framework (disciplined people, thought, action, hedgehog concept, flywheel, Level 5 leadership, etc.) before it can become an enduring great company.

**Examples:**
- The **Built to Last** companies (Procter & Gamble, American Express, Johnson & Johnson, GE, Wal-Mart, HP) actually followed the Good to Great framework during their formative years, but as entrepreneurs building from scratch rather than as CEOs transforming established firms.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Assess whether your organization is currently achieving sustained, remarkable results.
2. Apply the **Good to Great** framework to build breakthrough momentum:
   - Get the right people on the bus (first who).
   - Confront the brutal facts of your reality.
   - Develop your Hedgehog Concept.
   - Build your flywheel — turn by turn, with disciplined action.
   - Practice Level 5 leadership (humility + will).
3. Once you have achieved sustained great performance (the flywheel is turning with real momentum), layer on **Built to Last** principles to transform it into an enduring great company.
4. Do not try to apply Built to Last concepts before the GtG foundation is in place — you will lack the sustained performance required to support an enduring core.

**Best Practices:**
- Treat the transition in stages: first get great results, then ensure they last.
- Do not skip straight to core ideology without first achieving performance discipline.

**Keep in Mind:**
- Good to Great is not a sequel to Built to Last; it is the prerequisite. As Collins states: *"Apply the findings in this book to create sustained great results... and then apply the findings in Built to Last to go from great results to an enduring great company."*

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Trying to build an enduring great company without first achieving sustained great results.
- Assuming Built to Last principles alone can transform a mediocre organization.
- Starting from a biased frame of reference (the GtG research team began as if Built to Last didn't exist to avoid circular reasoning).

### Lesson 2: Build Your Flywheel Before Seeking Breakthrough (Buildup Before Breakthrough)

**Overview:**  
Enduring great companies do not experience "overnight success." They go through a **buildup-breakthrough** pattern — turning the flywheel turn by turn, year after year, before the breakthrough becomes visible to the outside world.

**Examples:**
- **Wal-Mart:** Sam Walton opened a single dime store in 1945. His second store didn't come until seven years later. It took a **quarter of a century** to grow from that single dime store to 38 Wal-Marts. From 1970 to 2000, Wal-Mart exploded to over 3,000 stores and $150 billion in revenues — but only after decades of flywheel building. Walton himself noted: *"Like most overnight successes, it was about twenty years in the making."*
- **Hewlett-Packard:** Bill Hewlett and David Packard spent months "stumbling around" trying to find what to manufacture — yacht transmitters, air-conditioning controls, bowling alley sensors, electronic shock jiggle machines. None of it mattered much in the early days; what mattered was building the company with the right people. HP scaled up by hiring extraordinary people even when they had no specific role in mind.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Accept that breakthrough is not the starting point — **buildup is**.
2. Begin with a single, small turn of the flywheel: one great hire, one disciplined decision, one step of your Hedgehog Concept.
3. Keep turning the flywheel consistently:
   - Each turn builds momentum.
   - Each turn provides data and evidence.
   - Each turn attracts more great people who want to be part of a spinning flywheel.
4. Do not expect or force a visible breakthrough early.
5. Let the flywheel build momentum until the breakthrough becomes natural and inevitable.
6. Resist the temptation to sell or celebrate "instant success" stories — understand that what looks like overnight success was decades of invisible buildup.

**Best Practices:**
- Focus on the **process**, not the event. As Collins notes: *"Greatness is not primarily an event — it is a process."*
- Celebrate small, consistent turns of the flywheel.
- Build the team culture around the flywheel concept so everyone understands that momentum takes time.

**Keep in Mind:**
- Like the chicken breaking out of its egg in the earlier flywheel chapter, the company has been incubating for decades before the breakthrough appears. *"Wal-Mart had been incubating for decades before the egg cracked open."*

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Expecting immediate results and declaring failure too early.
- Seeking a single "killer app" or revolutionary idea instead of building consistent momentum.
- Hiring for immediate needs only, missing the surplus-of-great-people strategy that HP used.
- Comparing yourself to organizations that appear to have "exploded" onto the scene without understanding their hidden buildup.

### Lesson 3: Practice "First Who, Then What" — Relentlessly Build Your Talent Base

**Overview:**  
The foundation of both enduring and great companies is getting — and keeping — the **right people**. This principle applies equally to start-ups building from scratch and to established CEOs transforming companies.

**Examples:**
- **Hewlett-Packard:** Hewlett and Packard's entire founding concept was *"not what, but who."* They didn't know what to manufacture at first; they just knew they wanted to build a company with each other and other like-minded people. Even after the company was established, when wartime contracts ended and revenues shrank, they hired a whole batch of great people with nothing specific in mind — living out **Packard's Law**: *"No company can grow revenues consistently faster than its ability to get enough of the right people to implement that growth and still become a great company."*
- **Cross-country team case study:** The head coach rebuilt the program around "first who." She kept an assistant coach who was a 300-pound ex-shot-putter (hardly a lean runner image) because he was exactly the right "who" — someone who shared the values and had the traits needed to build a great team. The program nearly tripled in size (from 30 to 82 kids) because great coaches attracted great runners.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Before deciding on strategy, vision, or product, **assess your current people**:
   - Who is on your bus?
   - Are they the right people for the journey you want to take?
2. **Do not compromise on getting the right people on the bus** — even if it means slower growth in the short term.
3. **Do not tolerate the wrong people** — remove them systematically and with compassion.
4. Once the right people are on the bus, **give them freedom and responsibility** to figure out what to do together.
5. Build a **surplus of great people** — hire great talent even when you don't yet have a specific role for them, as HP did.
6. Make your culture so compelling that great people are naturally attracted to it — creating a self-reinforcing flywheel of talent attraction.
7. Consistently re-evaluate: is everyone still the right person as the organization evolves?

**Best Practices:**
- Apply this principle at every stage: hiring, promoting, and restructuring.
- Let the right people help define the vision — trust that great people will figure out the right direction together.
- Build talent pipelines before you need them.

**Keep in Mind:**
- The right people don't need tight management — they need a common context and freedom to perform.
- *"If you have the right people, they will be motivated by the same things that motivate you, and you won't need elaborate incentive schemes."* (paraphrased from the cross-country example)

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Hiring for a specific role instead of for shared values and character.
- Keeping the wrong people out of loyalty or fear.
- Trying to motivate the wrong people instead of replacing them.
- Believing you can "develop" the wrong people into the right people — it's much harder than finding the right people in the first place.
- Hiring only to fill immediate needs instead of building for the future.

### Lesson 4: Define and Build Your Core Ideology — Then Preserve It Relentlessly

**Overview:**  
Enduring great companies are guided by a **core ideology** — a set of core values and a core purpose that goes beyond making money. This ideology acts as the North Star across generations of leaders, product cycles, and market changes.

**Examples:**
- **Hewlett-Packard:** HP's core values included technical contribution, respect for the individual, responsibility to communities, and a belief that profit is *not* the fundamental goal of a company. These values were radical in the 1950s. Bill Hewlett said he was most proud of *"having helped create a company that by virtue of its values, practices, and success has had a tremendous impact on the way companies are managed around the world."* The "HP Way" distinguished the company more than any of its products.
- **Walt Disney:** Disney's core purpose — *"to bring happiness to millions, especially children"* — cut across national borders and endured through every strategic shift, from cartoons to theme parks. This purpose remained constant even as everything else changed. The man who brought Disney to the Middle East said: *"The whole idea is to bring a smile to a child's face. That's really important here, where there aren't enough smiles on the children."*
- **Merck:** Merck developed and distributed a drug that cured river blindness, giving it away free to millions of poor people in remote places. Despite this, Merck was highly profitable — outperforming the market ten-to-one from 1946 to 2000. George Merck II said: *"Medicine is for the patient. It is not for the profits. The profits follow, and if we have remembered that, they have never failed to appear. The better we have remembered it, the larger they have been."*

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Articulate your core values:** Identify the essential, enduring tenets that guide your organization. Ask: "What principles are so fundamental that we would not abandon them even if it cost us financially?"
2. **Articulate your core purpose:** Ask: "Why do we exist beyond just making money?" What deeper mission drives us?
3. **Make your ideology explicit:** Write it down, share it widely, and build it into every process — hiring, decision-making, onboarding, strategy reviews.
4. **Ensure everyone knows it:** From the newest hire to the CEO, every person should be able to articulate the core ideology.
5. **Preserve the core:** Treat your core ideology as an anchor point. Do not change it lightly. Changes to core values should be rare and deliberate.
6. **Stimulate progress in everything else:** Strategies, practices, products, and structures should adapt endlessly to a changing world. The only things that stay fixed are core values and purpose.

**Best Practices:**
- Make your core ideology so clear that it acts like the principles in the Declaration of Independence — *"never perfectly followed, but always present as an inspiring standard."*
- There is no "right" set of core values. What matters is that you **have** them, know what they are, and preserve them. Sony didn't need passion for customers; Ford didn't need social responsibility. The specific content doesn't matter — the existence of a core does.
- Test for durability: would these values still be valid 100 years from now?

**Keep in Mind:**
- Profits and cash flow are like blood and water to a healthy body — essential for life, but not the **point** of life.
- Enduring great companies don't exist merely to deliver returns to shareholders.
- Core ideology is not a marketing slogan — it is a genuine operating principle that shapes real decisions.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Confusing core ideology with strategy or practices (strategies change; core does not).
- Adopting core values that are trendy but not genuinely held.
- Abandoning core values during hard times or competitive pressure.
- Believing there is a single "right" set of core values for every great company.
- Using core values as window dressing while making decisions that contradict them.

### Lesson 5: Master the "Preserve the Core / Stimulate Progress" Dynamic

**Overview:**  
The fundamental challenge of enduring greatness is the tension between **preservation** and **change**. Enduring great companies resolve this through a dual approach: hold core values and purpose fixed while relentlessly adapting strategies, practices, and innovations.

**Examples:**
- **Walt Disney:** Disney held firm to core values (passionate belief in creative imagination, fanatic attention to detail, abhorrence of cynicism, preservation of "Disney Magic") while constantly changing strategies — from hand-drawn animation to full-length feature films, from cartoons to television (Mickey Mouse Club), and from animation to theme parks (Disneyland, Walt Disney World, EPCOT Center). The core stayed the same; the manifestations changed endlessly. Walt Disney's willingness to venture into theme parks — calling existing amusement parks "dirty, phony places" — showed how a company can create something entirely new while staying true to its core purpose of bringing happiness.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Clearly define your **core** (values + purpose) — this is non-negotiable and must not change.
2. Clearly define your **non-core** — everything that is not core ideology (strategies, products, structures, practices, systems).
3. In the **non-core** domain, practice relentless change, experimentation, and improvement:
   - Challenge every strategy, practice, and system.
   - Encourage innovation and adaptation.
   - Set ambitious BHAGs that push the organization forward.
4. When making any change, **test it against the core**: does this change preserve or violate our core ideology?
5. When facing new opportunities or threats, ask: "Can we pursue this while remaining true to our core?" If yes, find a way. If no, walk away.
6. Communicate this dual commitment throughout the organization: *"Our core is fixed. Everything else is up for grabs."*

**Best Practices:**
- Frame change as the norm, not the exception. People should expect practices to evolve; they should not expect core values to shift.
- Use the core as the **filter**, not the constraint. Let the core *enable* bold change by giving people clear boundaries within which to innovate.
- Celebrate changes that stimulate progress while honoring the core.

**Keep in Mind:**
- This is not an "OR" dynamic — it is an "AND" dynamic. The Genius of AND applies here: **preserve the core AND stimulate progress**.
- Change without core preservation is just chaos. Preservation without change is stagnation.
- The magic lies in holding both extremes simultaneously.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Changing core values to chase short-term opportunities or profits.
- Becoming rigid and resisting all change in the name of "tradition."
- Conflating core values with specific practices or strategies.
- Allowing the desire for progress to erode the very principles that made the organization great.

### Lesson 6: Set Good BHAGs — Grounded in Understanding, Not Bravado

**Overview:**  
A **BHAG (Big Hairy Audacious Goal)** is a powerful tool for stimulating progress while preserving the core. However, not all BHAGs are created equal. A **good BHAG** is set with **understanding** of the three Hedgehog circles. A **bad BHAG** is set with **bravado** — big, daring, but disconnected from reality.

**Examples:**
- **Boeing (Good BHAG):** In 1952, Boeing set out to build a commercial jetliner (the 707), committing 25% of the company's entire net worth to a prototype. This was audacious — at the time, Boeing had virtually no presence in commercial aviation and airlines openly mocked them: *"You make great bombers up there in Seattle. Why don't you just stick with that."* But Boeing's BHAG was **not** bravado. It was grounded in understanding:
  1. **Passion:** Boeing's people were deeply passionate about building commercial aircraft.
  2. **Best at:** Boeing understood its cumulative experience with large aircraft and jet engines made it uniquely capable of building the best commercial jet.
  3. **Economic engine:** Commercial aircraft economics would vastly outperform the military market.
  
  The result: Boeing produced five of the most successful commercial jets in history (707, 727, 737, 747, 757) and dominated the industry for decades.
- **Bad BHAG (contrast):** Swimming from Australia to New Zealand is a BHAG, but it would kill the swimmer — it lacks any grounding in realistic understanding.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Before setting a BHAG, **deeply understand your three Hedgehog circles**:
   - What can you potentially be the best in the world at?
   - What drives your economic engine (profit per x)?
   - What are you deeply passionate about?
2. **Ensure your BHAG aligns with all three circles.** If it doesn't fit all three, it is a bad BHAG.
3. Make the BHAG **huge and daunting** — it should feel almost impossible, yet inspiring.
4. Make it **clear and compelling** — people should "get it" immediately.
5. Make it a **unifying focal point** that galvanizes the entire organization.
6. Set it with **quiet understanding** — not with hype, posturing, or empty bravado.
7. Communicate the BHAG simply and consistently.
8. Let the BHAG serve as a catalyst for the flywheel — every action should move the organization toward the BHAG.

**Best Practices:**
- Use the three-circle Hedgehog Concept as the **foundation** for your BHAG.
- A BHAG without understanding is dangerous; a BHAG with understanding is transformative.
- Let the BHAG emerge from deep analysis, not from boardroom bravado.
- The BHAG should stretch the organization but still be achievable through disciplined execution.

**Keep in Mind:**
- *"When you combine quiet understanding of the three circles with the audacity of a BHAG, you get a powerful, almost magical mix."*
- Boeing never left its three circles or abandoned its core ideology — but it created an exciting new BHAG and adjusted its Hedgehog Concept to include commercial aircraft. You can change the **specific manifestation** of what's inside your three circles over time while staying within the circles themselves.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Setting a BHAG based on hype, fear, or competition rather than genuine understanding.
- Choosing a BHAG that doesn't align with all three Hedgehog circles.
- Setting a BHAG that is so disconnected from reality it demoralizes rather than inspires.
- Using a BHAG as a one-time motivational speech rather than a sustained unifying force.
- Believing a BHAG alone will create greatness without the underlying GtG disciplines.

### Lesson 7: Integrate Good to Great and Built to Last — Apply All Key Concepts Consistently Over Time

**Overview:**  
Enduring greatness requires **all** the key concepts from both studies, applied consistently over time. Ceasing any one of the disciplines will cause the organization to slide back toward mediocrity. It is much easier to **become** great than to **remain** great.

**The Four Built to Last Key Ideas (all must be applied):**

1. **Clock Building, Not Time Telling:** Build an organization that can endure through multiple generations of leaders and multiple product life cycles. Do not build around a single great leader or a single great idea.
2. **Genius of AND:** Embrace both extremes simultaneously — purpose AND profit, continuity AND change, freedom AND responsibility.
3. **Core Ideology:** Instill core values and core purpose as principles to guide decisions and inspire people over a long period of time.
4. **Preserve the Core / Stimulate Progress:** Preserve core ideology as an anchor while stimulating change, improvement, innovation, and renewal in everything else.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Study and internalize **all** key concepts from both the Good to Great and Built to Last frameworks.
2. Map how each GtG concept enables each BtL concept — they are interdependent.
3. Apply the complete integrated framework consistently across all areas of your organization:
   - Leadership development (Level 5)
   - Talent management (First Who)
   - Strategy (Hedgehog Concept + BHAGs)
   - Culture (Core Ideology)
   - Execution (Flywheel + Disciplined Action)
4. **Never stop applying any one of them.** Consistency over time is the differentiator between good, great, and enduring great.
5. Audit regularly: are we still doing all of these things, or have we lapsed into old habits?
6. Train new leaders and new employees on the complete framework — not just pieces of it.

**Best Practices:**
- Use the conceptual links table (referenced on page 198 of the book) as a diagnostic tool.
- Understand that GtG lays the groundwork for BtL — each GtG finding enables all four BtL ideas.
- Treat the framework as an integrated system, not a menu to pick and choose from.

**Keep In Mind:**
- *"Tocreate anenduring great company requires allthekeyconcepts from both studies, tiedtogether andapplied consistently over time."*
- *"If youever stop doing anyoneofthekeyideas, your organization willinevitably slide backward toward mediocrity."*
- *"Itismuch easier tobecome great than toremain great."*

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Cherry-picking concepts from GtG or BtL and ignoring others.
- Believing that a single concept (like a BHAG or a strong culture) is sufficient on its own.
- Applying GtG disciplines to achieve breakthrough but then failing to layer on BtL principles — the result is a great company that doesn't last.
- Getting lazy after achieving breakthrough — resting on past success rather than maintaining the disciplines.

### Lesson 8: Choose Meaningful Work — Greatness Is Driven by Passion and Purpose

**Overview:**  
The deepest driver of enduring greatness is not money, size, or status — it is **meaningful work**. The question "Why greatness?" is almost nonsensical if you are doing work you truly care about. If you have to ask why you should try for greatness, you may be in the wrong line of work.

**Examples:**
- **Sina Simantob:** Runs the building where Collins keeps his research lab. By one definition of results — attracting the most interesting people in Boulder, setting a standard other buildings measure themselves against, and generating the highest profit per square foot — Simantob has built a truly great institution. He never defined greatness by size. It is a small enterprise, but it is great.
- **The cross-country coach:** Has an MBA from an elite business school and was a Phi Beta Kappa economics graduate who won the prize for the best undergraduate honors thesis. Most of her classmates went into investment banking, Internet startups, consulting, or IBM. None of those held meaning for her. She chose coaching because she believed in running and its impact on kids' lives. She said: *"It's because I really care about what we're doing. I believe in running and the impact it can make on these kids' lives."*
- **Level 5 Leaders across the GtG study:**
  - **Bill Hewlett:** Cared deeply about creating a company that impacted how companies are managed around the world.
  - **Ken Iverson (Nucor):** Felt a crusader's purpose to obliterate oppressive class hierarchies.
  - **Darwin Smith (Kimberly-Clark):** Driven by an inner quest for excellence itself.
  - **Lyle Everingham (Kroger) and Cork Walgreen (Walgreens):** Simply loved the business they were in.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Reflect honestly** on whether the work you are doing truly matters to you.
2. Ask yourself: *"If I have to ask 'Why should we try to make this great? Isn't success enough?', am I in the right line of work?"*
3. Identify work that you care about **deeply enough** that the question of trying for greatness is self-evident.
4. Align your responsibilities with your **personal three Hedgehog circles**:
   - What are you passionate about?
   - What can you be great at?
   - What drives your economic engine (or fulfills you)?
5. If your current work doesn't meet this test, **search for meaningful work** — whether in a company, a nonprofit, a church, a community organization, or a class you teach.
6. Engage in something you care about so much that you want to make it the greatest it can possibly be — **not for what you will get, but because it can be done.**
7. When you do meaningful work, you will naturally grow toward **Level 5 leadership** — because you will have the drive and discipline to practice all the other GtG findings.

**Best Practices:**
- Greatness does not depend on size. A small institution can be truly great if it is excellent at what it does.
- Meaning is personal — there is no single "right" reason to care about your work.
- The search for meaning is not a luxury — it is the foundation of the discipline and energy needed for greatness.
- When meaningful work, personal responsibility, and the three circles align, the result is both great work and a great life.

**Keep In Mind:**
- *"It is impossible to have a great life unless it is a meaningful life. And it is very difficult to have a meaningful life without meaningful work."*
- *"The real question is not 'Why greatness?' but 'What work makes you feel compelled to try to create greatness?'"*
- The cross-country coaches were not working harder than other teams — they were focusing on the right things and not wasting energy on the wrong things. The process was simple, clean, straightforward, elegant — and fun.
- Building something great does not require more suffering than perpetuating mediocrity — in fact, it may require less.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Staying in work that is successful but meaningless, and wondering why you lack energy and drive.
- Defining greatness solely by size, revenue, or external recognition.
- Believing that financial success alone will create fulfillment.
- Thinking that meaningful work is only available in certain types of organizations (e.g., nonprofits) — it can be found anywhere if you align your responsibilities with your values.
- Using "success" as an excuse to avoid striving for greatness — if you have to ask whether success is enough, it likely isn't, because you're not engaged in work that truly matters to you.

## Summary

Chapter 9 serves as the bridge and culmination of both research studies. Its central thesis is that **Good to Great** and **Built to Last** are not competing frameworks but **sequential and interdependent** ones. First, apply the GtG disciplines to achieve sustained great performance. Then, layer on the BtL principles — core ideology, preserve the core/stimulate progress, clock building, and the Genius of AND — to make that greatness endure across generations. At the heart of both frameworks is a simple truth: **greatness is not about size, bravado, or shortcuts.** It is about disciplined, consistent application of timeless principles — starting with the right people, building momentum turn by turn, grounding ambition in understanding, and being engaged in work that truly matters.
