# Do The Work!: Overcome Resistance and Get Out of Your Own Way

## Overview

Do The Work!: Overcome Resistance and Get Out of Your Own Way is a practical guide to overcoming internal resistance and completing creative projects. It teaches that Resistance—an invisible, impersonal force of fear and self-sabotage—is a primary enemy of all creative and entrepreneurial endeavor. The book provides actionable strategies to recognize, outmaneuver, and ultimately defeat Resistance through instinctual action, stubborn persistence, commitment without guarantees, and commitment to the act of creation itself.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When starting any creative project (writing, art, music, business) and feeling blocked or unmotivated
- When procrastination, self-doubt, or fear is preventing you from beginning
- When you've started a project but hit a wall mid-way through
- When you're afraid to ship or share your work with the world
- When you need to understand why you self-sabotage and how to stop
- When you want to develop professional habits and finish what you start on creative or professional work

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Understand Your Enemy - Resistance

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Resistance Targets Meaningful Work

Resistance strikes whenever you pursue any act that rejects immediate gratification in favor of long-term growth—whether that's writing a novel, launching a business, or overcoming addiction. The more important the project is to your growth as a creator or professional, the more Resistance you will feel.

##### Example 2: Resistance Is Universal

Henry Fonda still threw up before stage performances at age 75. Fear doesn't go away; you as a creator or professional must fight the battle anew each day you engage in creative or professional work.

#### Step 1: Recognize Resistance's Five Key Traits

Resistance is invisible but can be felt as an energy field repelling you from your work. It is subtle and deceptive, telling any lie to stop you. It is impersonal—not targeting you specifically but acting as a force of nature. It is infallible, always pointing to your true calling. It is universal and always active. Its goal is not to wound but to derail your creative output and passion.

#### Step 2: Identify Resistance's Manifestations

During creative or professional work, watch for: fear, self-doubt, procrastination, addiction, distraction, timidity, ego and narcissism, self-loathing, perfectionism, rationalization, and victimhood.

#### Step 3: Name the Other Enemies

Beyond Resistance, rational thought (ego-based analysis) and friends/family (who know you as you are and want to keep you that way) can sabotage your creative or professional growth.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Name Resistance when you feel Resistance
- ✅ Remember Resistance is impersonal—don't take it personally
- ✅ Use Resistance as a guide—it points to what you must do
- ✅ Respect Resistance as a significant challenge
- ❌ Don't underestimate Resistance's strength
- ❌ Don't reason with Resistance—Resistance lies
- ❌ Don't let friends/family derail your higher creative or professional calling

#### Keep In Mind

Resistance is strongest at the finish line. It aims to derail your creative output, not just inconvenience you.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Resistance is internal. No external security measures can protect you from Resistance. The struggle must be fought within.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Taking Resistance personally and feeling like a failure
  **Solution:** Remember Resistance is impersonal, universally and without personal bias. It acts on everyone.
- **Problem:** Listening to rational thought instead of intuitive judgment
  **Solution:** Work from your authentic creative self, not the ego. Trust your unconscious creative process.
- **Problem:** Letting friends/family discourage your evolution
  **Solution:** Prepare to make new friends who support your higher creative self.

### Lesson 2: Begin Before You're Ready

#### Examples

##### Example 1: W.H. Murray's Principle

"Until one is committed, there is hesitancy, the chance to draw back, always ineffectiveness... the moment one definitely commits oneself, then progress becomes possible." —W.H. Murray, from his mountaineering writing

##### Example 2: The Foolscap Sheet

Norm Stahl, a screenwriting teacher, taught: "God made a single sheet of yellow foolscap exactly the right length to hold the outline of an entire novel." Don't overthink. Outline fast on intuitive judgment.

#### Step 1: Commit Immediately

Don't prepare. Begin. Resistance is the enemy, not lack of preparation. Your overthinking mind will produce endless excuses if given a brief moment. Start before you feel completely prepared.

#### Step 2: Limit Research

You're allowed to read three books on your subject. No more. No underlining or highlighting. Let ideas settle into your unconscious mind. Research can become Resistance—work, don't prepare to work.

#### Step 3: Stay Primitive

The creative act is instinctual, raw and unrefined. Be clueless enough to not know how hard it will be, and confident enough to believe you can pull it off anyway.

#### Step 4: Aim for a Major Achievement

An ambitious attempt that results in a missed target is better than a small successful effort. Don't work small. Bring big ideas. You can always reduce it later.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Start now, not when conditions are perfect
- ✅ Limit research to avoid Resistance
- ✅ Work from intuitive judgment, not over-analysis
- ✅ Aim larger than your comfort zone
- ❌ Don't wait until you feel completely prepared
- ❌ Don't excessively prepare or excessively research
- ❌ Don't let your overthinking mind talk you out of starting

#### Keep In Mind

Positive results happen when you start before you feel completely prepared. Taking courageous action builds more courage. The universe rewards courageous action with approval.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Starting before feeling completely prepared means accepting flaws or incomplete work. You have permission to produce imperfect work. The self-critical voice is not permitted in the workspace.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Waiting for ideal conditions or ideal preparation
  **Solution:** Start now with what you have. The moment you commit, progress becomes possible.
- **Problem:** Getting lost in research instead of creating
  **Solution:** Put yourself on a research diet. A small number of reference books. Work first, research later.
- **Problem:** Thinking your idea is too small
  **Solution:** Aim for a major achievement. A failed ambitious attempt beats a successful small one.

### Lesson 3: Structure Your Project with Three Acts

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Leonardo's Last Supper in Three Acts

1. Supper table stretching across the canvas
2. Jesus standing in the center, apostles arrayed left and right
3. Perspective and background receding into the background

##### Example 2: Facebook in Three Acts

1. A digital commons for personal pages
2. Each page owner controls access
3. A worldwide community of friends interacting

#### Step 1: Break Your Project into Three Parts

Divide your single sheet of paper into: Beginning, Middle, and End. This is how screenwriters and playwrights work. Act One, Act Two, Act Three.

#### Step 2: Start at the End

Work backwards. If writing a movie, solve the climax first. If opening a restaurant, begin with the dining experience you want to create. Determine your end state, then work backwards to find the steps to get there.

#### Step 3: Define Your Theme

Answer: "What is your project about?" When you know the theme, you'll know the end state and the steps to reach it. Write the theme on a strip of paper and tape it to your workspace.

#### Step 4: Fill in the Gaps

Aim for approximately 7-8 major sequences (according to filmmaker David Lean's rule for feature films). Break your project into compelling opening, major middle set pieces, powerful climax, and concise theme statement.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Use a single sheet of paper for your outline
- ✅ Work from intuitive judgment at the conception stage
- ✅ Define your theme before diving into details
- ✅ Break project into beginning, middle, end
- ❌ Don't spend an extended period on detailed outlines
- ❌ Don't over-analyze at the conception stage
- ❌ Don't work in strict sequence if ideas come out of order

#### Keep In Mind

At the project's beginning, the only questions that matter: Do you feel strongly committed to your idea? Does it feel right on intuitive judgment? Are you willing to sacrifice significantly for it?

#### Security & Safety Notes

The theme is your guiding principle. Everything that doesn't serve the theme gets cut. Protect your theme consistently.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Overthinking the structure before starting
  **Solution:** Outline quickly based on your initial ideas. You can always revise.
- **Problem:** Not knowing the end before beginning
  **Solution:** Work backwards from your desired end state. What is your project about?
- **Problem:** Filling gaps with non-essential elements
  **Solution:** Ask "What's missing?" then fill that missing element. Each element you include must serve the theme.

### Lesson 4: Trust the Process - Act and Reflect

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Bob Dylan's Instinctive Method

Bob Dylan finds songs by intuitive instinct, open and unselfconscious. The song already exists in inherent form—your role is to listen for the song's natural structure.

##### Example 2: The Answer Is Always Yes

When an idea comes to mind and you think "No, the idea is too unusual"—that's the idea you want. Never doubt your unconscious creative process. Never say no.

#### Step 1: Separate Action from Reflection

The project creation process has two stages: action (putting words on paper, making the creative work) and reflection (evaluating what you have). Avoid acting and reflecting simultaneously. For your initial draft, go heavy on action and light on reflection.

#### Step 2: Spew Without Judgment

Begin without a complete plan and let your ideas guide you. Your idea contains wisdom beyond your conscious thinking. Your role is not to control the outcome but to discover what the work wants to become.

#### Step 3: Trust the Soup

When you say "Trust your unconscious creative process," you mean your creative unconscious, your unconscious mind, the source of creative ideas. The creator begins their work trusting unconscious inspiration will appear. Begin your creative work to catch ideas.

#### Step 4: Stay Stupid

Don't criticize your own work, don't indulge in doubt about your ability, don't permit judgment about your work's quality. Forget over-analysis. Work with openness and lack of self-criticism. Your idea contains insight beyond conscious awareness.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Act first, reflect later
- ✅ Let your unconscious mind work while you sleep
- ✅ Record ideas as soon as the ideas arrive
- ✅ Say yes to unconventional ideas
- ❌ Don't mix action and reflection in your initial draft
- ❌ Don't self-censor or judge your initial creative output
- ❌ Don't overthink—let your unconscious creative process work

#### Keep In Mind

Creative ideas follow a non-linear creative logic—not rational, not sequential. You may develop the middle section before the ending. Be ready for this pattern. Don't resist this pattern.

#### Security & Safety Notes

This method requires a willingness to produce work that needs revision. You must be willing to produce work that needs revision. Your self-critical voice is banned from the workspace.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Trying to edit while creating
  **Solution:** Separate action and reflection. Create first, evaluate later.
- **Problem:** Self-censoring "crazy" ideas
  **Solution:** The answer is always yes. The most unconventional ideas are sometimes the most powerful.
- **Problem:** Overthinking instead of acting
  **Solution:** Work with openness to ideas beyond your conscious thinking. Your idea contains insight beyond your conscious awareness. Trust the creative process.

### Lesson 5: Navigate the Middle - The Wall and The Belly of the Beast

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Universe Is Actively Hostile

When you set out to create, you encounter a strong counter-force from the creative process: Resistance. It is persistent, adaptable, harmful, tireless, relentless, and difficult to overcome. You cannot fully eliminate it, only outsmart it.

##### Example 2: The Big Crash Is Predictable

The creative professional enters rehabilitation, the financial backer withdraws support, a key collaborator leaves the project. The project stall arises organically from mistakes made at the project's inception. Count on it—it will occur.

#### Step 1: Expect the Wall

Approximately two-thirds through your project, intense fear strikes. Your confidence collapses. Your self-critical inner voice returns. You are sufficiently far to have invested heavily, not sufficiently far to see the end. This is normal.

#### Step 2: The Seven Principles of Resistance

1. **There Is an Enemy:** Recognize there is an intelligent-seeming, active, and harmful force working against you.
2. **This Enemy Is Implacable:** It aims to seriously harm your creative work, not just obstruct.
3. **This Enemy Is Inside You:** Resistance comes from within, not from external circumstances. You could isolate yourself completely and it would still be present.
4. **The Enemy Is Inside You, But It Is Not You:** The thoughts of Resistance are not your fault. You have done nothing wrong. You are blameless.
5. **The "Real You" Must Duel the "Resistance You":** Within your authentic self, your authentic self and Resistance are in conflict. You are your authentic self. Resistance is Resistance. There is no negotiating—only struggle.
6. **Resistance Arises Second:** First comes the idea, passion, dream. Resistance is a consequence of your creative impulse. The urge to create came first. That urge is your genuine passion.
7. **The Opposite of Resistance Is Assistance:** Align with supportive creative energies. Your work-in-progress generates its own attractive influence, attracting ideas and help.

#### Step 3: Pass Resistance's Two Tests

**Test One:** "How committed are you?" Scale: casually trying → moderately engaged → curious but hesitant → deeply engaged → fully committed. If not "fully committed," stop reading and reconsider.

**Test Two:** "What is your motivation?" Options: romantic attraction, financial gain, public recognition, a sense of earned reward, influence or control, a desire to prove others incorrect, serve a creative vision, enjoyment or aesthetic purpose, no alternative path. Only 8 (enjoyment or aesthetic purpose) and 9 (no alternative path) are aligned answers. Others require immediate reflection on your motivation.

#### Step 4: Enter a Period of Honest Self-Reflection

Leave at the door: your self-centered perspective, expectation of special treatment, desire for instant results, anxiety about outcomes, expectation of specific outcomes, resentment about circumstances, resentment about your starting point in life, belief that you are special due to birth or achievements. Keep only: genuine passion for the work, determination to complete, passion to serve your creative purpose.

#### Step 5: Survive the Big Crash

When your project fails or stalls, remember: the failure is not your identity. The issue is the specific failure. A project stall means you have not completed it and must develop. It compels you to solve what you avoided addressing at the start.

#### Step 6: Work the Problem

Go back to your project outline. What specific step failed in the start, middle, or finish? The solution is practical and actionable, not based on luck or inspiration. Get the core issue back on track. Set the completion date back a reasonable period to repair problems.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Expect the Wall and the Big Crash—they are expected based on past patterns
- ✅ Remember: the failure is separate from your identity
- ✅ Work the problem practically and systematically
- ✅ Call on allies: ignoring over-analysis, persistent determination, commitment without guarantees
- ✅ Refine your project's core message two times per calendar week
- ❌ Don't take not completing the project personally
- ❌ Don't experience intense anxiety—intense anxiety signals you are developing as a creator
- ❌ Don't underestimate Resistance in the middle section of your project

#### Keep In Mind

Project stalls are useful for learning. They mean you are at a threshold of learning. Intense anxiety is useful—it signals you are about to reach a threshold of learning to a higher level of skill.

#### Security & Safety Notes

In the most challenging middle section, you are on your own during this phase. Limited external support or validation. The only thing you have in common with military trainees is the quit signal. Will you quit or not?

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Crashing and taking it as a failure of your identity
  **Solution:** An experienced creative professional does not take project completion or non-completion personally. The issue is just an issue to solve.
- **Problem:** Not seeing the solution because you are too immersed to see clearly
  **Solution:** Get help from others. Let others explore the problem from different angles to generate new ideas.
- **Problem:** Quitting when things get hard
  **Solution:** Be determined enough to continue and stubborn enough to persist. The only items to keep: genuine passion for the work, determination to complete, passion to serve your creative purpose.

### Lesson 6: Ship and Conquer Fear of Success

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Author Who Couldn't Ship

A writer friend finished a high-quality novel, had it in its mailing box with cover letter, but couldn't send it. Fear of rejection immobilized him. Shipping requires decisive determination—you've got Resistance down, now completely and decisively finish.

##### Example 2: Michael Crichton's Finish

Michael Crichton got up progressively earlier as he approached the end of a novel, eventually checking into a hotel to work around the clock until finished. An experienced professional writer does whatever effort is necessary to finish.

#### Step 1: Recognize Fear of Success

A common deepest fear is not inadequacy but greater creative or professional impact. Fear of success is a core form of Resistance—hidden and subtle, affecting multiple areas of life. When offered great success or fulfillment, people choose work that talks about success without achieving it.

#### Step 2: Understand Exposure

When you ship, you are publicly visible. There is nothing but uncertainty or lack of support beneath you. The public will judge your work. You can receive negative feedback. You can feel embarrassed or rejected. This is why shipping is very frightening.

#### Step 3: Slay the Dragon Once

Defeat Resistance once by shipping, and it will have significantly less power over you again. You join an unspoken community of those who have actually done the work. You recognize fellow members. They recognize you.

#### Step 4: Start the Next One

Take the remainder of the workday off. Celebrate. Then get back to work the next workday. Begin the next project before you feel completely prepared.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Ship no matter what—it builds confidence in your ability to finish
- ✅ Remember: finishing is the essential step
- ✅ Do any unconventional methods needed to finish
- ✅ Start the next project as soon as possible after shipping
- ❌ Don't let fear of rejection prevent shipping
- ❌ Don't let perfectionism delay the finish
- ❌ Don't stop after a single completed project—continue creating

#### Keep In Mind

Shipping connects you to real-world feedback and gets you out of unrealistic fantasies. This is highly empowering. After you complete your first project, you will find it easier to finish future projects.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Shipping means public exposure and judgment. The danger is genuine but required for growth. The pain of not shipping is worse than the pain of shipping.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Freezing at the finish line
  **Solution:** Shipping requires decisive determination. Completely and decisively finish. Be unconventional if necessary.
- **Problem:** Fear of rejection or humiliation preventing shipping
  **Solution:** The cost of being in the arena is facing criticism or setbacks. Stop focusing on negatives and be grateful you are participating in the creative process.
- **Problem:** Thinking Resistance is finished after shipping
  **Solution:** Resistance changes into a more challenging form. Remain aware of Resistance. Start the next project.
