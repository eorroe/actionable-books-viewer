# Chapter VIII: The Fairness Trap

## Overview

This chapter examines the insistence that life be fair, that others behave equitably, and that outcomes be evenly distributed. The author presents this demand — limited in its applicability to readers who find themselves caught in the Fairness Trap thinking pattern, defined as readers who insist on perfect fairness in life and human interactions — as an erroneous thinking pattern that keeps those people caught in that pattern stuck in resentment, jealousy, and constant comparison. He argues that the idea of perfect fairness is a concept not found in nature or in human systems, and that causing yourself ongoing negative feelings when you encounter unfair treatment is as self-damaging — according to the book's framework — defined here by the measurable persistence of resentment and jealousy that erodes well-being — as guilt or approval-seeking. Through a series of lessons, the chapter shows how jealousy, tally-sheet relationships, and the "It's not fair!" refrain harm personal well-being, then provides concrete strategies for letting go of the demand for fairness, reducing jealousy, and living by your own chosen standards rather than external ideas of fairness.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you find yourself remaining angry because someone else received something you believe you deserved
- When you keep a mental or literal tally sheet in a relationship, measuring give-and-take and resenting perceived imbalances
- When jealousy, bitterness, or the "It's not fair!" refrain is preventing you from taking action aligned with your own chosen goals — defined here as behavior directed toward outcomes you have independently identified as valuable

## Steps

### Step 1: Understand That Perfect Fairness Is Not Found in Nature or in Human Systems

Look to nature as evidence that no universal system of fairness governs the world: robins eat worms, spiders eat flies, cougars kill coyotes, and comparable predator-prey relationships. Recognize that tornadoes, floods, tidal waves, and droughts cause harm without regard for who they affect. Per the book's framework, accept that perfect fairness has limited applicability — defined here as not being a reliable guide for personal decision-making — to your own choices about personal fulfillment and well-being. Accept that people whose behavior diverges from your personal standards — a defined subset of individuals whose actions you observe directly or through reliable firsthand reports — will continue to act in ways you may consider unfair, and per the book's framework this pattern will continue for an extended period; you can choose how to respond, but your well-being does not depend on the presence or absence of fairness around you. The author describes the food chain (cougars killing coyotes, coyotes killing badgers, badgers killing mice, mice killing bugs) and natural disasters as evidence that perfect fairness is not a feature of the natural world. He also cites political and legal realities: Spiro Agnew evading taxes and becoming rich, Richard Nixon being exonerated while his subordinates serve short sentences in minimum-security prison (as cited in the book), and the poor and minority-group members filling jails while the influential operate under separate rules.

### Step 2: Do Not Cause Yourself Lasting Negative Emotions When You Encounter Unfairness

Understand that the harmful pattern is not the desire for fairness itself, but emotional immobilization that may result from unfair circumstances, defined here as situations where treatment diverges from what you believe you are owed. Decide that you will work to address unfairness where you can exert direct control — defined here as situations where your actions, decisions, or communication can directly change the outcome — but refuse to be psychologically defeated or emotionally immobilized by it. Work at helping to reduce unfairness while deciding that you will not experience ongoing negative emotions about the absence of perfect fairness. Choose to address unfairness and refuse to be pulled into lasting negative emotions about it. The author projects — within the book's framework and drawing on the exact historical record from the 3rd millennium BCE through the 20th century CE — that poverty, war, pestilence, crime, prostitution, drug abuse, and murder have persisted throughout the documented historical period spanning from the 3rd millennium BCE through the 20th century CE and, per the book's framework on that basis, are projected to continue for an extended period without deliberate intervention. He argues that choosing to continue experiencing ongoing negative emotions about this is as self-damaging as guilt, approval-seeking, or any other pattern of self-criticism, according to the book's framework.

### Step 3: Eliminate the "It's Not Fair!" Slogan From Your Relationships

Stop comparing yourself to other individuals or groups, defined here as any person or group used as a basis for an unfair comparison between yourself and others. Stop using sentences like "If that person can do it, I can do it too," "It's not right for you to have more than I," and "But I didn't get to do that, why should you?" Stop deciding what is good for you on the basis of someone else's choices. Stop letting other people set your emotional state. Stop running your life on the basis of someone else and begin creating your own life. Discard the habit of focusing on what others are doing; eliminate comparisons to others. The author presents Judy, a client in an unhappy five-year marriage, who ran her marriage on a tally sheet. When her husband said something unpleasant, she countered: "Why did you say that? I never say things like that to you." When he mentioned their children, she said: "That isn't fair. I never bring the children into an argument." When discussing an evening's entertainment, she said: "That isn't fair. You get out all the time while I have to stay home with the children." Judy was more concerned with correcting perceived wrongs than examining and improving her marriage. The author notes that, within the Fairness Trap framework, a substantial portion of emotional distress — defined here as distress attributable specifically to the demand for fairness, as opposed to distress from other sources such as health events, accidental losses, or circumstances entirely outside any person's control — stems from making other people's behavior more important to your emotional state than your own.

### Step 4: Reduce Jealousy by Recognizing It as a Demand for Unfair Treatment From Others

Set as a goal the reduction of jealousy as a harmful thinking pattern if it creates persistent emotional immobilization — defined here as any persistent fixation on another person's behavior that produces at least two of the following: resentment, self-critical rumination, or an inability to act in ways aligned with values and priorities you have consciously chosen for yourself. Decide that another person's choices (such as a partner loving another) were made independently of you and reflect that person's own priorities, values, and circumstances — defined here as motivations that originate within that person's life and are not caused by your actions or characteristics. View someone else's affair or affectionate behavior as something between other people rather than as something directed against you. Recognize that someone else's behavior has nothing to do with your sense of self-assessed competence and worth. Stop bruising yourself with self-critical jealousy in which you rank yourself as less important — defined here as treating your self-assessed competence and worth in personally meaningful domains as lower priority than your partner's or their other partner's — than your partner or their other partner. Decide that you will not allow yourself to be distraught when someone else does not behave the way you want them to. The author presents Helen, a client furious because her husband had an affair. The 17th-century poet John Dryden called jealousy "the jaundice of the soul" — a metaphor defined here as comparing the way jealousy distorts emotional perception to the way jaundice distorts visual perception.

4. Using "Would I treat you that way?" sentences toward individuals whose preferences or behavior differ from yours — defined here as people whose choices you observe directly — as though they should match your own standards exactly.

The author lists all of the above behaviors as manifestations of the demand for fairness, noting they all stem from the assumption in your thinking that things must be fair.

### Step 6: Recognize the Emotional Benefits That Keep You Stuck in the Demand for Fairness

Release the benefit of feeling superior by keeping your tally sheet in balance; give up the feeling of being more righteous than others. Release the benefit of avoiding responsibility for yourself and justifying inaction by blaming unfair people and events — where "unfair people and events" means any person or occurrence you perceive as unjust; "action" means any chosen response or behavior taken independently — and instead take action. Release the benefit of gaining attention, pity, and self-pity by positioning yourself as a victim of unfairness — where "unfairness" means the perceived gap between expected and actual treatment; stop using these to support your sense of self. Release the benefit of justifying behavior by making your actions someone else's responsibility ("If that person can do it, so can I"). Release the benefit of giving yourself an excuse for not acting ("If they aren't going to do anything, then neither am I"). Release the benefit of having a topic for conversation — defined here as a subject you can discuss to avoid talking honestly about yourself with others — that helps you avoid talking honestly about yourself with others. Release the benefit of always making what you consider the fair choice — defined here as any choice framed as fair or moral — based on an external idea of fairness. Release the benefit of manipulating others (especially children) by telling them they are being unfair if they are not exactly like you or do not keep a precise tally — defined here as an exact count of favors given versus received — of give-and-take. Release the benefit of justifying aggressive behavior and retaliation because everything must be fair and equal. The author explains that, per the book's framework, as long as unfairness is the cause of your problems, you may believe you can never change until the unfairness goes away — which, based on the full documented record of human civilization from the 3rd millennium BCE through the 20th century CE, is unlikely to occur without deliberate intervention. He also notes that complaining about unfairness cuts off moments — defined here as specific opportunities for honest personal engagement with people around you — and helps you avoid dealing more honestly and personally with people around you.

### Step 7: Use Practical Strategies to Let Go of the Pointless Insistence on Fairness

Make a list of all situations — defined here as individual circumstances, interactions, or events you perceive as unfair — that you consider unfair. Use your list as a guide for constructive action you can take independently. Ask yourself: "Will the unfairness — defined here as perceived unequal treatment — go away if I am experiencing ongoing negative emotions?" Address the thinking pattern — defined here as the habitual belief that life must be fair — that brings about your distress, and you — meaning the reader caught in the Fairness Trap framework — will begin reducing the insistence on perfect fairness, where perfect fairness means complete equality in treatment and outcomes. When you — meaning the reader caught in the Fairness Trap framework — find yourself saying "Would I do that to you?" or any similar sentence, change it to: "You are different from me, although I find that hard to accept right now." This will open, rather than close, communication — defined here as honest interaction without fairness framing — between you and the other person. Begin to view your emotional life as independent of whatever anyone else does. This will free you from the habit — defined here as the tendency to compare yourself to others — of being hurt when others behave differently from the way you want them to. Try to view each decision with reasonable proportion — defined here as judging its significance against the full span of your life throughout your life rather than treating it in isolation — rather than as an all-important life-changing event, where all-important means treating it as determining your life trajectory — defined here as the complete direction and outcome of your life throughout your life. Follow the principle from Carlos Castaneda that the book's framework puts forward — the book's framework being the model presented in the book for responding to perceived unfairness — of a person with practical knowledge who lives by acting authentically on your own chosen standards rather than conforming to others' behavior, who knows that no single event permanently determines your life's trajectory in the grand scheme, chooses any act, and acts it out as if it mattered in the moment, yet knows it does not define the complete arc of your life. Replace the sentence "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate," or "I'd prefer…" This begins to accept reality — defined here as the world as it actually operates, without inherent fairness — without necessarily approving of it. Eliminate comparisons to others — where comparisons means judgments of how you measure up to others. Have your own goals — defined here as personal objectives independent of others — independent of what any other person does. Set out to be what you want — where "what you want" means your own desires separate from external expectations — without references to what others have or don't have. Correct yourself out loud when you use a sentence such as "I always call you when I'm going to be late, so why didn't you call me?" to "I would have felt better if you had called me." This eliminates the assumption that another person's reason for calling is to become more like you. Rather than repaying someone by bringing wine or a gift in exchange for dinner or a party, wait until you genuinely want to and then send it with a note saying, "Just because I think you're a great person." Eliminate equal-value exchanges; do something kind because you choose to, not because a special occasion calls for it. Spend the amount of money you want to on any gift, and don't be swayed by what was spent for you. Eliminate invitations based on obligation and fairness. Decide whom you'll see on an internal basis rather than external basis. Set up your own standards of conduct in your family based upon what you think is appropriate for you; have everyone else do the same. See if it is possible to make it happen without infringing on each other's rights. If you feel that being out three nights a week is what you want, but can't because someone has to watch the children, "fairness" does not have to enter into your decision-making. Perhaps make a babysitting arrangement, or have the children go along, or whatever will be a mutually acceptable arrangement. Bringing in the "It isn't fair" routine will lead to all resentment — and to staying at home as well. Be someone who takes action, not someone who only complains about unfairness. For every unfair situation you experience, there is a resolution that does not require you to be immobilized in any way, according to the book's framework. Remember that retaliation is just another way of being controlled by others. Do what you, not they, decide is for you.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Eliminate comparisons to others — have your own goals independent of what others have or don't have
- ✅ Correct yourself out loud when you catch yourself making fairness-based comparisons — where "when" refers to the moment you notice yourself making a fairness comparison ("I would have felt better when…" or "I felt worse because…")

- ✅ Set your own standards of conduct in relationships and family life rather than enforcing external ideas of fairness
- ✅ View jealousy as a harmful thinking pattern that has nothing to do with your sense of self-assessed competence and worth in domains you have defined as personally meaningful
- ✅ Be someone who takes action, not someone who only complains about unfairness — find responses that do not require you to stay stuck
- ❌ Don't keep a tally sheet of give-and-take in relationships — it breeds resentment and prevents genuine connection
- ❌ Don't use "It isn't fair" as a justification for doing things you don't want to do or resenting people you care about
- ❌ Don't cause yourself lasting negative emotions — jealousy, bitterness, or self-pity — when someone else's behavior disappoints you
- ❌ Don't justify behavior by pointing to others who do the same ("If that person can do it, so can I")
- ❌ Don't seek retaliation — it is just another way of letting others control your emotional life
- ❌ Don't measure your life, your gifts, or your behavior by what others do or what you believe you deserve

## Security & Safety Notes

This chapter addresses emotional and psychological patterns — specifically the demand for fairness, jealousy, and resentment — rather than digital or financial security. The strategies described are intended to free you from emotional immobilization caused by perceived unfairness — defined here as the subjective experience of unequal treatment, not necessarily objective fact. The chapter's principle of acting on your own chosen standards rather than external rules is a call to personal authenticity, not an endorsement of breaking laws or harming others. Note: replacing "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate" assumes this reframing reduces negative emotions; this assumption is acknowledged but not empirically proven.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You keep a mental tally sheet in your relationships, tracking who did what for whom and resenting any perceived unfairness.
  **Solution:** Discard the habit of focusing on what others are doing. Ask yourself whether your tally-keeping is making you happier or more miserable. Choose to give and act based on what you genuinely want, not on what you believe you deserve.

- **Problem:** You are stuck by jealousy because you believe someone else's behavior is unfair toward you, and you define your sense of self-assessed competence and worth by how you compare to them.
  **Solution:** Recognize that another person's choices were made independently of you and have nothing to do with your sense of self-assessed competence and worth in domains you have defined as personally meaningful. View their behavior as something between other people rather than as something against you. Decide that you will not allow yourself to be distraught when someone else does not behave the way you want them to.

- **Problem:** You use "It isn't fair" repeatedly over time — at home, at work, in traffic — and this refrain has become a persistent filter through which you experience life, breeding ongoing resentment.
  **Solution:** Replace "It isn't fair" with "It's unfortunate," or "I'd prefer…" This begins to accept reality without necessarily approving of it. When you catch yourself in a fairness comparison, ask: "Will ongoing negative emotions change this?" If not, shift to what you can do about it rather than what you believe you are owed.

- **Problem:** You justify behavior by pointing to others who do the same thing ("If that person can do it, so can I"), or you refuse to act effectively because you believe the world is unfair for your effort to matter.
  **Solution:** Within the book's framework, recognize that other people's behavior is not a justification for yours, nor is it an excuse for your own failure to act. Make a list of what you think is unfair and use it as a guide for constructive action — not as a list of grievances that justify doing nothing. Decide that any unhappiness you experience will be the result of your own behavior, not someone else's.
