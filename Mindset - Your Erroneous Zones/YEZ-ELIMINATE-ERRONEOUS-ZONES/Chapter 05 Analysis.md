# Chapter V: The Useless Emotions—Guilt and Worry

## Overview

This chapter explains that guilt and worry function as immobilizing emotions that prevent taking observable, behaviorally specific actions—such as completing a task, engaging in a planned activity, or fulfilling a commitment—that advance the author's stated goals of reducing guilt and worry and the values of self-determination and personal responsibility in one's current observable behaviors. Guilt anchors you in past regrets by compelling you to dwell on completed events you cannot alter, while worry locks you in fear of future events by compelling you to dwell on hypothetical outcomes you cannot control. The chapter provides specific strategies to identify, defuse, and reduce guilt and worry from your present-moment daily activities. It distinguishes healthy learning from guilt, identifies the cultural, parental, institutional, and psychological forces that promote guilt, and then offers a set of techniques to reclaim your present moment. The chapter closes by extending the same logic to worry, showing that guilt and worry cannot change past events or future outcomes, and that living in the present moment is one approach to reducing their impact.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you find yourself unable to act due to regret over a specific past action or decision
- When anticipatory fear about future events prevents you from acting in the present moment
- When you have verifiable behavioral evidence that guilt is being used—by others or by yourself—as an instrument of influence or avoidance

## Steps

### Step 1: Recognize That Guilt and Worry Are Useless

Recognize that guilt immobilizes you in the present over past behavior, and worry immobilizes you in the present over future events. Understand that guilt and worry serve a similar function of preventing action in your present moment. Robert Jones Burdette expressed it in plain, straightforward language about people: "It isn't the experience of today that drives people mad. It is the remorse for something that happened yesterday, and the dread of what tomorrow may disclose." As for worry, the chapter asks within its framework: "Is there any specific outcome that will ever change as a result of my worrying about it?" The chapter's framework answers no.

### Step 2: Distinguish Guilt From Learning From Your Past

Identify whether you are simply learning from your past and vowing to avoid repeating the specific behavior you just performed (this is NOT guilt). Determine if you are unable to take action now as a result of having behaved in a certain way previously (this IS guilt). Learning from mistakes supports growth. Guilt uses present-moment cognitive and emotional resources feeling hurt, upset, and depressed about a specific past event. Guilt cannot undo past events.

### Step 3: Identify the Two Basic Origins of Guilt

Determine if your guilt originates from "Leftover Guilt" — emotional reactions carried from childhood experiences of parental disapproval such as "Daddy won't like you if you do that again," "You should feel ashamed of yourself," or "Oh, all right, I'm only your mother." Determine if your guilt originates from "Self-imposed Guilt" — guilt imposed on yourself for violating an explicit adult rule or personal moral code you currently hold, such as having told a specific named person off and disliking yourself for it, feeling drained because of shoplifting, skipping a scheduled religious service you attend, or having said a specific past statement that conflicts with the moral standards you now endorse. In either case, guilt does not achieve its intended purpose.

### Step 4: Reject Cultural Messages That Promote Guilt

Recognize certain cultural attitudes that say, "If it's fun, you're supposed to feel guilty about it." Learn to experience pleasure without guilt. See yourself as an individual capable of choosing any action that fits your own value system and does not harm others — and performing it without guilt. If you do something and don't like it or yourself after having done it, vow to change the behavior you just performed in the future rather than experiencing guilt.

### Step 5: Recognize Parental Guilt Tactics

Identify parental guilt manipulation techniques. Examples include: "Never mind then, I'll do it—with my bad back. You just sit there and enjoy yourself." Recognize the "I sacrificed for you" mentality (e.g., "I went through eighteen hours of labor just to bring you into the world," "I stayed married to your father because of you"). Notice when parents use phrases like "We'll stay here by ourselves. You just enjoy yourself the way you've always done. Don't worry about us." Be aware of the "You disgraced us" tactic or "What will the neighbors think?" Recognize parental illness guilt: "You've made my blood pressure go up," "Your behavior is causing me acute emotional distress," "Your actions are causing me physical illness." Notice sexual guilt imposed by parents (e.g., "God forbid you should masturbate. It's bad," "You should be ashamed for reading such magazines"). The book illustrates this with Donny's mother using her chronic back pain to manipulate him into bringing up chairs, and the 52-year-old pediatrician who keeps his marriage a secret from his 85-year-old mother because he fears it might "kill her."

### Step 6: Recognize Lover- and Spouse-Related Guilt Tactics

Identify when a romantic partner, family member, or close associate uses the "If you loved me" guilt tactic to punish a partner for a specific action or choice. Recognize that grudges, extended periods of silence lasting hours or days, and hurtful looks—such as glaring, eye-rolling, or turning away during conversation—are methods of engendering guilt (e.g., "I'm not talking to you, that'll fix you"). Notice when past incidents are recalled to maintain guilt (e.g., "But don't forget what you did on [specific past date]"). Identify guilt used to make a love partner conform (e.g., "If you were responsible, you would have called me"). Examples of guilt-inducing phrases include: "How do you expect me to be loving after what you've done?" and "How could I ever trust you again, when you let me down before?"

### Step 7: Recognize Children-Inspired Guilt Tactics

Recognize when children use guilt to manipulate parents (e.g., "Sally's father lets her do it," "You don't love me. If you did you wouldn't treat me this way," "I must be adopted. My real parents wouldn't treat me like this"). Understand that children learn these guilt-inducing behaviors by watching adults use them. A child's public tantrum may result in the child receiving the desired object, privilege, or exemption from responsibility because the parent does not want to be seen as neglectful, overly permissive, or failing to uphold household rules.

### Step 8: Recognize School-Inspired Guilt Tactics

Identify guilt messages from teachers and authority figures (e.g., "Your mother is really going to be disappointed in you," "You should be ashamed of yourself for getting a C—with your academic ability," "How could you hurt your parents like that, after all they've done for you?"). Remember that even as an adult, you are a product of those school environments and may still be affected by these messages. For example: "You failed the exam because you did not complete the assigned study, now you'll have to live with yourself."

### Step 9: Recognize Church-Related Guilt Tactics

Identify guilt messages tied to religious doctrine (e.g., "If you loved God, you wouldn't behave that way," "You won't get into heaven unless you change your behavior and seek forgiveness for your actions," "You should feel bad because you haven't attended religious services during the current weekly cycle"). Recognize that these messages use the threat of divine punishment to create present-moment guilt. For example: "You've disobeyed one of the commandments or doctrinal rules of your faith tradition and you should feel ashamed of yourself."

### Step 10: Recognize Other Institutional Guilt Producers

Recognize that institutional agents—including criminal justice system operators—operate on the guilt theory: the rehabilitative premise that if a person sits long enough ruminating on how bad they have been, they will improve because of the guilt. Notice how tipping has become a practice reflecting the guilt of the person served rather than service that meets or exceeds established professional standards for hospitality work. Be aware that littering, smoking, and similar stigmatized behaviors can be used to make you feel guilty, and that a stern look by a stranger can trigger feelings of guilt. Recognize dieting as an area loaded with guilt — if you give in to counterproductive behavior, learn from it rather than feeling guilty. Some service workers use obvious hand-extending gestures and negative facial expressions to produce guilt and get bigger tips.

### Step 11: Understand the Psychological Payoffs for Choosing Guilt

Understand that guilt is an avoidance technique for working on yourself in the present through deliberate practice, honest self-assessment, or behavior change — it shifts responsibility for what you are or are not now to what you were or were not in the past. Recognize that guilt helps you avoid the hard work of changing yourself now and the attendant risks that go with change. Notice the tendency to believe that if you feel guilty for an extended period—such as multiple days or weeks—or with enough intensity to feel you have punished yourself for the specific transgression you committed, you will eventually be released from accountability for that specific transgression. See that guilt can be a means of returning to the psychological state of childhood dependency—the condition in which other people made decisions for you, relieving you of the burden and risk of choosing and being judged for those choices. Recognize that guilt is a method for transferring responsibility for your behavior from yourself to others. Understand that guilt can help you win the approval of others even when they don't approve of your behavior. Recognize that guilt can be a way to win pity from others, indicating low self-esteem.

### Step 12: Use the Strategy — View the Past as Unchangeable

Begin to view the past as something that can never be changed, regardless of how you feel about it. Repeat this sentence until you accept it as a core belief: "My feeling guilty will not change the past, nor will it make me a morally superior person." Use this thinking to help differentiate guilt from learning as a result of your past. The chapter tells of a client who had an extramarital affair and felt guilty, but continued the affair for months—demonstrating that his guilt was easier than examining his marriage closely.

### Step 13: Use the Strategy — Ask What You Are Avoiding

Ask yourself what specific task or responsibility you are avoiding in the present moment with guilt about the past. Go to work on that particular item, which should eliminate the need for guilt in most cases, though results differ from person to person depending on the specific relationship dynamics or situational complexity of the marital or relational problem being avoided. The extramarital affair client illustrated this: he had two choices—recognize that guilt was easier than examining his marriage, or accept his behavior and live up to his own value system.

### Step 14: Use the Strategy — Accept Your Choices Despite Others' Disapproval

Begin to accept the particular choices you have made about yourself—such as your career path, hobbies, or relationship style—that specific people in your life—such as parents, employers, neighbors, or your spouse—may disapprove of. See that it is common for parents, employers, neighbors, or spouses to take a stand against some of your behavior. Remember that it is necessary that you approve of yourself; the approval of others is pleasant but not essential. Once you no longer need approval, the guilt for behavior which does not bring approval will disappear.

### Step 15: Use the Strategy — Keep a Guilt Journal

Keep a written Guilt Journal and record instances of guilt that arise. Note when the guilt occurs, what specific identifiable event or statement caused it, and with whom it occurs. Document the specific task or responsibility you are avoiding in the present moment with this focus on the past. Use the journal to gain helpful insights into your particular guilt patterns.

### Step 16: Use the Strategy — Reconsider Your Value System

Reconsider your guiding principles — determine which principles are consistent with your actual behavior and which principles you claim to hold but routinely violate in specific situations: inauthentic principles you pretend to accept in order to gain approval or avoid conflict. List values you pretend to hold. Resolve to live up to a code of ethics that is self-determined, not one imposed by others. As the chapter states: "List all of these phony values and resolve to live up to a code of ethics that is self-determined, not one that has been imposed by others."

### Step 17: Use the Strategy — Make a Guilt Points List

List the specific past actions you regret. Assign yourself guilt points for each of them on a scale of one to ten using defined criteria for each point value. Add up your score. Observe that it makes no practical difference in the present whether the score totals one hundred points or one million points — the current moment is still unchanged, and all of your guilt is merely time spent ruminating on past events without producing change.

### Step 18: Use the Strategy — Assess Real Consequences Rather Than Emotional Feelings

Assess the verifiable consequences of your behavior. Rather than looking for an emotional feeling to determine yes's and no's in your life, determine whether the results of your actions are satisfying and produce defined, useful outcomes for you.

### Step 19: Use the Strategy — Teach Others They Cannot Manipulate You With Guilt

Teach the specific people in your life—such as family members, partners, or coworkers—who attempt to manipulate you with guilt that you are perfectly capable of handling their disappointment in you. Learn new responses that do not accept guilt (e.g., "Okay, Mom, if you want to risk your back on a few chairs because you can't wait a few minutes, I guess there is little I can do to dissuade you"). Understand that with consistent non-reinforcement of guilt-producing behaviors, their behavior will change once they see they cannot force you to choose guilt. Once you defuse the guilt, the emotional control over you and the possibility of manipulation are eliminated.

### Step 20: Use the Strategy — Do Something That Will Result in Guilt

Deliberately choose an action that you anticipate will trigger the emotion of guilt—such as declining an invitation without a lengthy explanation, or spending money on yourself without seeking approval from others—to practice tolerating the emotion without yielding to it. As you check into a hotel and a bellboy is assigned to show you to a room you can find alone, announce that you'll do it alone. If rejected, tell your unwanted companion that he is wasting his time and energy since you will not be leaving a tip for a service you don't want. Take a defined solo period—such as one weekend or one full week—to be alone if you've always wanted to do so, despite protestations from other family members. Use these behaviors to tackle the pervasive guilt that institutional sectors of the environment are adept at engendering.

### Step 21: Use the Strategy — Role-Working Exercise to Defuse Parental Guilt

Engage in structured response-practice exercises to practice outmaneuvering guilt-producing statements. When responding to guilt-producing statements, begin responses with references to the other person as responsible for their own emotional reactions (e.g., say "You feel" rather than "I feel") to reduce the potential for guilt. Practice refusing to yield to obvious guilt producers. Learn new responses that do not accept the guilt (e.g., "Is there anything you'd like to say to me before you leave?" in response to "I'm having a tachycardia attack right now. I think I'm going to die. You're killing me"). The chapter includes a dialogue between daughter and mother where the daughter's responses begin with "You feel" rather than "I feel," reducing the potential for guilt.

### Step 22: Understand Worry — Definition and Distinction From Planning

Understand that worry is defined as being immobilized in the present as a result of things that are going or not going to happen in the future. Do NOT confuse worrying with planning for the future — if the present-moment activity contributes to a demonstrably better future outcome, this is NOT worry. Recognize that worry is only present when you are immobilized in the present about a future happening. "If you are planning, and the present-moment activity will contribute to a demonstrably better future outcome, then this is not worry. It is worry only when you are in any way immobilized in the present about a future happening."

### Step 23: Recognize That Worry Does Not Equal Caring

Recognize the cultural equating of worrying with caring ("Of course I'm worried, it's only natural when you care about someone"). Understand that worry is not the same as love, which predicates a relationship in which each person has the right to be what they choose without any necessary conditions imposed by the other. Stop proving your love by worrying at a scheduled "worry-time" period. The chapter cites the Civil War example: "In 1975, 110 years after the end of the Civil War in 1865, all of those worriers are dead and all their combined worrying did not change a moment of what is now history."

### Step 24: Use the Strategy — Ask What You Are Avoiding With Worry

When you catch yourself worrying, ask yourself: "What specific task or responsibility am I avoiding right now by filling the present moment with worry?" Begin to address whatever it is you're avoiding. Remember that the best antidote to worry is action. The chapter tells of a client who spent a three-hour period playing tennis, touch football, and Ping-Pong while another man continued to worry about the stock market. Neither man's behavior produced any measurable effect on the stock market's direction or outcome.

### Step 25: Use the Strategy — Recognize the Preposterousness of Worry

Ask yourself this question three times in a row: "Will the particular outcome I am worried about actually change as a result of my worrying about it?" Repeatedly confront yourself with this question to see the futility of worry.

### Step 26: Use the Strategy — Designate "Worry-Time"

Designate a ten-minute period once in the morning and once in the afternoon as your worry periods. Use these periods to consider every potential concern you can fit into the time slot. Using your ability to control your own thoughts, postpone any further worry until your next designated "worry-time." You will see the waste of using any time in this fashion and will eventually reduce your worry significantly, assuming you practice consistently over time.

### Step 27: Use the Strategy — Make a Worry List

Make a worry list of every specific concern you worried about during the previous 24 hours, the previous seven days, and the previous twelve months. See if any of your worry produced a measurable, useful result for you. Assess how many of the specific things you worried about actually occurred. Recognize that worry is doubly wasteful: it does nothing to alter the future, and the feared negative outcome you imagine sometimes turns out to be less severe than expected or even beneficial when the actual event occurs. The chapter includes a complete Worry Sheet listing 27 common worries (children, health, dying, job, economy, heart attack, security, spouse's happiness, doing the right thing, healthy child, prices, accidents, what others think, weight, money, car breaking down, bills, parents dying, getting into heaven, weather, getting old, flying, daughter's virginity, talking in front of groups, spouse not calling, going into the city, having nothing to worry about).

### Step 28: Use the Strategy — "Just Worry!" Demonstration

When tempted to worry, stop and turn to a friend, family member, or coworker and say, "Watch me—I'm about to worry." You may find it difficult to demonstrate worry because worry is an internal mental state rather than an observable, externally measurable action that another person can observe and verify.

### Step 29: Use the Strategy — The Worry-Eradicating Question

Ask yourself: "What is the most severe negative outcome I can imagine happening to me or to the specific person I am worried about, and what is the statistically probable chance of it occurring based on available evidence?" This can help you see the futility of worry by honestly evaluating both the severity and probability of your feared outcome.

### Step 30: Use the Strategy — Act in Direct Conflict With Your Worry Areas

Deliberately choose to act in a manner that is directly in conflict with your usual subjects of worry—such as finances, health, or relationships. If you habitually and repeatedly save money for the future out of anxiety, worried about accumulating a specific target amount of savings, begin to use your money today. Adopt the mindset: "Being of sound mind, I spent all my money while I was alive." The chapter uses the image of the rich uncle's will: "Being of sound mind, I spent all my money while I was alive."

### Step 31: Use the Strategy — Face Your Fears With Productive Behavior

Begin to face the fears you possess with productive thought and behavior. Identify a particular fear or worry you experience regularly (e.g., fear of wild dogs). Prepare yourself with a practiced, prepared mindset (e.g., carry a rock for protection). Refuse to show fear or back down when confronted with the feared situation. Addressing a fear or worry directly is one productive way to reduce it from your life. The chapter tells of a woman who carried a rock to address her fear of wild dogs on an island off the coast of Connecticut. She refused to slow down when the dogs growled and came running toward her. As the dogs charged forward and encountered someone who refused to back down, they gave up and ran away.

### Step 32: Live in the Present Moment — The Core Principle

Learn to live now and not waste your current moments in immobilizing thoughts about the past or future. Recognize that there is no other moment to live but the present, and all futile guilt and worry are done in the present moment. Apply this understanding to both guilt (past) and worry (future) as they both take time from the present moment. Lewis Carroll's Alice Through the Looking Glass illustrates the principle that: "Promises of future reward, memories of past gratification…but never actual fulfillment in the present moment." This maps directly to how guilt and worry displace present action—guilt fixates on past events that cannot be changed, worry fixates on future events that have not yet occurred, and both prevent you from acting in the present. "It must come sometimes to 'jam-today,'" Alice objected. The challenge the chapter draws from this: "Any jam today? Since it must come sometime, how about this present moment?"

## Examples

A client who had an extramarital affair felt guilty but continued the affair for months. The point was made that his guilt was easier than examining his marriage closely—illustrating that guilt can function as an avoidance technique for working on yourself in the present through deliberate practice, honest self-assessment, or behavior change, shifting responsibility to the past rather than addressing current problems.

A client spent a three-hour period playing tennis, touch football, and Ping-Pong while another man continued to worry about the stock market. Donny's mother used her chronic back pain to manipulate him into bringing up chairs, saying "Never mind then, I'll do it—with my bad back. You just sit there and enjoy yourself." You may find it difficult to demonstrate worry because worry is an internal mental state rather than an observable, externally measurable action that another person can observe and verify.

### Example 5: The Bellboy and Taking a Week Alone

To actively defuse guilt, do something you know will produce the emotion of guilt in yourself—such as announcing to a bellboy in a hotel that you'll find your room alone, or taking a week alone despite protestations from family members. These behaviors tackle the pervasive guilt that institutional sectors of the environment—such as workplaces, schools, churches, and media—are adept at engendering.

### Example 6: The Civil War Worriers

## Best Practices

- ✅ Distinguish between learning from the past (action-oriented, directed toward current action) and guilt (paralyzing, directed toward past events that cannot be changed)
- ✅ Keep a Guilt Journal to track patterns of guilt triggers and the specific tasks or responsibilities you are avoiding in the present moment
- ✅ Ask "What am I avoiding now?" whenever you catch yourself feeling guilty or worried
- ✅ Designate fixed "worry-time" periods—such as 4:00 PM to 4:10 PM—to contain your worry rather than letting it consume your day
- ✅ Teach others they cannot manipulate you with guilt by calmly responding without accepting the guilt
- ✅ Act in deliberate opposition to your typical worry-driven behaviors—such as compulsively checking accounts, ruminating about health, or replaying social interactions—to test whether the feared outcomes are manageable
- ✅ Face fears with action-oriented behavior rather than avoiding the feared situation
- ✅ Live in the here-and-now moment of action — there is no other moment to live but now
- ❌ Do not confuse worrying with planning — planning contributes to the future; worry prevents you from acting in the present moment
- ❌ Do not believe that feeling intensely guilty for an extended period will absolve you of responsibility for the past event or undo the past
- ❌ Do not accept that worry is the same as caring — worry is not the same as love
- ❌ Do not allow others to use "If you loved me" or claims of having sacrificed for you to manipulate your behavior
- ❌ Do not keep a tally of grievances or try to maintain an informal tally of who has given and received fair treatment in relationships
- ❌ Do not let messages from institutions such as churches, schools, or families impose guilt that does not serve your values

## Security & Safety Notes

This chapter addresses emotional and psychological health rather than digital or financial security. The strategies described are self-help exercises intended for developing specific emotional skills. If you are experiencing guilt, anxiety, or worry that is severe enough to interfere with sleep, appetite, work performance, or relationships—meaning it substantially impairs your ability to carry out routine daily responsibilities such as attending work, maintaining hygiene, or fulfilling family obligations—consider seeking support from a licensed mental health professional with credentials in cognitive-behavioral therapy or a related discipline. The chapter's advice to "do something that will result in guilt" as an exposure exercise—deliberately choosing guilt-producing actions to practice tolerating the emotion without yielding to it—should be applied thoughtfully and at a pace that is safe for you.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You confuse learning from past mistakes with guilt and continue to immobilize yourself with severe self-criticism.
  **Solution:** Ask whether you are unable to take action now as a result of past behavior (guilt) or actively planning to avoid repeating it (learning). If it is guilt, use the Guilt Journal to identify the specific task or responsibility you are avoiding in the present moment and take action on it instead.

- **Problem:** You accept guilt-based manipulation attempts from parents, partners, or children because you fear being labeled as selfish or ungrateful by others.
  **Solution:** Respond using "You feel" language rather than "I feel" language, making the other person responsible for their own emotional reactions. Practice new responses until the guilt producers in your life learn they cannot control you this way.

- **Problem:** You worry about the future and mistake this anxiety for expressions of caring or for planning that produces concrete future benefits.
  **Solution:** Ask "Is there any specific outcome that will produce a measurable improvement as a result of my worrying about it?" If the present-moment activity does not contribute to a demonstrably better future outcome, it is worry — not planning. Use designated "worry-time" periods to contain it, and shift to productive action as the approach to addressing concerns.

- **Problem:** You demand exact reciprocity or tit-for-tat fairness and keep an informal tally of who has given and received fair treatment in relationships, which breeds persistent resentment over weeks, months, or years and prevents authentic emotional connection with others.
  **Solution:** Recognize that exact 50-50 balance in every exchange is not required in personal relationships. Focus on whether your record-keeping is producing a net increase or decrease in your overall life satisfaction, and choose to live by your own self-determined principles rather than comparing yourself to the behavior or outcomes of other people.
