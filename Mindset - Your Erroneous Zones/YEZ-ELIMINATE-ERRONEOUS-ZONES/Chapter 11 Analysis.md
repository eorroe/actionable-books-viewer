# Chapter XI: Farewell to Anger

## Overview

This chapter presents the book's view that hostile angry outbursts are a choice rather than a necessary human response, and that you can learn to feel the emotion of anger internally without expressing it through hostile verbal outbursts, insults, or aggressive behavior that harms yourself or others or interferes with daily responsibilities—such as missing work, damaging relationships, or disrupting household routines—by consciously choosing how you think and react. It provides practical strategies for delaying an angry response, using a designated feedback partner who gives you feedback when they see you becoming angry, keeping a written log of situations in which you felt angry, and reducing hostile impulses in the initial moments after the trigger before an angry response escalates. The chapter also addresses deeper psychological patterns such as beliefs about how others should behave, the psychological rewards that reinforce angry responses, and the deliberate use of humor as a remedy. By learning to think thoughts other than angry thoughts when frustrated, you can replace angry responses that interfere with your activities with more fulfilling emotions.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you find yourself responding with hostile anger to routine frustrations—such as being cut off in traffic, slow service, or someone disagreeing with your opinion—that cause you to feel annoyed, or to actions by people you interact with regularly—such as family members, coworkers, or friends—that differ from your preferences
- When your angry responses are causing arguments, withdrawal, or reduced trust with people you interact with regularly, and you want practical strategies to reduce angry outbursts to a point where they no longer damage those relationships or disrupt your daily activities
- When you need to break the habit of using anger as a tool for manipulation or control

## Steps

### Step 1: Recognize That Anger Is a Choice, Not a Human Necessity

1. Recognize that hostile angry outbursts are an erroneous zone, a state in which hostile feelings disrupt your ability to think and act clearly, causing you to say or do actions you later regret and preventing you from choosing a reasoned response.
2. Stop accepting anger as a part of your life by eliminating the internal sentence: "If only you were more like me."
3. Begin to think of yourself as someone who can learn to think thoughts other than angry thoughts when frustrated so that angry responses that interfere with your activities—such as arguments, damaged relationships, or missed commitments—can be replaced by more fulfilling emotions.
4. Accept that annoyance, irritation, and disappointment are feelings you will continue to experience, but hostile angry outbursts can be reduced to a level that no longer disrupts your daily functioning.

The book notes that you may have justified quick angry responses by saying "It's only human" or "If I don't express it, then I'll store it up and have an ulcer," but these statements do not match documented outcomes. For example, many people who control their anger never develop ulcers, and expressing anger does not solve practical problems—such as resolving conflicts or improving relationships—or improve outcomes in daily interactions.

### Step 2: Use Controlled Outward Display of Anger as a Strategy for Teaching

1. When trying to use a deliberate outward display of anger—meaning you raise your voice and look stern while remaining internally calm and not experiencing hostile feelings—to constructively teach a child something, display anger outwardly without feeling it.
2. Raise your voice and look stern, but do not experience the physical tension, elevated heart rate, and intrusive thoughts that accompany genuine hostile feelings.
3. Think like this: "This behavior your child is exhibiting is dangerous to her. I want to make her see that playing in the street will not be tolerated. I'll raise my voice to demonstrate my strong feelings about it. But I won't go around being mad."

A mother who cannot master a controlled outward display of anger punishes her children, sends them to their room, screams constantly, and is almost always in a state of irritation. The book explains that angry responses often fail to produce lasting behavioral change in others—defined as change that persists beyond a few weeks. According to the book, only a minority of angry responses produce such change, and they consistently increase the other person's resistance to the angry person's demands.

### Step 3: Allow Others the Right to Be Different

1. Remind yourself at the moment of anger that everyone has the right to make their own choices.
2. Stop demanding that anyone be different, as this will simply prolong your anger.
3. Work at allowing others to choose just as you insist on your own right to do the same.

Instead of choosing anger when you run into someone or something you don't like, you can begin to think of others as having a right to be different from what you'd prefer. You may not like it, but you don't have to be angry about it.

### Step 4: Postpone Anger to Gain Control

1. If you typically respond with anger in a particular circumstance, delay the angry response for fifteen seconds, and then respond with anger using the same words, tone, and intensity that you would normally use in that situation.
2. Next try thirty seconds, and keep lengthening the intervals.
3. Once you begin to see that you can delay an angry response, you will have learned control.
4. With regular practice over time, you will eventually reduce angry responses to a point where you may feel annoyance or frustration but do not allow them to interfere with work, family obligations, or social plans.

Delaying an angry response is a form of control; the book presents this as a progressive practice in lengthening the intervals between provocation and angry response.

### Step 5: Get in Touch with Your Thoughts at the Time of Anger

1. First and most important, identify the exact thoughts you are having at the time of your anger.
2. Remind yourself that you don't have to think that way simply because you've always done so in the past.
3. Remember that awareness is paramount.

The book presents the view that angry responses result from thoughts you choose, rather than being automatic reactions that happen without your input. When faced with circumstances that are not going the way you would like, you tell yourself that those circumstances should not be happening, and then you select a familiar angry response.

### Step 6: Use a Trusted Person Who Gives You Feedback

1. Ask a spouse, relative, or longtime friend who knows you well and whose opinion you value to help.
2. Have them tell you when they see your anger either verbally or with an agreed signal.
3. When you get the signal, think about what you're doing and then try the postponing strategy.

This is presented as a concrete strategy for reducing the speed of your angry responses by using feedback from another person.

### Step 7: Keep a Written Log of Angry Situations

1. Keep a written log of situations in which you responded with anger, and record the date, location, triggering situation, and the thought that preceded the angry response.
2. Be consistent with the entries; force yourself to record all angry behavior.
3. After consistent logging over a period of weeks, you will likely find that the very act of having to write each incident down will persuade you to choose anger less often.

The journal is designed to make you accountable to yourself by creating a written record of every angry choice.

### Step 8: Announce After an Angry Outburst That You Responded With Anger When You Intended Not To

1. After you have had an angry outburst, state aloud that you just responded with anger when you intended not to.
2. State that one of your goals is to think differently so that you don't experience this anger.
3. The verbal statement will remind you of the behavior you want to change, and will show that you are actively trying to change your behavior.

This verbal acknowledgment serves as a reminder of your goal to reduce angry responses.

### Step 9: Use Physical Proximity With Someone You Trust to Reduce Angry Feelings

1. Try sitting or standing near someone close to you when you feel angry.
2. One way to reduce your angry feelings is to hold hands, despite your desire to pull away.
3. Keep holding hands until you have expressed how you feel and feel less angry.

This is presented as a direct physical approach—such as holding hands or sitting close—to break the cycle of angry reaction in a loving relationship.

### Step 10: Discuss Anger-Provoking Patterns When You Are Not Angry

1. Talk with the people who experience your anger most often—typically a spouse, children, or coworkers—at a time when you are not angry, and discuss the specific situations and behaviors that most often provoke an angry response.
2. Share with each other the specific activities that provoke anger in the other person.
3. Devise a way of communicating your feelings without debilitating anger.
4. Agree on a written note, a messenger, or taking a five-minute walk alone to calm down, so that you do not respond to each other with anger during disagreements.
5. After using this approach multiple times, you will begin to recognize that angry responses do not solve the problem.

This exercise is designed to create mutual understanding and agreed-upon protocols for handling conflict before it escalates.

### Step 11: Defuse Anger in the First Ten Seconds

1. Reduce your angry response in the initial moments after the trigger by naming the emotion you are experiencing.
2. Also label how you believe your partner feels as well.
3. Focus on the first ten seconds after provocation as the most important window for intervention; once you have passed that ten-second window, your angry response intensity often decreases naturally within the next thirty seconds.

The book identifies the first ten seconds after provocation as the most important window for intervention before an angry response escalates.

### Step 12: Expect Disagreement from Others

1. Keep in mind that about half of the people you encounter will disagree with your preferences about half of the time.
2. Once you expect others to disagree, you can reduce the likelihood that you will respond with anger.
3. Instead, remind yourself that the world operates as it does because other people are not obligated to agree with everything you say, think, feel, and prefer.

This reframing exercise helps you accept disagreement as normal rather than as a trigger for anger.

### Step 13: Reduce Beliefs About How Others Should Behave

1. Reduce the beliefs you have about how others should behave.
2. When these beliefs decrease, angry responses tend to decrease as well.

The book states that most of the time, people and things will not match your preferences, and according to the book's framework the likelihood that you can change them through angry responses is very low—estimated at a small minority of angry responses. Every time you respond with anger when you run into someone or something you do not like, you are choosing to feel hurt or unable to act because of reality.

### Step 14: Remind Yourself About the Nature of Children

1. Remind yourself that children between the ages of two and twelve are naturally physically active and frequently make noise as part of their normal development.
2. Recognize that getting angry about it will not change their behavior.
3. While you can help children to make constructive choices in other areas, accept that you cannot alter their developmental stage.

This is a specific application of the belief-reduction approach to parenting.

### Step 15: Treat Yourself With Respect to Reduce Angry Responses

1. Treat yourself with respect and refuse to let others control your emotional state.
2. If you do, you can reduce the occurrence of angry responses that harm your well-being.

The book frames self-respect as a protective factor against choosing angry responses, because when you think highly of yourself and refuse to let others control you, you can reduce angry responses in the moment.

### Step 16: Use Deliberate Humor to Counteract Anger

1. Encourage others to laugh during situations that are not serious or emotionally charged, such as minor inconveniences or everyday mishaps, when humor is unlikely to cause offense.
2. Learn to step back and notice when situations do not match your expectations.
3. Allow yourself to laugh at small or silly things even when nothing particularly funny has happened. You do not need a specific reason to laugh.
4. Observe yourself and others in the world around you, and then decide whether to carry around anger or to use humor deliberately.

The book claims that you cannot experience genuine anger and laughter simultaneously. Angry feelings and laughter do not occur together, and you can choose which emotional state to pursue. The book quotes Winston Churchill: "It is my belief, you cannot deal with the most serious things in the world unless you understand the most amusing."

### Step 17: Use a Traffic Jam as a Practice Session for Delaying Angry Responses

1. In a traffic jam where your vehicle is stationary or moving less than five miles per hour, time yourself using a watch or phone timer. See how long you can go without responding with anger.
2. Practice delaying your angry response.
3. Instead of yelling at the person sitting next to you in the car, ask them a neutral question about the situation or the other person's day.
4. Use the time to do something that occupies your mind: write a letter, compose a song, or devise ways out of the traffic jam, or recall a positive intimate experience, or better yet, plan a future intimate experience you would enjoy.

The traffic jam is presented as a specific opportunity to practice delaying and shifting your focus away from angry thoughts during a period of enforced waiting.

### Step 18: Use Frustrating Situations as a Problem to Solve, Not a Reason to Respond With Anger

1. Instead of being a person controlled by your emotional reactions to every frustrating circumstance, use the situation as an opportunity to take action to improve it.
2. By focusing on changing the situation, you will have less mental space for angry thoughts.

The book contrasts being a person controlled by emotional reactions to circumstances with using them as opportunities to act, noting that angry responses do not help you solve practical problems or improve situations with others.

### Step 19: Think New Thoughts About Others' Behavior as Their Responsibility, Not Yours

1. Replace angry thoughts with thoughts such as: "If they want to behave in a way that seems foolish, I'm not going to choose to be upset. They, not me, own their behavior."
2. Or think: "Things aren't going the way I think they should. While I don't like it, I'm not going to prevent myself from acting."
3. Refuse to give other people's behavior and ideas the power to upset you.
4. Think highly of yourself and refuse to let others control you.

The book presents these exact internal sentences as replacements for angry thinking, changing the focus from blaming others to managing your own responses.

### Step 20: Recognize the Rewards That Maintain Angry Responses

1. Understand why you may continue to respond with anger, including:
   - Directing responsibility for your emotional state to the other person or event instead of recognizing that you choose your own response
   - Manipulating people who are afraid of your anger into complying with your wishes
   - Drawing attention to yourself and feeling temporarily important or powerful when others submit to your outbursts
   - Using anger as an excuse to behave rudely or aggressively and then justifying that behavior afterward
   - Getting your way because other people choose to accommodate you rather than endure your hostility
   - Avoiding closeness, intimacy, or love by creating conflict over minor issues
   - Manipulating others into feeling guilty so they will do what you want
   - Breaking down or avoiding communication in which you feel vulnerable or threatened
   - Avoiding the difficult inner work of examining and changing your own behavior and beliefs
   - Indulging in self-pity by blaming circumstances or others instead of taking responsibility for your response
   - Avoiding clear thinking by allowing emotion to override logical analysis of the situation
   - Excusing poor performance at work, losing at games, or failing at tasks by blaming others or circumstances
   - Justifying your angry responses as "just human nature" or "everyone gets angry"
2. Once you see these rewards, you can begin to reduce the rewards that maintain your angry responses.

The book lists these specific psychological rewards to help you understand why you may respond with anger without deciding to, so you can begin to change the thinking that leads to angry responses.

## Security & Safety Notes

- If you are in a relationship with someone who uses threatening behavior, the silent treatment, or violence as control strategies, seek professional help and safety planning immediately
- When using a nonverbal signal with a dominant partner, ensure the signal does not escalate conflict or put you at risk

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You react with anger to multiple frustrating situations each week, letting circumstances control your emotions and cause arguments, withdrawal, or reduced trust with coworkers, family members, or friends you see daily or weekly.
  **Solution:** Delay your angry response for fifteen seconds, then thirty, and keep lengthening the intervals. Once you begin to see that you can delay an angry response, you have developed the ability to choose when to respond.

- **Problem:** You have beliefs about how others should behave, and respond with anger when those people's actual behavior does not match your mental image of how they should act.
  **Solution:** Reduce the beliefs you have about how others should behave. When these beliefs decrease, angry responses tend to decrease. Remind yourself that frequently people and things will not match your preferences.

- **Problem:** You think angry responses are just "human nature" and that you cannot change them.
  **Solution:** Recognize that hostile angry outbursts are a choice, not a necessary human response. Accept that annoyance and disappointment are feelings you will continue to experience, but angry responses can be reduced to a level that no longer disrupts your daily activities.

- **Problem:** You want to use anger to teach or control others, but the book states that angry responses rarely produce lasting behavioral change in others, and they only increase resistance.
  **Solution:** When trying to use a controlled outward display of anger constructively to teach, display anger outwardly without feeling it. Raise your voice and look stern, but do not experience the physical tension, elevated heart rate, and intrusive thoughts that accompany genuine hostile feelings.
