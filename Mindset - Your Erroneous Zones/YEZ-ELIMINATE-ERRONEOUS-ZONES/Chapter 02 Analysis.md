# Chapter II: Your Unchanging Right to Pursue Your Own Well-Being Regardless of Circumstances or Others' Opinions

## Overview

This chapter presents the claim that each individual person has an unchanging right to pursue their own well-being and personal life goals as a starting premise—not something to be earned through approval from family members, friends, coworkers, or strangers; not something confirmed by other people's behavior toward you; and not something borrowed from other people's expressed positive regard for you. It provides the foundational argument that your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals is present regardless of the specific choices you make, the explicit opinions other people express about you, or the specific results you produce, along with practical exercises for building and maintaining consistent actions that treat yourself as worthy of consideration. The chapter also identifies the specific psychological advantages people gain from criticizing themselves and offers a structured set of actions for eliminating self-critical statements and actions and replacing them with accurately describing your current situation without assigning a global negative judgment to yourself as a whole person.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you find yourself depending on explicit approval from specific individuals—verbal compliments, favorable responses, or visible signs such as smiles, nods, or verbal praise from them—to judge yourself as acceptable as a person by your own standards
- When you engage in self-critical actions—refusing verbal compliments from others, making excuses about your physical appearance to specific people, or refusing yourself activities you genuinely want to do
- When you want to build a consistent self-assessment that does not fluctuate with events outside your direct control, specific measurable results such as completed tasks or achieved goals you produce, or the explicit opinions specific individuals express about you

## Steps

### Step 1: Claim Your Unchanging Right to Pursue Your Own Well-Being and Life Goals Without Relying on Other People's Approval

Assess your own right to pursue your own well-being and life goals without relying on the explicit opinions specific other people express or the way they behave toward you. Stop depending on other people's reactions for your own judgment of yourself as acceptable or valuable; if you do, your assessment becomes based on other people's opinions rather than your own independent judgment. If you trust your own assessment of situations, you neither want nor need other people to conform to the explicit standards you have stated to them. When you accept yourself and feel secure in your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals, you can help other people without needing a specific response from them—not for verbal expressions of gratitude or tangible benefits returned to you, but because of the personal satisfaction that comes from being a supportive person to others. Helping other people without needing a specific response from them requires that you already treat yourself as worthy of the same consideration you give to others. If your current self-assessment is negative, helping other people becomes difficult.

### Step 2: Separate Your Unchanging Right to Pursue Your Own Well-Being From Your Specific Choices and Actions

Separate your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals—which is an unchanging starting premise—from your specific choices and actions and from the specific choices and actions of other people toward you. Reframe internal messages: instead of "I am unacceptable because I acted poorly in this specific situation," think "That person does not approve of my choice. That is that person's independent decision, and while I would prefer that person's approval, I am still a person with inherent worth." Even if you have made a choice that contradicts the values you have explicitly stated to yourself and the behavioral standards you have deliberately chosen for yourself, do not assign a global negative judgment to your entire person based on one specific choice. Learn from the specific choice and resolve not to repeat it, but do not associate it with your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals.

### Step 3: Acknowledge Your Current Circumstances and Qualities Without Assigning a Global Negative Judgment to Yourself Across All Areas

Reject the claim that you have one single overall description of yourself as a person that is either entirely positive or entirely negative. You have a range of specific self-assessments in different areas such as work, relationships, and health that vary from situation to situation. Do not combine all critical self-assessments into one global negative conclusion when asked "Do you view yourself positively overall?" Break down the areas of dissatisfaction into specific measurable targets for improvement. This gives you specific targets for improvement to work on. Acknowledge that your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals—the unchanging baseline, the basis for your personal well-being and development of new skills—must be unrelated to your specific self-assessments in individual areas. You are a human being. You exist. That is all you need. Choose to judge yourself as acceptable as a person indefinitely, then work on your specific self-assessments in individual areas separately from that unchanging baseline right to pursue your own well-being.

### Step 4: Develop Acceptance of Your Physical Self

Make a written list of every major and minor physical component of your body—hair, forehead, eyes, eyelids, cheeks, mouth, nose, teeth, neck, arms, fingers, breasts, stomach, kidneys, spleen, arteries, femurs, Rolandic fissure, cochlea, pinna, adrenal glands, uvula, and all other physical components. Identify which parts you would prefer to change. For parts that can be changed (for example, stomach size, hair color), view them as choices you made during a specific earlier period of your life and make new decisions about them in the present moment. Make changing them a stated goal if you wish. For parts that cannot be changed (for example, leg length, eye shape, breast size), reject the specific cultural standards of physical appearance that are prevalent in the cultural context where you live and decide to accept your current physical state. No body part is inherently better or worse than any other. Decide to accept your current physical state and declare it acceptable and appealing to you, thereby rejecting the comparisons and expressed opinions of other people. Accept your body as it currently is without shame. Do not be embarrassed or apologetic when your body performs functions without deliberate conscious control from you. If you choose to use cosmetic aids, do so only for experimentation or personal preference—not from dissatisfaction with the underlying physical feature.

### Step 5: Choose Positive Self-Assessments Deliberately

Choose to judge yourself as intelligent by applying your own written criteria to yourself. Understand that your demonstrated skill level in a specific area is directly related to the cumulative hours of deliberate, focused practice you have completed in that area rather than a fixed trait present at birth. With sufficient cumulative hours of focused practice you could develop demonstrated proficiency in the most academic skills. If you dislike your current demonstrated skill level, recognize this as self-criticism, not a permanent limitation on your ability. Apply the same reasoning to all self-assessments: you are as socially skilled, artistically skilled, mechanically skilled, athletically skilled, and musically skilled as the cumulative practice time you have chosen to invest in those areas indicates. Do not confuse your demonstrated skill levels with your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals. Decide now to base your acceptance of your current capabilities on what you believe is achievable given your current demonstrated skill level and cumulative practice time. Improving skills that are less developed can be a satisfying activity.

### Step 6: Identify and Eliminate Self-Sabotaging (Self-Critical) Actions

Become aware of when you engage in self-critical verbal actions and behavioral patterns. Each time you do, you reinforce uncertainty about your own independent judgment and reduce opportunities for close relationships. Stop refusing verbal compliments from others. Accept them with a simple "thank-you." Stop making excuses about your physical appearance to specific individuals. Acknowledge your physical appearance without making excuses to others. Stop assigning your achievements to other people. Speak for yourself. Stop requesting that other people confirm your opinions before you accept them. Trust your own independent judgment. Stop refusing to order something you want because you do not believe you are worthy of your own investment in yourself. Stop deliberately preventing your own sexual satisfaction from occurring. Stop not purchasing items for yourself because you believe you must purchase those same items for other people instead. Stop refusing yourself small sources of pleasure (such as flowers and wine) because you believe the expense is unjustified. Stop reacting in an observable way when someone calls your name in a public space. Stop using derogatory terms you use for yourself (and allowing other people to use them for you): Dum-dum, Silly, Sweetie-pie, Baby (for an adult), Funny-face, Shorty, Fatty, Baldy. Stop interpreting verbal compliments from others as having hidden negative motives like "You must have a drawer full of jewelry at home for other girls" or "You're practically blind or trying to make me feel good." Stop believing that close relationships will end when other people see your actual self. Stop believing other people accept you only out of a sense of obligation or pity.

### Step 7: Acknowledge Your Current Circumstances Without Expressing Dissatisfaction About Circumstances You Cannot Change

Accept yourself as a person with the right to pursue your own well-being and life goals because you choose to extend that acceptance to yourself. Stop expressing dissatisfaction to other people about circumstances you cannot alter through direct action, especially about physical conditions you cannot alter (such as a rough physical surface, cloudy weather, or cold temperature). Stop expressing dissatisfaction to other people about your own dissatisfaction; other people are almost always unable to change that specific external circumstance. Stop allowing other people to impose their own dissatisfaction and unhappiness about circumstances on you. Ask them: "What do you want me to do about this specific circumstance?" or "Can I help you take action to address this specific problem?" Ask yourself the same questions when you notice yourself expressing dissatisfaction about circumstances you cannot change: "Why am I expressing this dissatisfaction to myself right now?" and "Can I take action to improve this specific situation?" Do not express dissatisfaction about being fatigued or physically uncomfortable. If you are fatigued, choose actions other than expressing dissatisfaction about it. Voicing dissatisfaction burdens the listener and will not reduce your fatigue. If you are genuinely working on treating yourself as worthy of consideration and experience physical or emotional discomfort, take action to address it yourself rather than depending on other people for emotional support. If you notice qualities in yourself or other people that you dislike, actively take actions to improve the specific situation rather than expressing dissatisfaction. Practice internally acknowledging your positive actions or helping another person achieve satisfaction of their stated goals instead of expressing dissatisfaction about circumstances you cannot change.

### Step 8: Distinguish Self-Support Without Validation From Other People From Seeking Validation From Other People Through Exaggerated Claims

Understand that treating yourself as worthy of consideration has nothing to do with making exaggerated claims about your achievements designed to receive verbal recognition and agreement from other people. Recognize that exaggerated self-praise is motivated by fear of negative judgment from other people and motivated by concern about how other people see you. It means the individual is basing their self-assessment on the explicit opinions other people have expressed about them. Treating yourself as worthy of consideration without validation from other people requires no agreement from other people. Accepting yourself is sufficient on its own. Do not demand expressions of positive regard from other people as evidence of your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals. Treating yourself as worthy of consideration means you accept yourself regardless of the explicit positive regard other people express toward you.

### Step 9: Examine the Psychological Advantages of Self-Critical Verbal Actions and Behavioral Patterns (The System That Maintains Self-Criticism)

Understand that observable behavior typically has identifiable causes. To eliminate self-critical verbal actions and behavioral patterns that harm your well-being, you must first understand why you engage in those specific observable actions and what specific set of factors such as thoughts, environment, and habits maintains those specific behaviors. Identify which of the following psychological advantages you receive from self-criticism:

- A pre-existing explanation for why you cannot form close relationships: you simply are not worthy of other people's affection.
- Avoiding the risk of close relationships, eliminating any possibility of being rejected or receiving disapproval from other people.
- Avoiding the effort of making changes; if you are not worthy of improvement, there is no defined practical purpose in trying to improve your skills or your current life situation, so you avoid the discomfort of trying and failing at something.
- Receiving sympathy, recognition, and even agreement from other people, which replaces the risk of a close relationship that could fail and leave you hurt.
- Having an explanation for your unhappiness, so you do not have to take action to improve your situation yourself.
- Periods of low mood that allow you to avoid actions that would lead to improvement; directing sadness inward toward yourself serves as a way to avoid expending effort to improve your situation.
- Returning to childhood behavior patterns, calling upon behaviors you learned as a child to please the adult authority figures you learned to regard as having higher social status; returning to childhood behavior patterns feels less threatening than trying new behaviors as an adult.
- Reinforcing dependence on other people by prioritizing other people's needs over your own needs.
- Being unable to make decisions about your own life because you do not believe you are worthy of the happiness you want.

Recognize that these psychological advantages, while unhealthy, are genuine and explain why you continue using old behavior patterns. Recognize that choosing not to develop into a person who supports themselves is a choice that prevents any additional personal development; continued personal development usually involves developing new skills and improving your current life situation.

### Step 10: Master Self-Support Through Practical Actions

**Correct self-critical statements out loud:** When you catch yourself saying similar to "I'm really not that smart, I guess I was just lucky," recognize that you have just behaved in a self-critical way. Correct yourself immediately out loud with a statement such as: "I just said I was lucky, but luck really had nothing to do with it. I earned that grade through my own effort." This is one small step toward treating yourself as worthy of consideration—a recognition of the self-critical statement you just made and a decision to act differently.

**Accept expressions of positive regard and agreement with gratitude:** When other people express positive regard or agreement toward you, choose a different response. Instead of promptly questioning whether their statement is sincere, accept it with "thank-you" or "I'm happy that you feel that way."

**Say "I love you" directly:** If there is someone you feel genuine positive regard toward, say it directly: "I love you." While observing their response, acknowledge your own courage for taking the risk of expressing that feeling.

**Order what you genuinely want:** In a restaurant, order something you genuinely enjoy regardless of the monetary price. Give yourself that item because you have the right to spend your own resources on yourself. Begin to select items that you would prefer in all everyday situations, including the grocery store. Indulge yourself with a favorite product because you have the right to spend your resources on yourself. Stop refusing yourself small sources of pleasure unless a specific, defined practical reason requires it—and such specific practical reasons are uncommon in the book's framework.

  **Rest and restore your energy:** After a physically tiring day and a full meal, take a 20-to-30-minute nap or a walk in a park even if you have tasks remaining to complete. It will help you feel more energized afterward.

  **Join a group or activity that genuinely interests you according to your own interests:** You may have delayed joining such a group because of obligations to other people in your life and perceived time limits on your available time. By choosing to treat yourself as worthy of consideration and participating in activities you want to do, the people you help or care for will begin to develop the ability to manage their own affairs independently. You will help them out of choice rather than obligation, and you will experience reduced resentment toward them.

**Eliminate jealousy:** Recognize that jealousy is a negative judgment of yourself. By comparing yourself to another specific person and imagining you are loved less by that person, you make other people seem more important than you are to yourself. Remind yourself that (1) a specific other person can always choose a different partner without it being a reflection on your worth as a person, and (2) whether or not you are chosen as a close relationship partner by any specific person is not how you assess your own right to pursue your own well-being and life goals. With daily practice at treating yourself as worthy of consideration, situations that previously caused jealousy will produce less emotional distress. You will believe so strongly in your own worth that you will not need expressions of positive regard or agreement from other people to determine your assessment of your own right to pursue your own well-being and life goals.

  **Treat your body well:** Select foods with high nutritional value, reduce excess body weight beyond the range your physician considers healthy based on current medical standards (a medical risk to your physical health as well as an indication of self-criticism about your body), take 30-minute walks or bicycle rides three times per week, choose physical exercise that improves your cardiovascular fitness or muscular strength, spend time outdoors in fresh air because it feels good to you. Keep your body healthy and appealing because you are a person of value and are going to treat yourself that way. Spending an entire day indoors doing unvaried routine tasks without physical movement is a choice that reinforces self-criticism rather than caring for your body.

  **Practice positive sexual self-acceptance:** Stand naked in front of a mirror and tell yourself that you view yourself positively as a sexual being. Become aware of your physical sensations. Explore your own physical sensations to experience pleasurable feelings. With other people, choose sexual satisfaction for yourself rather than making your partner's pleasure more important than your own. Slow down the process of sexual activity, communicating to your partner what you enjoy with both words and physical actions. Choose orgasm for yourself. Achieve maximum physical pleasure for yourself by believing that you have the right to experience sexual pleasure, and then focusing fully on the physical sensations of confirming your own assessment of your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals.

  **Stop equating the specific measurable task outcomes with your right to pursue your own well-being:** You may lose your job or fail to complete a specific project. You may not be satisfied with how you performed a specific task. But that does not mean you are without a right to pursue your own well-being and life goals. You must determine for yourself that you are acceptable as a person regardless of your specific measurable outcomes. Once you eliminate this confusion, you will be able to undertake any project, and your overall measurable outcomes—while they may be relevant to your stated goals—will in no way determine your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals.

## Examples

### Example 1: Noah — Needing Positive Regard Before Saying "I Love You"

Noah could not say "I love you" to his wife and children because he needed explicit confirmation from them—specific verbal or behavioral expressions from them that he was acceptable as a person—in return. Once he recognized that his right to pursue his own well-being and life goals was present before he began the relationship, saying those words would become possible regardless of their specific responses. His withholding of expressions of positive regard was itself a self-critical action rooted in the belief that he needed other people's explicit positive regard to judge himself as acceptable.

### Example 2: Shirley — Damaging Relationships Through Self-Criticism

Shirley damaged each romantic relationship she formed. When a man told her he had positive feelings toward her, her internal thoughts contradicted that statement: "He's only saying that because he knows it's what I want to hear." She searched for statements from him that contradicted her sense of being acceptable because she did not believe she was worthy of receiving affection from another person. The pattern of self-criticism reinforced her sense of being unacceptable to other people. Her behavior exemplifies how self-critical statements reduce opportunities for close relationships.

- ✅ Break down critical self-assessments into specific life areas rather than combining them into one global negative judgment
- ✅ Reject the specific cultural standards of physical appearance prevalent in your society and decide for yourself what is appealing to you

- ✅ Stop refusing yourself small pleasures, treats, or sources of satisfaction
- ✅ Correct self-critical statements out loud at the moment you notice them
- ✅ Accept expressions of positive regard and affection with gratitude
- ✅ Treat your body with care because you have the right to maintain your physical health

- ✅ Know that your overall measurable outcomes in life do not determine your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals
- ❌ Don't depend on other people to assess your own right to pursue your own well-being and life goals or to make you feel acceptable as a person

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You depend on other people to assess your own right to pursue your own well-being and life goals, and feel significantly distressed when their specific response is negative or absent—such as experiencing persistent anxiety, sadness, or self-doubt about their reaction.
  **Solution:** Recognize that your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals is present before you begin any interaction or project. Other people's reactions reflect their own internal state and circumstances, not yours. You neither need nor want other people to conform to your explicit stated standards in order to judge yourself as acceptable as a person.

- **Problem:** You engage in self-critical actions—refusing verbal compliments from others, minimizing your specific measurable outcomes, or refusing yourself activities you genuinely want—that reinforce uncertainty about your own independent judgment and reduce opportunities for close relationships.
  **Solution:** Become aware of each self-critical statement in the specific moment it occurs. Correct it out loud: "I just said I was lucky, but luck had nothing to do with it. I earned that result through my own effort." Accept expressions of positive regard and verbal compliments from other people with "thank-you."

- **Problem:** You confuse treating yourself as worthy of consideration with seeking validation from other people through exaggerated claims about your achievements, and avoid claiming your right to pursue your own well-being and life goals because you do not want to appear exaggerated or dismissive of other people.
  **Solution:** Understand that treating yourself as worthy of consideration is self-acceptance that requires no agreement from other people. Seeking validation from other people through exaggerated claims is behavior motivated by fear of other people's explicit opinions. Treating yourself as worthy of consideration without validation from other people is internal and sufficient without other people's agreement.

- **Problem:** You do not understand why you continue to engage in self-critical actions that harm your well-being even when you are aware that those actions hurt you.
  **Solution:** Identify the specific psychological advantages you receive from self-criticism—such as avoiding the risk of rejection, receiving sympathy from other people, or having a pre-existing explanation for your unhappiness. These psychological advantages, while unhealthy, are genuine and explain why you continue using old behavior patterns. Only by identifying them clearly can you act differently in the future.
