# Chapter IV: Breaking Free from the Past

## Overview

This chapter teaches that self-defeating "I'm" labels—such as "I'm shy," "I'm not good at math," or "I'm clumsy"—function as learned avoidance patterns rather than fixed traits, and serve as a central mechanism through which past experiences shape your current behavior. It provides a structured process for identifying, challenging, and eliminating these labels by understanding their origins, recognizing the costs they impose, and replacing them with language that specifies present-tense choices rather than fixed characterizations. Through 19 focused lessons, the chapter gives you the tools to stop treating yourself as a finished product of your history and start being the sum of the choices you make today.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you catch yourself using self-limiting "I'm" labels—"I'm bad at math," "I'm shy," "I'm not musical"—and accepting them as permanent, unalterable characterizations of who you are
 - When you are repeating these same avoidance behaviors and have been unable to stop, even though you know they are preventing you from achieving the specific goals you have written down for yourself
- When you want to stop letting childhood messages, old choices, or other people's opinions dictate how you describe yourself now

## Steps

### Step 1: Identify and List Your Self-Descriptors ("I'm" Labels)

 Ask yourself: "Who are you?" and "How do you describe yourself?" — write down every label you use. Compile a complete list of your self-descriptors (I'm nervous, I'm shy, I'm lazy, I'm not musical, I'm clumsy, I'm forgetful). Focus specifically on the negative, self-limiting "I'm" labels — the ones that interfere with your stated goals. Examine whether you want to continue using any of these labels. Write down specifically which labels you will stop using and commit to changing them.

### Step 2: Recognize the Four Neurotic Sentences That Keep You Chained to Your Past

 Identify every self-defeating "I'm" you use as explicitly linked to one or more of the following four justification statements:

- "That's me."
- "I've always been that way."
- "I can't help it."
- "That's my nature."

Understand that each time you use one of these sentences, you are saying, "And I intend to continue being the way I've always been." Check whether you use these four sentences at work, at home, or in social gatherings where you could select a different response.

### Step 3: Understand the Two Origins of Your "I'm" Labels

Categorize each of your "I'm" labels into one of two origins:

- **From others:** Others pinned labels on you as a child by parents, teachers, grandparents, and family friends.
- **From self-created avoidance:** Labels you adopted to avoid specific activities.

 For labels from others: Recognize that you accepted these beliefs before you reached age eighteen without examining whether they were true. For self-created labels: Recognize that you used these as inaccurate excuses to avoid specific activities. In both cases, you are stating, "I am a finished product in that specific domain."

### Step 4: Have a Conversation With Those Responsible for Your "I'm" Labels

 Identify the people in your life most directly responsible for your "I'm" labels (parents, family friends who have known you since childhood, teachers from your school years, grandparents). Have a conversation with them in which you ask: "How do you think I got to be the way I am? Have I always been that way?" Tell them: "I have stopped using these labels, and I am acting differently now." Ask them whether they believe you are capable of being different. Be prepared for their interpretations — they may feel you "can't be any different, since you've always been that way."

### Step 5: Learn the Ten Typical "I'm" Categories and Their Neurotic Dividends

 Review the ten categories below and identify which ones apply to you. For every category that matches one of your labels, name the neurotic dividend (payoff) you receive by keeping the label — this represents the specific activity or situation you avoid by keeping the label. Recognize that each neurotic dividend is a form of avoidance. Decide which labels you want to eliminate.

**The Ten Categories:**

1. **I'm poor at math, spelling, reading, languages** — Neurotic dividend: Reduces the perceived reason to put in the effort to change. The academic "I'm" keeps you from putting in sustained effort to master uninteresting subject matter. As long as you label yourself inept, you have a reason to avoid it.

 2. **I'm lousy at concrete skill areas such as cooking, sports, crocheting, drawing, and acting** — Neurotic dividend: Provides a reason to avoid these activities in the future and justifies past performance you consider poor. Reinforces the notion that you should not do activities in which you do not perform well. According to the book's framework, avoidance is preferable to participation for you unless you rank in the highest-performing ten percent in that specific concrete skill area.

 3. **I'm shy, reserved, temperamental, nervous, afraid** — Neurotic dividend: Blames genetics as an excuse. Lets you blame your parents and use them as the reason for your current "I'm." You choose this withdrawn behavior to avoid being assertive in situations where you prefer not to assert yourself. It's a pattern from childhood when others had you believe you were incapable of thinking for yourself. You excuse self-forfeiting behavior as something you cannot control.

4. **I'm clumsy, uncoordinated** — Neurotic dividend: Enables you to avoid potential ridicule for lack of physical skill. The lack of skill may result from avoiding physical activity after accepting the label. "You tend to improve at activities you practice and tend not to improve at activities you avoid."

 5. **I'm unattractive, ugly, big-boned, plain, too tall** — Neurotic dividend: Keeps you from taking risks in romantic situations and justifies poor self-image and lack of romantic relationships. Gives you a ready-made excuse for not putting yourself forward in a love relationship. The labels you have accepted shape your self-perception.

6. **I'm unorganized, meticulous, sloppy** — Neurotic dividend: Convenient for manipulating others and justifying why things must be done a certain way. "I've always done it that way." By relying on tradition, you do not have to consider the possibility of doing it differently, and you can ensure everyone else does it your way. This "I'm" relies on the excuse "I've always done it this way" rather than considering whether a different approach might work.

7. **I'm forgetful, careless, irresponsible, apathetic** — Neurotic dividend: Especially useful to vindicate yourself for ineffective behavior. The "I'm" keeps you from working on your memory or carefulness, and you excuse yourself with "That's me." As long as you use this "I'm," you avoid having to work at changing.

8. **I'm Italian, German, Jewish, Irish, Black, Chinese** — Neurotic dividend: Works well when you run out of other reasons to explain behaviors that don't work for you but are too difficult to tackle. You use your ethnic "I'm" as a justification for stereotypical behavior. Example: A male maître d' responds to being asked why he overreacts, "What do you expect from me? I'm Italian. I can't help it."

 9. **I'm bossy, pushy, authoritarian** — Neurotic dividend: Allows you to continue hostile acts rather than work at developing self-discipline. You cover the behavior with "I can't help it, I've always been that way."

 10. **I'm old, middle-aged, tired** — Neurotic dividend: Uses age as a reason for not participating in risky or threatening activities (sports, dating after divorce or death of a spouse, traveling). Eliminates the risks of trying something new. The book's framework implies that you believe you have permanently stopped developing in that area, and because you will always get older, you will not expand into it.

### Step 6: Understand the "I'm Circle" — The Vicious Cycle of Self-Labeling

Understand the cycle: A thought or belief about yourself (point 1) → produces an emotional response (point 2) → leads to the behavior you expect of yourself (point 3) → which reinforces the original thought (point 4), completing the circle. Recognize that your "I'm" is what you call upon to justify yourself and explain why you persist in behavior that does not serve the specific outcomes you have written down for yourself — instead of intervening between points 3 and 4. Challenge any aspect of your life in which you have chosen to be a "finished product." Break the circle by choosing differently at point 3 — act in a way that contradicts the label, rather than confirming it. Reframe your sentence: Change "I'm shy" to "Up until now, I've behaved in a shy manner."

### Step 7: Do Not Give Others Responsibility for Your Present-Day "I'm"

 Recognize that blaming your parents, teachers, or other authority figures for your present-day "I'm" assigns them influence over your life today. Understand that blaming others grants them authority over you and creates a justification for staying in your ineffective condition. Recognize that this removes the need to take risks — if it is due to cultural influences, you cannot do anything about it. State clearly to yourself: "I am the only person who can change this label." According to the book's framework, every "I'm" you have chosen to keep, you can restate as, "I've chosen to be."

### Step 8: Eliminate the Word "I'm" and Substitute With Past-Tense Language

Eliminate the word "I'm" in every self-description you write or speak that refers to your self-defeating labels. Substitute "I'm" sentences with:

- "Before today, I chose to act in that way."
- "I used to label myself…"

Understand that the book describes the idea of fixed human nature as a phrase used to categorize people and create excuses. You create your current behavior through the choices you make each day.

### Step 9: Announce Your Commitment to Eliminating "I'm" Labels to Those Close to You

Decide which "I'm" labels you want to eliminate. Announce to family members, close friends, and coworkers you see regularly that you are going to work at eliminating these specific "I'm" labels. Ask them to remind you whenever you use an old "I'm" label. Let their reminders serve as an accountability mechanism.

### Step 10: Set Behavioral Goals to Act Differently Than You've Ever Done Before

For each "I'm" you want to eliminate, set a behavioral goal that forces you to perform the exact opposite behavior of what the label predicts. Make the goal specific to the situation you are in. Carry it out regardless of how you feel. For example: If you consider yourself shy, introduce yourself to one new colleague you might otherwise have avoided at the team lunch.

### Step 11: Enlist a Trusted Confidant to Signal You When You Fall Back Into Your "I'm"

Identify a trusted confidant who will help you work against patterns from your past. Ask this person to signal you silently with an agreed-upon signal (e.g., a tug to his ear) when the confidant notices you falling back into your "I'm." Use the signal as a reminder to correct yourself.

### Step 12: Keep a Journal on Your Self-Destructive "I'm" Behavior

Keep a journal dedicated to tracking your self-destructive "I'm" behavior. For seven consecutive days beginning on the day you start this exercise, record in a notebook the time, date, and occasion when you used any of the self-destructive "I'm" labels. Also record what action you took and how you were feeling about yourself when you used the label. Use the list of ten typical "I'm" categories provided in this chapter as a guide for your journal-keeping. Work at reducing the number of entries.

### Step 13: Correct the Four Neurotic Sentences Out Loud When You Catch Yourself Using Them

Watch out for the four neurotic sentences in your own speech. Each time you hear yourself speak one of these sentences, correct yourself out loud in the following ways:

- Change **"That's me."** → **"That was me."**
- Change **"I can't help it."** → **"I can change that if I work on it."**
- Change **"I've always been that way."** → **"I am choosing to act differently from now on."**
- Change **"That's my nature."** → **"Before today, I believed that was my fixed nature."**

Say the corrected version aloud each time you catch yourself.

### Step 14: Work on Eliminating One "I'm" Per Day

Select one "I'm" label to eliminate for just that single day. Devote the day to working at being aware of this tendency. Actively alter a behavior associated with that "I'm" on that day. Repeat with a different "I'm" the following day, working on them one at a time until each is eliminated.

### Step 15: Interrupt Your Own "I'm Circle" Between Point 3 and Point 4

Visualize your own "I'm circle" — the cycle where a thought leads to a feeling, which leads to behavior, which reinforces the original thought. Learn to catch yourself between the behavior impulse (point 3) and the reinforcing completion (point 4), and most specifically at the exact moment before you act on the impulse, before the behavior reinforces the original thought (point 4). When you notice the impulse to act on the label, choose an action that contradicts the label instead. Resolve to not let the "I'm" complete the cycle.

### Step 16: Try Something New That You Have Avoided

Identify an activity you have not done before and that you have avoided in the past due to an "I'm" label. Set aside exactly four consecutive hours for uninterrupted participation in that new activity. Engage in the activity for the full four hours. After the activity, ask yourself: Can you still use the same "I'm" label that you applied before starting? Use the experience as evidence to challenge and potentially discard that label.

### Step 17: Reframe Your Self-Description Using Present-Moment, Choice-Based Language

Go back to the initial questions: "Who are you?" and "How do you describe yourself?" Think about new descriptions that reflect choices you could make now, rather than descriptions based on what others have said about you or what you have done before. Reframe old "I'm" labels using present-moment language:

 - Instead of "I'm shy," say "Before this week began, I chose to act in a withdrawn manner."

Recognize that the book states "You are what you choose today, not what you've chosen before." Describe yourself based on the choices you make, not by your past.

### Step 18: Adopt the "I'm an 'I'm' Exorcist" Identity

If you must keep an "I'm" label, choose this one: "I'm an 'I'm' exorcist — and I like it." Make identifying and eliminating erroneous "I'm" labels a conscious part of your identity. Treat every limiting "I'm" as a belief to be examined and changed. Embrace the identity of someone who actively identifies and changes their own self-imposed limitations.

### Step 19: Understand That "I'm" Labels Are Learned Avoidance Patterns — and Can Be Unlearned

The book states that "I'm" labels are learned avoidance patterns, not fixed traits. Remind yourself: "You can learn any new skill or behavior you choose to learn." Let go of the idea that your current limitations are permanent or inherent. Make the active choice to learn something new, since "learning is the only thing which the mind can never exhaust, never alienate, never be tortured by, never fear or distrust, and never dream of regretting." Use learning itself as a way to move forward from periods of sadness and stagnation.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You accept "I'm" labels as descriptions that you cannot modify about yourself—"I'm bad at math," "I'm shy," "I'm not athletic"—and don't question their origins.
  **Solution:** Categorize each label by its origin. Labels from others are beliefs you accepted before age eighteen without examining where they came from. Self-created labels are adaptive but inaccurate excuses. In both cases, you are saying "I am a finished product." You are not. Ask: "Who decided this?" and realize it was a choice, not an established truth.

- **Problem:** You use the four neurotic sentences ("That's me," "I've always been that way," "I can't help it," "That's my nature") to justify staying the same, and believe you cannot change.
   **Solution:** Correct each sentence out loud the precise moment you notice the thought: "That's me" → "That was me"; "I've always been that way" → "I am choosing to act differently from now on"; "I can't help it" → "I can change that if I work on it"; "That's my nature" → "Before today, I believed that was my fixed nature." Each correction is a statement that you are choosing differently today.

- **Problem:** You blame specific individuals (parents, teachers, culture) for your present-day "I'm" labels, which gives them influence over your life and provides a justification for not changing.
   **Solution:** State clearly to yourself: "I alone can change this label." Every "I'm" you have chosen to keep you can restate as, "I've chosen to be." Blaming specific individuals grants them authority over you and creates a reason to avoid risk-taking. Failing to acknowledge a pattern makes it harder to change.

- **Problem:** You are stuck in a self-defeating pattern and do not know how to break the cycle—the thought produces the feeling, which produces the behavior, which reinforces the thought.
  **Solution:** Interrupt the "I'm circle" between point 3 (behavior impulse) and point 4 (reinforcing completion). At that moment, choose to act in a way that contradicts the label. Set a specific behavioral goal that forces you to act differently. One new action is enough to create evidence that contradicts the label.
