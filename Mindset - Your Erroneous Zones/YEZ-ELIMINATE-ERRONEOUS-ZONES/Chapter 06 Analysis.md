# Chapter VI: Exploring the Unknown

## Overview

This chapter confronts the fear of the unknown as the central fear-of-the-unknown pattern — a persistent anxiety-driven pattern of avoiding unfamiliar experiences, people, or ideas that keeps a person from living fully — and shows that, according to the book's framework, the preference for safety can function as a habit of avoidance that limits how fully a person can live. Through a series of lessons and exercises, the author demonstrates that, according to the book's framework, the concrete criteria for breaking free from routine are: (1) spontaneous action — the deliberate choice to try a specific new or unfamiliar activity simply because you feel like it in the moment, without pre-planning, overthinking, or requiring external reasons or justifications, evidenced by actually initiating the new behavior; (2) inner confidence — the practiced, evidence-based self-trust that you can cope with specific unfamiliar circumstances where you lack familiar reference points or established routines, evidenced by past instances in which you successfully managed unexpected situations; and (3) a willingness to abandon the need for reasons and plans, evidenced by acting without first justifying the action to yourself or others. The chapter reframes failure as a myth, eliminates the "do your best" neurosis, and provides a 23-item behavioral checklist and 18 concrete strategies for actively exploring the unknown and choosing growth over safety.

## When to Follow These Book Teachings

- When you catch yourself eating the same foods, wearing the same clothes, visiting the same places, and thinking familiar thoughts across multiple occasions over an extended period without variation or conscious reconsideration — these repeated, unexamined behaviors constitute the habitual patterns the chapter identifies
- When the need for a plan, a reason, or certainty is preventing you from trying something new or taking a risk
- When you are afraid of failure, embarrassment, or the unknown and this fear is keeping you from specific activities you have previously enjoyed or wanted to try, or from relationships with people you find interesting

## Steps

### Step 1: Understand Your Conditioned Reflex to Avoid New Experiences

Recognize that, according to the book's framework, early training in society often encourages caution while discouraging curiosity, and encourages safety while discouraging adventure. Acknowledge that these early messages ("Avoid the questionable, stay in areas that you know, never wander into the unknown") have become a habit of avoiding new experiences. Realize that this habit limits activities that you personally value and choose to pursue, and limits your current experience of engaged, deliberate activity, in many ways. According to the book's framework, many people who feel insecure strive for security. The author observes, "You may be a safety expert—an individual who shuns the unknown in favor of always knowing where they are going, and what they can expect when they get there."

### Step 2: Abandon the Need for a Reason for Everything You Do

Accept that you can choose any action that involves personal preference rather than external obligation simply because you prefer it, and for no other reason. You don't need a justification for any action that involves personal preference rather than external obligation. Stop requiring a justification for every action that involves personal preference rather than external obligation, as this need for reasons keeps you from engaging with unfamiliar experiences and perspectives. When someone asks you why, remember that you don't have to provide a justification that will meet their standards of validity — you can act just because you prefer to. As the chapter notes: "Before adolescence you could play with a grasshopper for an extended period, for no reason but that you liked it...But from adolescence onward, you have to come up with a reason that meets externally imposed standards of justification." Emerson observed in his personal Journal of April 11, 1834: "Four snakes gliding up and down a hollow for no purpose that I could see. Not to eat. Not for love…But only gliding."

### Step 3: Eliminate the "If It's Unusual I Must Stay Away From It" Mentality

When you see deaf people using sign language, watch with curiosity but attempt to converse with them. When you encounter people who speak a language other than your own — that is, people whose native language or current language of communication differs from yours — find a way to communicate instead of wandering off. Do not avoid the uncertainty of communicating in a language other than your own. Stop avoiding activities and people merely because they are unknown or treated with discomfort or avoidance by mainstream social groups. The author places people who are attracted to the same sex, people who dress in clothing traditionally associated with a different gender, people with physical or mental disabilities, people with intellectual disabilities, and people who practice social nudity in the category of people whose behavior or appearance falls outside what a majority of people in mainstream social contexts encounter in daily life — noting that people are unsure how to interact and therefore avoid them entirely.

### Step 4: Practice Spontaneity

Examine your spontaneous action — can you try something unfamiliar or do you cling tightly to familiar habits? Define spontaneous action as the ability to try something on impulse just because it is something you would enjoy, without pre-planning, overthinking, or requiring external justification. Be willing to be judged as acting without regard for physical safety or causing harm to others — what does the judgment of others matter when you're having an enjoyable experience discovering the unknown? Ask yourself: "Have you really lived thousands of days or have you lived one day thousands of times?" Can you spontaneously take paths that don't always lead to a predictable or guaranteed outcome? A teacher of the author — a graduate-level instructor with thirty or more years of classroom experience — asks teachers with thirty or more years in the classroom: "Have you really been teaching for thirty years or have you been teaching one year, thirty times?"

### Step 5: Eliminate Rigidity and Prejudice

Recognize that rigidity — an unwillingness to adjust plans or perspectives despite new information — contributes to prejudice, which means forming a negative evaluation of a person, group, or idea without direct personal experience. According to the book's framework, understand that prejudice is based less on hate or even dislike and more on the fact that it feels easier and safer to stay with the familiar. Eliminate your prejudgments and allow yourself to engage with new people and ideas. Realize that if you don't trust any unfamiliar person, you cannot form an accurate evaluation of them — one possible interpretation is that this distrust of others reflects an underlying self-doubt about your own ability to handle unfamiliar situations, rather than a genuine judgment about the other person. "Being spontaneous means eliminating your prejudgments and allowing yourself to engage with new people and ideas."

### Step 6: Avoid the "Always Having a Plan" Trap

According to the book's framework, realize that the phrase "planned spontaneity" involves a logical contradiction: by definition, spontaneous action cannot be pre-determined or scheduled, so planning to be spontaneous is not spontaneous. Don't become rigidly attached to your plan — this rigid attachment, not the plan itself, is the real problem. Make a new decision each morning and each evening rather than consulting an agenda to see where you ought to be according to a predetermined schedule. Have confidence in yourself — based on past evidence of your ability to adapt to changing circumstances — to be able to alter the plan. Don't let the plan override your own reasoned judgment. Henry, a client in his mid-twenties, suffered from planning obsession — the compulsive need to pre-arrange every aspect of daily life. He always ate the same breakfast, planned his clothing one to two days in advance, had dresser drawers arranged by color and size, and imposed rigid rules on his family. After counseling, he began to try spontaneous living, saw his plans as ways of controlling or pressuring others and convenient excuses for avoiding the uncertainty of new experiences. After several months, he actually applied for a job in a firm that would require him to accept a position requiring him to relocate every one to two years for work.

### Step 7: Test and Develop Internal Security

Perform this exercise as a hypothetical mental scenario: Imagine that without prior notice you are transported to a remote, unfamiliar location — a place you have never previously visited, with no money, possessions, or familiar social connections. Ask yourself: "Could you survive this hypothetical situation? Could you make friends, obtain food and shelter, or would you simply feel helpless about how unfortunate you were to have this unexpected disruption to your normal circumstances?" According to the book's framework, depending exclusively on external structures and guarantees can create vulnerability — under some conditions, such reliance leaves you unable to cope if those structures are removed. But if you have inner confidence and are not afraid of the unknown, then you would survive. Redefine security as practical knowledge — tested, demonstrated ability based on past experience — that you can cope with difficult situations, including having no external support. Don't be trapped by dependence on external support, since it can rob you of your ability to live and grow fully. Take a look at people without external support, people who don't have it all mapped out — people who have learned to rely on their own resourcefulness are perhaps better off than those who depend entirely on external structures and guarantees. James Kavanaugh's poem "Some Day" captures this spirit: "Someday I'll walk away / And be free / And leave the sterile ones / Their secure sterility. I'll leave without a forwarding address / And walk across some barren wilderness / To drop the world there. Then wander free of care / Like an unemployed Atlas."

### Step 8: Redefine Failure

According to the book's framework, understand that failure as an enduring identity does not exist — it is simply someone else's subjective opinion of how a certain specific act should have been completed. Once you accept that no single correct method exists for the majority of routine tasks, then failing becomes extremely unlikely within this framework. Not to succeed in a particular endeavor is not to fail as a person — it is simply not being successful with that particular trial at the current five-minute period. Apply the principle of natural behavior to yourself: The principle of natural behavior is the observation that instinctive actions — behaviors arising from innate behavioral patterns rather than conscious self-evaluation — are carried out without self-evaluation, judgment of success or failure, or comparison to external standards. Spiders construct webs as a matter of innate behavioral patterns, building the web they are capable of building without declaring it a "successful web" or an "unsuccessful web." Cats hunt mice; if they aren't successful in one attempt, they simply go after another. They don't lie there and whine or experience anxiety of clinical severity because they failed. Natural behavior simply is — it operates without self-evaluation, just as spiders build webs without judging them as successful or unsuccessful. Rid yourself of the fear of failure by applying this same principle of natural behavior to your own behavior. "Consider a dog barking for fifteen minutes, and someone saying, 'He really isn't very good at barking, I'd give him a C.' How absurd! It is impossible for an animal to fail because there is no provision for evaluating natural behavior — natural behavior operates outside the system of human evaluation and grading."

### Step 9: Eliminate the "Do Your Best" Neurosis

Change the maxim from "Do your best" to simply "Do." Don't paralyze yourself with the drive to perform at an externally defined perfect level — the demand to always produce top-quality results. For the majority of routine, low-stakes tasks, having to produce top-quality results, or even to perform at a level that meets or exceeds an external standard, is an obstacle to acting. Don't let the drive to perform perfectly keep you on the sidelines — avoiding participation in activities — avoiding activities you might enjoy. Where the reader is a parent, don't create anxiety and resentment by insisting they always perform at their best. Instead, talk with them about the activities they enjoy most, and give encouragement to try hard in those areas. In other activities, emphasize that acting is generally more valuable than producing a successful outcome, depending on the context and the specific activity. Teach them to play in the volleyball game rather than sit on the sidelines — avoiding participation — saying "I'm no good." Teach the lessons of enjoyment and pride in the activity itself — in the activities the individual values — not competitiveness or trying to produce top-quality results. Louann, an eighteen-year-old high school senior — a student who had received grades at the A level in every course — was focused on achievement and had developed a repetitive involuntary physical movement — a repetitive facial tic — when talking about socializing. She began to place importance on her feelings and set exacting criteria — requiring outcomes to meet extremely high, often unattainable standards — for developing new social behaviors. She received her first grade below A — a grade of C — in a college freshman English class, which Louann's mother was concerned about. The author recommended Louann celebrate it openly and take Louann out for dinner in celebration.

### Step 10: Don't Equate Your Self-Worth with Achievement

Refuse to use achievement rewards as substitutes for trying something new and unknown. Don't reside in safe areas of inquiry because "I know I can get an A." Risk a lower grade by embarking on a new discipline rather than staying safe. Don't take the safe job where you know you'll be successful instead of trying something new and risking failure. Think of Thomas Edison — if he'd used failure as an indicator of his own self-esteem, he would have given up on himself after his first trial at inventing a commercially viable light bulb. See failure as instructive, as an incentive to work and exploration. "Nothing fails like success because you do not learn anything from it. The only thing we ever learn from is failure. Success often reinforces unexamined beliefs."

### Step 11: Use the "Fear of the Unknown" Behavior Checklist

Assess your own behavior using this 23-item behavioral checklist and challenge yourself on why you want to live each day identically to the one before — examining whether sameness — maintaining identical routines without variation — is a choice or an unexamined habit:

1. Eating the same kinds of foods for three or more years — avoiding new or unfamiliar taste experiences entirely
2. Wearing the same kinds of clothes for an extended period — never trying a new style
3. Reading the same newspapers and magazines which support the same political or philosophical viewpoint every time without exception, and never admitting a contrary viewpoint
4. Seeing the same types of movies — films with similar philosophical or political themes despite having different titles — for three or more years — refusing to view anything that might support a different philosophical or political belief
5. Living in the same neighborhood, city or state for an extended period, simply because your parents happened to have chosen that location — never considering whether another location might better suit your current needs and preferences
6. Refusing to listen to ideas that differ from your own — immediately, without consideration, insisting the speaker is crazy or uninformed
7. Being afraid to try a new activity because you can't perform it at a level that meets or exceeds an external standard — saying "I don't think I'd be very good, I'll just watch"
8. Compulsive focus on achievement in educational or work settings — grades or performance metrics treated as more important than any other consideration, using achievement rewards as substitutes for trying something new
9. Avoiding anyone whose behavior or appearance you evaluate negatively without direct personal experience — using negative labels as protection from fear of the unfamiliar
10. Staying with the same job even though you dislike it, out of fear of the uncertainty of new work
11. Staying in a marriage marked by persistent dissatisfaction or conflict that shows no sign of improvement, out of fear of the unknown single life
12. Taking vacations in the same place, at the same hotel, during the same season annually
13. Using external performance metrics rather than enjoyment of the activity itself as the sole criteria for all activities
14. Measuring things by the amount of money spent — if it costs more, it is worth more
15. Striving for socially recognized markers of success such as important titles, high-value vehicles, recognizable brand names or logos on clothing and other status symbols
16. An inability to alter a plan when an option that differs meaningfully from current routines arises
17. Being excessively focused on time to the point of allowing clocks and schedules to dictate daily activities — sleeping, eating, and intimate activities strictly at predetermined times regardless of hunger, exhaustion, or desire
18. Dismissing activities such as meditation, yoga, astrology, backgammon, bocce, Mahjongg — a tile-based game of Chinese origin — or isometrics which you have never previously engaged in
19. Viewing intimate activity without willingness to improvise or deviate from routine — always doing the same thing in the same position
20. Hiding behind your small, established circle of friends and never forming new social connections outside existing circles with different people
21. At a social event, staying with your spouse or date for the full duration of the event not because you want to but because you feel protected from social judgment or discomfort doing so
22. Holding back from entering a conversation with people whose behavior or topics differ from what you are used to, out of fear of what might happen
23. Evaluating yourself negatively if the outcome of your activities or projects does not meet an externally defined standard of success

### Step 12: Recognize the Psychological Support System That Keeps You From Exploring

Understand that by maintaining identical daily routines you never have to respond spontaneously without a prepared plan — if you have a predetermined routine, you simply refer to that routine rather than drawing on your capacity to think spontaneously in the moment. Recognize that staying with the familiar has its own explicit function — the fear of the unfamiliar is strong, and as long as you stay with the familiar you are keeping that fear suppressed or avoided. Don't justify staying with the familiar by saying you are deliberately delaying immediate pleasure for a future benefit — according to the book's framework, this framing suggests that responsible, conventionally responsible conduct requires persisting with an already-chosen course of action and avoiding novelty, when it is actually rooted in anxiety and doubt about stepping into the unknown. Don't feel significant by having done it correctly and linking your sense of personal value to successful performance — the conventionally correct action in a given situation is only someone else's editorial opinion. "Think of Columbus. Everyone in his social circle warned him he would fall over the edge. It is easier to be one of many people continuing with an established routine or familiar pattern of behavior rather than an explorer risking everything."

### Step 13: Implement Specific Strategies to Come to Grips With the Unknown

Choose specific, manageable new activities to try — that is, intentional, targeted efforts — to try new foods, new routes, or new social interactions even if you feel drawn toward the familiar routine — at a restaurant, order a new menu item or new type of cuisine. Why? Because it would be meaningfully distinct from the usual and you might enjoy it. Invite a small, deliberately selected group of approximately three to five individuals — a group chosen specifically because their views differ significantly from your own and from those held by the people you usually socialize with — to your home, choosing to form new social connections outside existing circles rather than spending time with your small, established circle of friends and acquaintances. Give up having to have a reason for everything you do. Begin to take some risks that will get you out of your routine: take an unplanned vacation with no reservations or maps, where you just trust yourself to handle whatever might come along; interview for a new job; talk with someone that you've been avoiding because you were afraid of not knowing what might happen; take a new route to work; have dinner at midnight. Entertain yourself with a fantasy in which you allow yourself to have any food, activity, possession, or experience you desire — with no restrictions on what can be requested, unlimited money for fourteen consecutive days. You'll find that most of the random desires and daydreams that pass through your mind are indeed attainable for you. Take a risk that might involve some disruption to your familiar routines, relationships, and environments that you already know and find predictable — but which would be deeply satisfying and fulfilling to you. Whenever you find yourself avoiding the unknown, ask yourself: "What's the worst thing that could happen to me?" In most cases you will find that the fears of the unknown — the anxiety about what unfamiliar situations might bring — are disproportionately large compared to the actual consequences. Try to do something lighthearted and undignified — like going barefoot in the park, or skinny-dipping — explore unfamiliar experiences you have previously avoided because they seemed silly or undignified. Remind yourself that the fear of failure is very often — in a large proportion of cases — the fear of someone else's disapproval or ridicule — the judgment or mockery of other people — let them have their own opinions, which have nothing to do with you. Make an attempt to do some things that you've always avoided with the sentence "I'm just not good at that" — spend an afternoon painting a picture and have a hell of a time. If the end product is less than masterful — not of professional or expert quality — you haven't failed, you've had a half day of pleasure.

### Step 14: Choose Growth Over Security

Remember that, according to the book's framework, the author's perspective is that security — in the sense of absolute certainty about future outcomes — is an illusion — as long as you are a person on earth, according to this framework, you can never have absolute security. Recognize that security, as the author defines it, means knowing exactly what is going to happen, which excludes excitement, risks, challenge, and growth. According to the book's framework, choose the deliberate pursuit of uncertainty, exploration, and growth — the path of uncertainty, exploration, and growth that lies entirely outside your familiar routines, relationships, and environments that you already know and find predictable — over the author's concept of security, as illustrated by Robert Frost's poem "The Road Not Taken" (the deliberate choice of growth and exploration over maintaining familiar but unfulfilling patterns). Understand that the fear-of-the-unknown pattern — your fear of the unknown that keeps you stuck in familiar but unfulfilling patterns — is waiting to be replaced by new activities that challenge you to grow, explore, and step outside your familiar routines — activities that will bring pleasure to your life. Realize that you don't have to know exactly where you're going — as long as you are actively engaged in the process of growth and exploration. "Think of a road with a fork in it. In one direction lies security, in the other the great uncharted unknown. Which road would you take?" Robert Frost answered in The Road Not Taken: "Two roads diverged in a wood, and I— / I took the one less traveled by, / And that has made all the difference."

### Example 2: The Author's Colleague Who Lived in Europe

A teacher of the author who taught graduate courses stayed with the same university, consulting work, and comfortable lifestyle for several years. In 1974 this teacher decided to live in Europe for six months because it was an experience they had wanted to have for some time. They taught two courses for an overseas graduate program and observed firsthand that they could manage the uncertainty. After three weeks in Germany, because of their inner confidence, they had a comparable number of opportunities to conduct workshops, work with clients, and give lectures as they had had in New York. Even in a remote village in Turkey, where they lived for two months, they were busier than they had been in New York.

### Example 3: Louann and the "C" in English

### Example 4: The Reader With the Hidden-Camera Editorial

"A study in which a participant whose stated political views were publicly known was asked to read an editorial that began with a position identical to their own. Partway through the editorial, the viewpoint shifted, and a recording device revealed that the participant's gaze similarly shifted to another part of the page." This illustrates how people refuse to engage with ideas that differ from their own — a hallmark of the fear of the unknown.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Accept that you can choose any action that involves personal preference rather than external obligation simply because you prefer it — no external justification required
- ✅ Practice spontaneous action regularly: try a new food, take a new route, have dinner at midnight
- ✅ Redefine failure as simply an outcome that differs from your hoped-for result — not a judgment on your person
- ✅ Replace "Do your best" with simply "Do" — acting matters more than producing top-quality results
- ✅ Separate your sense of personal value from achievement — you have inherent worth regardless of outcomes
- ✅ Develop inner confidence — the practiced ability to draw on internal resourcefulness when familiar structures are absent — by imagining yourself handling situations in which all familiar supports and references are absent and you must rely entirely on your own resourcefulness
- ✅ Engage with people, ideas, and activities that feel unfamiliar rather than avoiding them
- ✅ Make a formal declaration of separation from the people and beliefs that constrain your growth and keep you afraid — severing ties with influences that limit your willingness to explore
- ❌ Don't let your attachment to a plan override your reasoned judgment — be willing to alter it when a better opportunity arises
- ❌ Don't measure your life by performance metrics, socially recognized markers of success such as titles or possessions, or how it compares to others
- ❌ Don't stay in a job, marriage, or neighborhood solely out of anxiety about what unfamiliar circumstances might bring
- ❌ Don't avoid trying something new because you fear you won't be good at it
- ❌ Don't confuse deliberately delaying immediate pleasure for a future benefit with hiding from the unknown out of anxiety
- ❌ Don't let the drive to perform perfectly keep you from participating in activities you might enjoy

## Security & Safety Notes

This chapter addresses emotional and psychological patterns rather than digital or financial security measures. The inner confidence exercise — imagining that you are transported without prior notice to a remote, unfamiliar location where you have no money, possessions, or familiar social connections — is a hypothetical mental exercise designed to build confidence in your own resourcefulness — it is not an instruction to take unplanned risks in physically unsafe situations. Apply these principles thoughtfully and at a pace that matches your current life circumstances, responsibilities, and your willingness to engage with unfamiliar experiences — engaging only when you have adequate time, energy, and support to do so safely.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You confuse spontaneous action with acting without regard for physical safety or harm to others, and resist change entirely, maintaining identical routines without variation.
  **Solution:** Start small — try a new food, take a new route, have a conversation with someone outside your usual circle. According to the book's framework, the ability to act spontaneously is like a skill that strengthens with repeated use; use the 23-item behavioral checklist to identify areas where you have become rigid and choose one item to challenge per week, working systematically through the list.

- **Problem:** You use "doing your best" and the drive to perform perfectly to avoid trying any new activity, which keeps you from participating in activities you might enjoy.
  **Solution:** Replace "Do your best" with simply "Do." Focus on the experience of participating rather than the outcome. The author's recommendation to Louann — celebrating a "C" with dinner — captures the spirit: doing something imperfectly is better than not doing it at all.

- **Problem:** You link your sense of personal value to achievement and stay in situations where past success guarantees future success — rather than attempting something new where past performance doesn't predict future results.
  **Solution:** Recognize that failure is not a judgment on your person — it is simply a result that differs from what you hoped for. Think of Thomas Edison, who saw every failed experiment as instructive rather than as evidence of inadequacy. Accept a lower grade or an uncertain outcome in exchange for genuine growth.

- **Problem:** You justify staying with the familiar by calling it "mature" or "deliberately delaying immediate pleasure for a future benefit" when it is actually anxiety about the unknown in disguise.
  **Solution:** Ask yourself honestly: "What's the worst thing that could happen to me?" and "What am I avoiding?" According to the book's framework, in the majority of documented cases the anticipated negative outcomes — the anxiety and self-consciousness of performing below one's usual standards — are disproportionate to the actual reality of what could happen, and what you are avoiding is the discomfort of being bad at a new activity.
