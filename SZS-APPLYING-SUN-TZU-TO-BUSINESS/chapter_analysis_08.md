# Chapter Seven: Practice Good Intelligence
## Source: "Sun Tzu for Success" — Pages 20–22

---

## Chapter Overview

Chapter Seven teaches that superior intelligence, flexibility, and disciplined maneuvering are the foundations of victory. Even when outnumbered, a force that knows the enemy's plans and terrain can compel the enemy to fight on unfavorable terms. The chapter is organized into three sections: **Practice Good Intelligence**, **Be Flexible**, and **Maneuvering**.

---

## Actionable Lesson 1: Know the Time and Place of Battle

### Core Principle
Victory depends on knowing the exact time and location of the coming engagement. Without this knowledge, even a large force cannot coordinate effectively.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Gather intelligence on when and where the critical confrontation will occur.
2. Align your deployment schedule so that troops can converge on the right field at the right time.
3. Ensure your forces are positioned such that the left wing can support the right wing, the front can reinforce the rear, and distant units can support near units.
4. If the deployment area is tens of li wide, pre-plan intermediate rally points and communication lines.

### Examples from the Text
> "Therefore, if one knows the place and time of the coming battle, his troops can march a thousand li and fight on the field. But if one knows neither the spot nor the time, then one cannot manage to have the left wing help the right wing or the right wing help the left."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do maintain a central intelligence hub that tracks both timing and location.
- Do rehearse mobilization plans so that long-distance deployment is feasible.
- Do build redundant communication channels between dispersed units.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't commit to engagement before confirming the battlefield coordinates.
- Don't disperse forces across a wide area without ensuring mutual support is possible.
- Don't assume the enemy will fight on ground of your choosing without intelligence.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Time and location intelligence must be protected; if compromised, the enemy can ambush your dispersed forces.
- A force that does not know where or when it will fight is vulnerable to being picked apart piece by piece.

## Actionable Lesson 2: Analyze the Enemy's Battle Plan

### Core Principle
Victory can be achieved even against a numerically superior enemy by understanding the enemy's strategy and exploiting its structural weaknesses.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Acquire the enemy's battle plan through reconnaissance, espionage, or interception of communications.
2. Map the plan's strong points (e.g., fortified positions, reserve forces, supply lines).
3. Map the plan's weak points (e.g., gaps in coverage, overextended flanks, reliance on single routes).
4. Design your strategy to neutralize the enemy's strong points and exploit the weak points.
5. Avoid direct confrontation with the enemy's strengths; instead, force the enemy to fight on terms you have chosen.

### Examples from the Text
> "Although I estimate the troops of Yue as many, of what benefit is this superiority in terms of victory?"
> "Thus, I say that victory can be achieved. For even if the enemy is numerically stronger, we can prevent him from fighting."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do invest in multiple intelligence sources (human intelligence, signals intelligence, open-source observation).
- Do diagram the enemy's battle plan visually to identify seams and dependencies.
- Do validate intelligence through multiple independent confirmations before acting on it.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't assume numerical superiority guarantees victory.
- Don't ignore the enemy's plan because it appears weak on paper; verify through active probing.
- Don't rely on a single intelligence source.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Intelligence collection must be conducted discreetly. Revealing that you have penetrated the enemy's plan can cause the enemy to change course.
- Counterintelligence measures should be in place to detect enemy attempts to feed you false battle plans.

## Actionable Lesson 3: Agitate the Enemy to Ascertain His Pattern of Movement

### Core Principle
By deliberately provoking the enemy, you can observe how he reacts, moves, and allocates resources — revealing his doctrine and habits.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Identify a low-risk provocation that the enemy is likely to respond to (e.g., a feint, a probe at a secondary objective, a simulated withdrawal).
2. Execute the provocation while maintaining observation coverage over the enemy's forces.
3. Record how the enemy moves: Does he rush to reinforce? Does he overextend? Does he abandon a position?
4. Identify patterns: Does the enemy always respond to flank threats? Does he prefer to consolidate rather than pursue?
5. Use the observed patterns to predict his behavior in the main engagement.

### Examples from the Text
> "Agitate the enemy so as to ascertain his pattern of movement."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do use controlled, reversible actions that do not commit your main force.
- Do have observers in place (visual, electronic, or human) to capture the enemy's reaction.
- Do vary your provocations to test whether the enemy has multiple response patterns or a single habitual response.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't use a provocation that is so strong it triggers an all-out counterattack before you are ready.
- Don't assume the enemy's reaction is his only pattern; commanders adapt.
- Don't expose your own disposition while agitating the enemy.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Provocations can escalate. Maintain a clear escalation ladder and abort criteria.
- The enemy may use your provocation as bait to draw you into an ambush; maintain security and reserve forces.

## Actionable Lesson 4: Lure the Enemy Into the Open to Find Vulnerable Spots

### Core Principle
An enemy in a fortified or concealed position is difficult to assess and defeat. By offering bait, you can draw him into terrain where his disposition is exposed.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Identify what the enemy values or fears losing (e.g., a supply convoy, a key position, a vulnerable ally).
2. Present that asset as exposed or weakly held.
3. When the enemy moves to seize or defend the bait, observe his formation, spacing, and command structure.
4. Identify gaps, overextended flanks, or congested routes.
5. Strike at the vulnerable spots while the enemy is committed to the bait objective.

### Examples from the Text
> "Lure him in the open so as to find out his vulnerable spots in disposition."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do choose bait that the enemy cannot ignore given his objectives and doctrine.
- Do have your main force concealed and ready to strike once the enemy is committed.
- Do use terrain to channel the enemy into the area where you want to observe and attack.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't use bait that is too valuable; losing it may damage your overall position.
- Don't reveal your main force before the enemy is fully committed.
- Don't assume the enemy will take the bait; he may see through the deception.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Bait operations are inherently risky. The enemy may turn the tables and use your bait as an ambush trigger.
- Maintain security around your main striking force; if the enemy discovers it, the operation fails and you are vulnerable.

## Actionable Lesson 5: Probe the Enemy to Identify Strength and Deficiency

### Core Principle
Active probing with small, expendable forces reveals where the enemy is strong and where he is weak, allowing you to allocate your main effort accordingly.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Deploy small, independent probe units against different sectors of the enemy line.
2. Vary the intensity and direction of probes to test enemy reactions across the entire front.
3. Record which sectors the enemy defends vigorously and which sectors he leaves thinly held or ignores.
4. Correlate probe results with other intelligence (terrain analysis, logistics, known unit capabilities).
5. Plan your main attack to concentrate against the deficient sectors while fixing or avoiding the strong sectors.

### Examples from the Text
> "Probe him and learn where his strength is abundant and where deficient."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do use probes that are light enough to be sacrificed without crippling your force.
- Do run probes simultaneously across multiple axes to prevent the enemy from shifting reserves.
- Do debrief probe leaders immediately to capture on-the-ground observations.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't send probes that are too strong; if they succeed, you may misinterpret the enemy's disposition.
- Don't allow probes to become engaged in decisive battle; their purpose is information, not territory.
- Don't probe the same sector repeatedly; the enemy will reinforce it and you will lose the opportunity to learn about other sectors.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Probe forces may be cut off. Ensure they have extraction routes or can link up with other forces.
- Enemy counterintelligence may use your probes to map your intentions and dispositions; vary probe directions and timing.

## Actionable Lesson 6: Conceal Your Own Troops Without Ascertainable Shape

### Core Principle
The ultimate form of force protection is operational security. If the enemy cannot determine your disposition, he cannot plan effectively against you.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Disperse and conceal your main force using terrain, darkness, camouflage, and deception.
2. Avoid creating recognizable patterns in your movements, logistics, or communications.
3. Use dummy positions, false radio traffic, and misleading supply routes to confuse enemy intelligence.
4. Restrict access to your battle plan to the minimum number of personnel necessary.
5. Conduct regular security audits to detect leaks or surveillance.

### Examples from the Text
> "Now, the ultimate in disposing one's troops is to conceal them without ascertainable shape. In this way, the most penetrating spies cannot pry nor can the wise lay plans against you."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do compartmentalize intelligence; commanders should know only what is necessary for their role.
- Do use terrain and weather to mask movement (night movement, fog, smoke, forest cover).
- Do vary routines and procedures so that patterns do not emerge.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't concentrate large forces in the open without concealment.
- Don't discuss plans in unsecured communications or locations.
- Don't allow your logistics tail to reveal your main force's location.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Even the most penetrating spies cannot pry if your force has no ascertainable shape. This is the highest standard of operational security.
- If the enemy can lay plans against you, your strategic options are severely limited. Concealment is not optional — it is foundational.

## Actionable Lesson 7: Modify Tactics According to the Enemy Situation

### Core Principle
There are no fixed postures or constant tactics in conflict. Victory goes to the commander who adapts his tactics to the specific enemy, terrain, and circumstances.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Assess the enemy's current disposition, morale, and capabilities.
2. Assess the terrain, weather, and time constraints.
3. Select tactics that exploit the specific vulnerabilities revealed by this assessment.
4. Execute the chosen tactics with full commitment.
5. Continuously reassess the situation and be prepared to change tactics as conditions evolve.

### Examples from the Text
> "Hence, there are neither fixed postures nor constant tactics in warfare. He who can modify his tactics in accordance with the enemy situation and thereby succeeds in winning may be said to be divine."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do maintain a repertoire of tactics so that you can select the right tool for each situation.
- Do empower subordinate commanders to adapt tactics within the overall intent.
- Do conduct after-action reviews to understand what tactical adaptations worked and why.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't repeat a tactic that worked in a previous situation without verifying that conditions are similar.
- Don't rigidly adhere to a battle plan when the situation has changed.
- Don't confuse flexibility with indecision; adapt deliberately, not randomly.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Ad-hoc tactical changes must be communicated securely to prevent enemy interception.
- Rapid tactical changes can create confusion among your own forces; maintain a clear command intent so that subordinate commanders can act independently and coherently.

## Actionable Lesson 8: Avoid the Enemy's Strength and Strike His Weakness

### Core Principle
Like water flowing to the lowlands, successful operations avoid the enemy's strong points and concentrate against his vulnerabilities.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Identify the enemy's center of gravity — the source of his strength (e.g., a elite unit, a fortified position, a supply line).
2. Identify the enemy's vulnerabilities — gaps, weak units, exposed logistics, political fractures.
3. Design your campaign to bypass or fix the enemy's strength with minimal forces.
4. Concentrate your main effort against the enemy's weakness.
5. Use the terrain and timing to ensure that your strike hits the weak point before the enemy can reinforce it.

### Examples from the Text
> "Now, the laws of military operations are like water. The tendency of water is to flow from heights to lowlands. The law of successful operations is to avoid the enemy's strength and strike his weakness. Water changes its course in accordance with the contours of the land. The soldier works out his victory in accordance with the situation of the enemy."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do conduct a thorough strength/weakness analysis before committing to a course of action.
- Do use terrain to channel the enemy's strength into areas where it is irrelevant.
- Do accept that avoiding the enemy's strength may require patience and longer timelines.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't attack the enemy's strongest position out of pride or expectation.
- Don't assume the enemy's weakness will remain open; it may be closed if you delay.
- Don't confuse the enemy's apparent weakness with a trap; verify through probing.

### Security/Safety Notes
- The enemy may deliberately expose a weakness to lure you into an ambush. Always probe suspected weak points before concentrating force.
- Concentrating force against a weak point creates a temporary vulnerability on your flanks; maintain security.

## Actionable Lesson 9: Change Tactics in Infinite Variety to Suit Changing Circumstances

### Core Principle
Circumstances are always changing. Tactics must be infinitely variable to match the infinite variety of situations.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Monitor the operational environment continuously for changes in enemy disposition, terrain, weather, and logistics.
2. Maintain a flexible planning process that can produce new tactical options quickly.
3. Train forces to execute a wide range of tactics so that they can adapt without extensive retraining.
4. When conditions change, re-evaluate the entire tactical picture rather than making minor adjustments to the existing plan.
5. Document tactical adaptations for institutional learning.

### Examples from the Text
> "The tactics change in an infinite variety of ways to suit changes in the circumstances."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do build organizational flexibility through cross-training and modular force design.
- Do use red-teaming and wargaming to practice tactical adaptation.
- Do reward initiative and creative problem-solving at all levels.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't over-specialize forces to the point where they cannot execute alternative tactics.
- Don't penalize commanders for deviating from the original plan when the situation demands it.
- Don't confuse variety with chaos; tactical changes must be directed toward a clear objective.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Rapid tactical changes can be exploited by enemy intelligence if they are predictable. Vary your adaptation patterns.
- Excessive tactical variation can undermine your own force's cohesion; balance flexibility with stability.

## Actionable Lesson 10: Maneuver to Gain Advantage by Making the Devious Route Direct

### Core Principle
The art of maneuver is to turn circuitous routes into direct advantage, and to turn disadvantages into advantages — arriving at the critical point before the enemy.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Identify the objective position that offers the greatest advantage.
2. Map all routes to that position, including circuitous or indirect routes.
3. Assess whether a devious route can be traversed faster than the direct route, given enemy dispositions.
4. If so, use the devious route to bypass enemy obstacles or shorten the effective distance.
5. Simultaneously use deception or bait to delay the enemy's march, ensuring you arrive first.

### Examples from the Text
> "What is difficult about it is to make the devious route the most direct and to turn disadvantage to advantage. Thus, forcing the enemy to deviate and slow down his march by luring him with a bait, you may set out after he does and arrive at the battlefield before him."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do use terrain to mask indirect movement (rivers, valleys, forests).
- Do synchronize maneuver with intelligence operations so that you know the enemy's route and timing.
- Do have a contingency plan if the devious route is discovered or blocked.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't assume the devious route is always better; calculate time, risk, and logistical requirements.
- Don't move so indirectly that you lose the initiative or allow the enemy to seize the objective first.
- Don't neglect to protect your logistics tail during maneuver.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Indirect routes may pass through enemy-held or contested territory. Secure these passages with screening forces.
- Maneuver forces are vulnerable during movement; maintain force protection and rapid reaction capabilities.

## Actionable Lesson 11: Balance Speed, Baggage, and Force Size in Maneuver

### Core Principle
Forced marches yield diminishing returns. The faster you march, the fewer troops arrive fit for combat. A force that arrives rapidly but in small numbers may be unable to hold the advantage.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Determine the required arrival time at the objective.
2. Calculate the force size needed to hold the position upon arrival.
3. Adjust the march speed so that enough troops arrive combat-effective.
4. If speed is essential, accept that only a portion of the force will arrive and plan accordingly.
5. Protect the main body and logistics so that the remaining force can sustain operations after the initial maneuver.

### Examples from the Text
> "In a forced march of a hundred li, only one-tenth of the army will arrive. In a forced march of fifty li, but half of the army will arrive. In a forced march of thirty li, but two-thirds will arrive."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do plan for staged arrivals: light forces first to seize the position, followed by heavy forces to consolidate.
- Do maintain a secure logistics corridor so that arriving forces can be resupplied and reinforced.
- Do train troops for sustained marching with realistic loads.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't force-march an entire army over long distances expecting full combat effectiveness.
- Don't abandon baggage and stores entirely; the army cannot survive without provisions.
- Don't push the march to the point where commanders and elite troops are incapacitated.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Stragglers from forced marches are vulnerable to enemy cavalry or irregular forces. Provide security for the rear.
- An army that arrives in a degraded state may be unable to defend against a fresh enemy force.

## Actionable Lesson 12: Know Your Neighbor's Designs Before Entering Alliances

### Core Principle
Alliances with entities whose intentions and capabilities you do not understand are dangerous and potentially fatal.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Before entering any alliance, conduct intelligence operations to understand the ally's true objectives, capabilities, and internal politics.
2. Assess whether the ally's interests align with yours for the duration of the partnership.
3. Evaluate the ally's reliability under stress: Will they fulfill commitments when the situation deteriorates?
4. Establish clear terms of engagement, exit clauses, and decision-making protocols.
5. Maintain independent intelligence on the ally's actions to detect betrayal or divergence.

### Examples from the Text
> "One who is not acquainted with the designs of his neighbors should not enter into alliances with them."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do treat alliance formation as a high-stakes intelligence operation.
- Do verify the ally's claims through independent sources.
- Do build redundancy into your plans so that the alliance is not a single point of failure.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't enter alliances based on shared enemies alone; shared interests may diverge when the enemy is defeated.
- Don't assume that diplomatic assurances substitute for verified intelligence.
- Don't share critical intelligence with an untested ally without assessing their security and counterintelligence posture.

### Security/Safety Notes
- An unreliable ally can be more dangerous than an enemy. They have access to your plans and dispositions.
- Intelligence sharing in alliances must be carefully controlled; compartmentalize sensitive information.

## Actionable Lesson 13: Know the Conditions of Terrain Before Conducting a March

### Core Principle
An army that marches through unfamiliar terrain without understanding its characteristics is at severe risk of ambush, disruption, and defeat.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Before any march, gather intelligence on the terrain: mountains, forests, hazardous defiles, marshes, swamps, rivers, and urban areas.
2. Assess the terrain for choke points, ambush sites, defensible positions, and water sources.
3. Plan routes that avoid or mitigate the most dangerous terrain features.
4. Assign guides who are familiar with the specific terrain to the maneuver units.
5. Brief all commanders on the terrain intelligence so that they can adapt if the march is disrupted.

### Examples from the Text
> "Those who do not know the conditions of mountains and forests, hazardous defiles, and marshes and swamps cannot conduct the march of an army."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do use topographic maps, satellite imagery, and local guides to build a comprehensive terrain picture.
- Do conduct terrain reconnaissance with small advance parties before committing the main force.
- Do plan alternate routes in case the primary route is blocked or compromised.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't assume that a road or pass is passable without verification; weather and enemy action can change terrain conditions rapidly.
- Don't march through hazardous defiles in column without flank security.
- Don't rely on a single guide; cross-verify terrain intelligence.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Guides may be enemy agents or may be coerced. Verify their loyalty and cross-check their information.
- Terrain intelligence is often classified; protect it as you would protect any sensitive operational data.

## Actionable Lesson 14: Use Local Guides to Obtain the Advantages of the Ground

### Core Principle
Even with maps and intelligence, local knowledge is irreplaceable. Local guides provide real-time understanding of terrain, customs, and enemy activity.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Recruit or contract guides from the local population before the operation.
2. Brief guides on the objectives and routes; gather their input on conditions.
3. Cross-check guide information with your own intelligence.
4. Assign guides to maneuver units and maintain communication between guides and commanders.
5. Protect guides from enemy retaliation; their value extends beyond a single operation.

### Examples from the Text
> "Those who do not use local guides are unable to obtain the advantages of the ground."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do compensate guides fairly and treat them with respect to encourage cooperation.
- Do have multiple guides so that you can cross-verify their information.
- Do debrief guides after each march to capture new intelligence.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't rely on a single guide; he may be compromised, mistaken, or hostile.
- Don't reveal your full operational plan to a guide; give him only the information necessary for his role.
- Don't use guides for tasks beyond their knowledge (e.g., don't ask a village guide about military installations in a distant city).

### Security/Safety Notes
- Guides have access to your movement plans and dispositions. Screen them for loyalty and monitor their communications.
- If a guide is captured by the enemy, assume that your route and timetable are compromised.

## Actionable Lesson 15: Practice Deception in All Operations

### Core Principle
War is based on deception. Appear weak when you are strong; appear strong when you are weak. Conceal your true intentions and movements.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. For each major operation, design a deception plan that masks your true objective.
2. Use feints, dummy formations, false radio traffic, and misleading logistics movements to draw enemy attention away from your real effort.
3. Vary your tactics so that the enemy cannot develop a fixed pattern of your behavior.
4. Exploit the enemy's expectations: if he expects you to attack from the north, prepare your main effort from the south.
5. After achieving surprise, exploit the resulting confusion before the enemy can reorganize.

### Examples from the Text
> "Now, war is based on deception. Move when it is advantageous and change tactics by dispersal and concentration of your troops."
> "When hiding, be as unfathomable as things behind the clouds; when moving, fall like a thunderclap."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do invest in dedicated deception planning as part of every major operation.
- Do use multiple deception channels (visual, signals, logistics) to reinforce the false narrative.
- Do time your true action to coincide with the enemy's maximum confusion.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't rely on a single deception method; the enemy may see through it.
- Don't let deception become so elaborate that it interferes with your real operation.
- Don't assume the enemy is deceived; verify through intelligence.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Deception plans must be closely held. If the enemy learns that you are planning deception, they will discount all your signals.
- Cover your deception preparations with appropriate security classifications and need-to-know restrictions.

## Actionable Lesson 16: Adopt the Appropriate Posture for Each Phase of Operations

### Core Principle
Your posture and behavior should change to match the phase of the campaign: swift when campaigning, majestic when marching, fierce when raiding, firm when standing, hidden when concealing, and overwhelming when striking.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. During active campaigning, move with speed and aggression to seize the initiative.
2. During deliberate marches, maintain discipline and order to project stability and strength.
3. During raids or punitive actions, apply overwhelming and ruthless force to destroy enemy will.
4. When holding a position, maintain unwavering firmness to demonstrate that you cannot be displaced.
5. When concealing your forces, maintain absolute operational security so that the enemy cannot detect you.
6. When the moment to strike arrives, deliver the blow with the suddenness and power of a thunderclap.

### Examples from the Text
> "When campaigning, be swift as the wind; in leisurely march, be majestic as the forest; in raiding and plundering, be fierce as fire; in standing, be firm as the mountains. When hiding, be as unfathomable as things behind the clouds; when moving, fall like a thunderclap."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do communicate the required posture clearly to all subordinate units.
- Do train forces to transition between postures rapidly and smoothly.
- Do use each posture to shape enemy perception and behavior.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't apply the wrong posture to the situation (e.g., being cautious when speed is required).
- Don't allow postures to become rigid habits; adapt the posture to the specific context.
- Don't neglect the transition between postures; this is when forces are most vulnerable.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Inappropriate postures can reveal your intentions to the enemy. A force that moves too cautiously may signal that it is conducting a delicate operation.
- The "thunderclap" strike requires complete surprise; any prior indication of your presence or intent compromises the effect.

## Actionable Lesson 17: Weigh the Situation Before You Move

### Core Principle
Do not act impulsively. Assess the full situation — enemy disposition, terrain, timing, logistics, and morale — before committing to movement or action.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Gather all available intelligence on the enemy, terrain, and your own force.
2. Conduct a deliberate analysis of advantages and disadvantages.
3. Identify the critical variables that will determine success or failure.
4. Model potential outcomes for different courses of action.
5. Select the course of action with the highest probability of success and the lowest risk of catastrophic failure.
6. Execute with confidence, but maintain the ability to reassess and adapt.

### Examples from the Text
> "Weigh the situation before you move. He who knows the artifice of deviation will be victorious."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do use a structured decision-making framework (e.g., political, military, economic, social, information, infrastructure, physical environment, time).
- Do involve multiple perspectives in the assessment to avoid blind spots.
- Do establish decision points and criteria for changing course.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't rush to judgment based on incomplete intelligence.
- Don't let past successes or failures bias your assessment of the current situation.
- Don't allow analysis paralysis; at some point, a decision must be made with imperfect information.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Intelligence used for weighing the situation must be accurate and timely. Bad intelligence leads to bad decisions.
- The deliberation process itself must be secured; enemy intelligence should not know that you are weighing a particular course of action.

## Actionable Lesson 18: Unify Troop Action Through Clear Signaling Systems

### Core Principle
Large forces cannot coordinate effectively without a shared communication system. Signals must be clear, standardized, and understood by all ranks.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Establish a standardized signal system appropriate to the environment (auditory signals for night/close terrain, visual signals for day/open terrain).
2. Assign specific signals to specific actions (e.g., drumbeat = advance, gong = halt, red banner = attack, white banner = retreat).
3. Train all troops to recognize and respond to signals automatically, even under stress and noise.
4. Use multiple redundant signals for critical commands (e.g., both drums and banners for the same order).
5. Designate signal officers and maintain communication security to prevent enemy deception.

### Examples from the Text
> "As the voice cannot be heard in battle, gongs and drums are used. As troops cannot see each other clearly in battle, flags and banners are used."
> "When the troops can be thus united, the brave cannot advance alone, nor can the cowardly retreat."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do rehearse signal systems regularly under realistic conditions (noise, smoke, darkness).
- Do have backup signal methods in case primary systems fail.
- Do ensure that signals are distinct and cannot be easily confused.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't use too many signals; troops will not remember them all.
- Don't change signal definitions during an operation without explicit warning.
- Don't rely solely on signals that the enemy can intercept and mimic.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Signal systems are vulnerable to enemy interception and imitation. Use codes, encryption, or one-time signal books to protect critical commands.
- Enemy deception using fake signals can cause friendly forces to advance or retreat at the wrong time. Counter this by cross-checking signals with multiple sources.

## Actionable Lesson 19: Time Your Attack According to the Enemy's Morale Cycle

### Core Principle
The morale of an army follows a predictable cycle: keen at the beginning of a campaign, declining after a period of time, and dwindling to nothing in the later stages. Attack when morale is lowest; avoid engagement when morale is high.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Assess the enemy's morale based on the duration of the campaign, recent victories or defeats, supply status, and leadership stability.
2. If the enemy has just begun the campaign and morale is high, avoid direct engagement; use maneuver, deception, and attrition to erode their morale over time.
3. If the enemy has been campaigning for an extended period and morale has declined, prepare to attack.
4. If the enemy is in the late stage of a campaign and morale has dwindled, strike decisively.
5. Monitor your own morale cycle and take steps to maintain it (rest, resupply, rotation, inspirational leadership).

### Examples from the Text
> "A clever commander, therefore, avoids the enemy when his spirit is keen and attacks him when it is lost. This is the art of attaching importance to moods."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do conduct psychological assessment of the enemy through observation, interrogations, and intercepted communications.
- Do plan campaigns that stretch over time to erode enemy morale deliberately.
- Do maintain your own force's morale through rest, resupply, and clear purpose.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't underestimate a fresh, high-morale enemy; even a veteran force can break against motivated troops.
- Don't allow your own force's morale to decline through neglect; a demoralized force cannot execute complex tactics.
- Don't assume the enemy's morale is low based on a single indicator; confirm through multiple observations.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Morale intelligence is often derived from intercepted communications, deserters, and civilian sources. Protect these collection methods.
- Psychological operations aimed at degrading enemy morale must be aligned with the overall campaign timeline.

## Actionable Lesson 20: Retain Self-Possession and Exploit Enemy Disorder

### Core Principle
A calm, ordered commander can exploit a disorderly, noisy, or agitated enemy. Self-possession is a force multiplier.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Maintain your own force in good order and serenity even when the enemy appears chaotic or aggressive.
2. When the enemy is clamorous, disorderly, or agitated, prepare to strike.
3. Do not be provoked into premature action by enemy taunts, feints, or displays of anger.
4. Use your serenity to observe the enemy's true state and identify the optimal moment to attack.
5. Train your command team to operate calmly under pressure and to resist emotional provocations.

### Examples from the Text
> "In good order, he awaits a disorderly enemy; in serenity, a clamorous one. This is the art of retaining self-possession."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do establish a calm, methodical command climate at headquarters.
- Do practice stress-inoculation training for commanders and staff.
- Do use deliberate decision-making processes that filter out emotional noise.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't mistake enemy noise and bluster for strength; it may mask weakness and desperation.
- Don't allow your own force to become disorderly in response to enemy provocations.
- Don't rush to attack a disorderly enemy before you have identified the exact cause of their disorder and how to exploit it.

### Security/Safety Notes
- A commander who loses self-possession is vulnerable to enemy deception, provocation, and psychological operations.
- Command post security and communications integrity are critical when operating against a disorderly enemy; they may use unconventional or desperate methods.

## Actionable Lesson 21: Husband Your Strength and Attack When the Enemy Is Exhausted

### Core Principle
Conserve your own resources and strike the enemy when he is fatigued, hungry, or far from his base. Strength is a finite resource; use it at the decisive moment.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Maintain your force at rest and well-fed whenever possible.
2. When the enemy is close to the battlefield but has marched a long distance, await him in a prepared position.
3. When the enemy has been campaigning without rest or resupply, strike while he is exhausted.
4. Rotate units to maintain freshness; do not commit elite or rested troops to holding actions.
5. Plan logistics to ensure that your force remains well-fed and supplied while the enemy's supply line degrades.

### Examples from the Text
> "Close to the field of battle, he awaits an enemy coming from afar; at rest, he awaits an exhausted enemy; with well-fed troops, he awaits hungry ones. This is the art of husbanding one's strength."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do prioritize logistics and force health over aggressive maneuver when conditions favor attrition.
- Do use defensive positions to let the enemy tire himself out against your fortifications.
- Do plan rest and resupply cycles into your campaign timeline.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't pursue a retreating enemy over long distances; you may become the exhausted force.
- Don't assume your logistics are secure; verify supply lines continuously.
- Don't let your force become complacent in a defensive posture; maintain readiness.

### Security/Safety Notes
- An exhausted enemy may fight with desperation. Leave an outlet if you surround him (see Lesson 26).
- Logistics bases must be secured; a well-fed force that loses its supply depot becomes the hungry force.

## Actionable Lesson 22: Assess the Enemy's Condition Before Attacking

### Core Principle
Do not attack an enemy whose banners are in perfect order or whose formations are in an impressive array. These are signs of discipline and readiness. Attack when signs indicate disorder or weakness.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Observe the enemy's camp discipline: Are banners orderly? Are formations aligned?
2. If the enemy's banners are in perfect order and his formations are impressive, do not attack; he is prepared.
3. Look for signs of disorder: confused formations, irregular banners, lack of sentries, disordered camps.
4. Attack when the enemy's condition indicates that he is unprepared or has lost cohesion.
5. Use observation posts and reconnaissance to continuously assess enemy condition.

### Examples from the Text
> "He refrains from intercepting an enemy whose banners are in perfect order, and desists from attacking an army whose formations are in an impressive array. This is the art of assessing circumstances."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do invest in trained observers who can read enemy formations and discipline from a distance.
- Do use multiple observation points to confirm the enemy's condition.
- Do document enemy condition assessments over time to track trends.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't mistake an enemy's elaborate but empty display for actual strength; some armies use show to mask weakness.
- Don't attack a well-ordered enemy out of overconfidence or pressure to act.
- Don't assume the enemy's condition is static; it can change rapidly.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Observation of enemy formations must be conducted from secure positions; enemy sentries and countersurveillance may detect your observers.
- Attack plans should include contingency options if the enemy's condition is better than expected.

## Actionable Lesson 23: Respect Terrain Advantages — Do Not Attack Uphill or Into Hills

### Core Principle
The army that occupies high ground or rests its back on hills has a natural defensive advantage. Attacking uphill or directly into terrain that protects the enemy's rear is extremely costly.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. When planning an attack, identify whether the enemy occupies high ground.
2. If the enemy is on higher ground, do not launch a direct frontal assault uphill; instead, maneuver to force the enemy off the high ground or attack from an unexpected direction.
3. If the enemy's back is resting on hills, do not make a frontal attack; the enemy is protected from encirclement and has strong defensive terrain.
4. Identify the terrain features that favor the enemy and develop plans to neutralize or bypass them.
5. Seek to occupy high ground yourself before the battle begins.

### Examples from the Text
> "Now, the art of employing troops is that when the enemy occupies high ground, do not confront him uphill, and when his back is resting on hills, do not make a frontal attack."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do conduct terrain analysis early in the planning process to identify high ground and defensible terrain.
- Do use maneuver to threaten the enemy's flanks or rear rather than attacking his fortified front.
- Do occupy high ground yourself to force the enemy to attack uphill.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't attack a strongly positioned enemy on favorable terrain unless you have overwhelming force and no alternative.
- Don't allow your own force to be caught on low ground with high ground occupied by the enemy.
- Don't underestimate the physical and psychological cost of an uphill assault.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Attacking uphill or into hill-protected positions can result in catastrophic casualties. Intelligence must confirm the enemy's exact position and terrain advantages before the attack.
- If the enemy occupies high ground, they likely have observation of your movements; maintain operational security accordingly.

## Actionable Lesson 24: Recognize and Avoid Enemy Traps and Bait

### Core Principle
The enemy may pretend to flee, offer bait, or create situations designed to draw you into a trap or an unfavorable engagement.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. When the enemy appears to retreat, assess whether it is a genuine withdrawal or a feigned retreat designed to draw you into an ambush.
2. Do not pursue a retreating enemy without first securing your flanks and rear and confirming that the retreat is not a trap.
3. When the enemy offers an attractive objective (e.g., a lightly guarded convoy, an exposed position), assess whether it is bait designed to fix your force while the main enemy force maneuvers against you.
4. Do not swallow bait without probing it first and ensuring that your main force is protected.
5. Maintain discipline: even if the temptation to pursue or seize bait is strong, wait until you have confirmed the situation.

### Examples from the Text
> "When he pretends to flee, do not pursue. Do not attack soldiers whose temper is keen. Do not swallow a bait offered by the enemy."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do use probes to test whether a retreat or exposed position is genuine.
- Do maintain reserve forces and security elements during pursuit operations.
- Do train commanders to resist the emotional pull of pursuit and to follow disciplined procedures.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't pursue a retreating enemy into unknown or unfavorable terrain.
- Don't commit your entire force to seizing bait; keep a strong reserve.
- Don't assume that an enemy who seems to be in disarray is actually defeated.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Feigned retreats and bait are classic deception operations. Enemy intelligence may be monitoring your reaction to shape the battlefield.
- Pursuit forces are often overextended and vulnerable to counterattack. Maintain communication with the main body and establish rally points.

## Actionable Lesson 25: Do Not Block a Retreating or Returning Enemy's Escape

### Core Principle
An enemy with no escape route will fight with desperate ferocity. Allowing an outlet reduces enemy resistance and preserves your own forces.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. When surrounding an enemy force, deliberately leave an outlet — a route of retreat that appears viable.
2. Do not completely encircle and cut off the enemy unless you have sufficient force to prevent breakout and can sustain the operation.
3. If the enemy is returning home or withdrawing from your territory, do not block his path entirely; let him leave.
4. A retreating enemy who sees a path to safety will abandon equipment, discard non-essentials, and flee — reducing his combat effectiveness without a fight.
5. If the enemy is desperate and has no outlet, prepare for maximum resistance and allocate sufficient force to overcome it.

### Examples from the Text
> "Do not thwart an enemy who is returning homewards. When you surround an army, leave an outlet free. Do not press a desperate enemy too hard."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- Do calculate whether the political and military benefits of total destruction outweigh the cost of a prolonged fight against a desperate enemy.
- Do use the outlet as a control measure: the enemy will flow through it, where you can attrit him with lighter forces.
- Do prepare to exploit the enemy's disorganized retreat once the outlet is opened.

### Common Pitfalls (Don'ts)
- Don't surround an enemy completely unless you are prepared for a decisive, high-casualty battle.
- Don't press a desperate enemy into a last-stand; the cost may be disproportionate to the gain.
- Don't let the enemy escape so completely that he can reorganize and return stronger.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Allowing an outlet may enable the enemy to regroup, rearm, and return. Assess whether the immediate cost savings outweigh the long-term risk.
- Pursuit forces that follow a retreating enemy through an outlet must maintain cohesion and security to avoid being ambushed by rear guards.
