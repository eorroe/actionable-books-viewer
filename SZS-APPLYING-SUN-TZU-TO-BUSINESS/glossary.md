# Glossary: Sun Tzu for Success

|Term|Definition|Index Pages|
|----|----------|-----------|
|Art of War|Sun Tzu's 13-chapter military treatise, positioned in this book as the world's oldest self-help guide whose timeless principles apply equally to war, business, and personal achievement.|1–44|
|Breakthrough|A planned, disruptive leap in results rather than incremental improvement, achieved through extraordinary effort and pleasant disruption to capture attention and overcome inertia.|119–122|
|Courage|The bridge between conviction and action, built through preparation and balanced confidence, enabling leaders to take calculated risks and act decisively.|67–69|
|Creativity|The practice of combining known elements in new ways to discover alternate solutions, using tools such as structured brainstorming, Ben Franklin balance sheets, and thinking outside the box.|73–76|
|Discipline|The differentiator between good and great, practiced through continuous improvement (kaizen), small consistent habits, and rigorous adherence to standards regardless of convenience.|70–72|
|Espionage|The use of secret agents for strategic foreknowledge, described by Sun Tzu as the most important element of war; five types of spies serve distinct functions in gathering enemy intelligence.|41–44|
|High Ground|A position of mental and physical advantage built on moral beliefs, integrity, personal health, and career security, occupied through disciplined planning and sustained effort.|147–151|
|Initiative|The strategic advantage gained by seizing and retaining the offensive, forcing the opponent to react rather than act, and sustaining momentum through continuous forward action.|131–134|
|Intelligence|Foreknowledge gained from human sources about the enemy, combined with practical communication systems (drums, signals, signs) that unify large forces and enable informed decision-making.|20–22, 41–44|
|Know Yourself|The foundational principle of self-knowledge as the prerequisite for all success, including self-assessment, commitment to core values, and leveraging personal strengths while accepting help from others.|50–55|
|Laying Plans|Chapter One of The Art of War, covering the five constant factors and seven comparative attributes used to evaluate any conflict situation and predict victory through careful analysis.|3–5|
|Leadership|The art of communicating vision, designing strategy, and marshaling resources while avoiding five fatal flaws: recklessness, cowardice, quick temper, oversensitivity to honor, and excessive concern for troops.|179–180|
|Moral Integrity|The bedrock of personal reputation and leadership credibility, requiring the courage to do what is right under pressure and maintaining moral clarity even when short-term losses result.|56–60|
|Momentum|Sustained offensive energy that transforms initial speed into lasting success, produced by rapid decision-making, simultaneous action, and the morale boost of continuous forward movement.|138–139|
|Networking|The deliberate cultivation of personal contacts as a source of foreknowledge and competitive advantage, requiring continuous investment through handwritten notes, phone contact, and online engagement.|94–97|
|Opportunity|Favorable moments for action that must be seized at the right time, identified through a prepared "ten sure things" list and leveraged reciprocally to create successive advances in success.|140–143|
|Opponent|The competitor or adversary whose style, strengths, and weaknesses must be understood through active intelligence-gathering in order to apply overwhelming force at the decisive point.|158–170|
|Planning|The disciplined process of translating vision into a written plan of action using the six honest serving men (what, where, when, why, how, who), with timetables, contingencies, and flexibility built in.|123–126|
|Positioning|Building a strong, authentic personal and professional position through character, expertise, relationships, and alliances, ensuring invincibility by selecting a narrow niche and forming lasting partnerships.|111–115|
|Preparation|The thorough assessment of conditions, knowledge of terrain and opponent, and disciplined readiness that ensures victory before battle begins, embodied in the motto "Be Prepared."|89–93, 46|
|Persistence|The consistent execution of strategy through three implementation modes—sequential, cumulative, and simultaneous—driven by courageous patience to overcome obstacles and maintain forward motion.|144–146|
|Risk|Calculated exposure accepted by courageous leaders who balance confidence with wisdom, avoiding both reckless overconfidence and paralyzing cowardice in pursuit of worthwhile objectives.|67–69|
|Sound Counsel|The practice of seeking advice from a personal staff of gurus, coaches, and sponsors while avoiding "eloquent ignorance," recognizing that quality of guidance matters more than quantity.|81–84|
|Standards|Benchmarks of excellence applied at the input, operating, and output levels, raised by improving processes upstream rather than merely chasing results, with new methodologies permanently replacing old ones.|77–79|
|Strategy|The correct allocation of resources and high-level plan that determines what battles to fight; a correct strategy can sustain many tactical failures, while no amount of good tactics can save a bad strategy.|98–100|
|Strength Against Weakness|The principle of concentrating overwhelming force at the decisive point where the opponent is vulnerable, reinforcing existing strengths rather than fixing weaknesses, and selecting niches with limited competition.|105–108|
|Surprise|The tactical principle of concealing dispositions, appearing where not expected, and striking with unexpected approaches to create confusion and reduce the enemy's ability to prepare or respond effectively.|16–19|
|Sun Tzu|Ancient Chinese military strategist (~500 B.C.) whose Art of War provides the foundational wisdom for this book, teaching that self-knowledge and strategic preparation precede all success in war, business, and life.|xi–xv|
|Tactics|The execution-level implementation of strategy, including offensive action, rapid movement, and variation of methods adapted to changing circumstances, doing things right within the framework of the strategic plan.|127–153|
|Teamwork|Collaborative effort in which shared purpose and mutual trust produce results superior to individual work, illustrated by the principle that four brave men who know each other will attack resolutely.|116–118|
|Terrain|The physical and situational environment that shapes strategy, classified into six ground types in Sun Tzu's framework, each demanding distinct positioning and movement principles for competitive advantage.|29–32|
|Timing|The critical factor in competitive success, requiring early action, rapid decision-making, and getting inside the opponent's observation-orientation-decision-action cycle to generate unavoidable mismatches.|169–170|
|Victory Without Fighting|The supreme excellence of strategy, achieved through superior preparation, intimidation, or innovation so that victory is assured before engagement, making actual conflict unnecessary.|101–104|
|Vision|A vivid, concrete image of desired future results expressed as strategic initiatives in career, family, health, and renewal, providing the organizing framework for all effective strategy and action.|98–100|
|Waging War|Chapter Two of The Art of War, emphasizing the economics and logistics of sustained effort, teaching that quick victories preserve resources and morale while prolonged campaigns exhaust armies and bankrupt states.|6–8|
