# Chapter Analysis: Section One — Personal Characteristics for Success
## Pages 47–84 from *Sun Tzu for Success* by Gerald Michaelson & Steven Michaelson

---

## Overview

Section One establishes the foundation for personal success through nine interconnected characteristics derived from Sun Tzu's *The Art of War*. Each characteristic is presented as a learnable behavior that can be practiced and integrated into daily life. The section argues that self-knowledge, moral integrity, effective listening, consideration, courage, discipline, creativity, high standards, and sound counsel form the bedrock of sustainable achievement.

---

## 1. Know Yourself (Pages 50–55)

### Core Principle
Accurate self-perception is the "golden key to success." Each person is perceived as three different people: who we think we are, who others think we are, and who we really are. The most important is "who we really are."

### Actionable Lessons

#### 1.1 Identify Your Personality Type
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Determine whether you lean toward being a "thinker" (logic-based decisions) or a "feeler" (values/emotions-based decisions).
2. Observe the people you associate with most comfortably — they likely share your personality preferences.
3. Use tools like the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator for structured self-assessment.
4. Compare your self-selected preferences with measured results to identify gaps.

**Examples from text:**
- Thinkers base decisions on factual data; feelers base decisions on personal values and emotions.
- Looking at your closest friends provides a "mirror image" of yourself.

**Best practices:**
- Base arguments on the decision preference of the person you are trying to convince, not your own.
- Use self-knowledge to understand others better — "self-knowledge breeds a universe of knowledge about human dynamics."

**Common pitfalls:**
- Assuming others make decisions the same way you do.
- Ignoring feedback from personality assessments when results differ from self-perception.

#### 1.2 Determine Your Values
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Ask: "What are the values that drive you?"
2. Identify what has driven your past successes and failures.
3. Write down your observations and find common links.
4. Review your list regularly to maintain clarity.

**Examples from text:**
- At age 26, Ben Franklin outlined his own list of personal values.
- Every decision you make reveals something about yourself.

**Best practices:**
- Use values as a compass for decision-making.
- Share values in conversations to attract like-minded people.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Failing to write down observations, making patterns hard to see.
- Letting priorities drift without conscious review.

#### 1.3 Show Commitment to Core Values
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Identify the core values that make you who you are.
2. Recognize that these values seldom change.
3. Let this commitment establish your identity and determine your future.

**Best practices:**
- View commitment as the foundation of identity.
- Let values guide long-term goals.

#### 1.4 Accept Help from Others
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Find a sounding board — someone who can help you determine your true identity.
2. Seek 360-degree feedback from peers, subordinates, and bosses.
3. Use professional assessments and oral reviews when available.

**Examples from text:**
- A young manager used test results and interviews to understand his qualifications better over time.
- 360-degree feedback replaces the old annual performance review.

**Best practices:**
- Share assessment results with employment interviewers.
- Use others' perspectives to build confidence in your strengths.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Dismissing feedback that contradicts your self-perception.
- Going through the motions of self-analysis without genuine openness.

#### 1.5 Think Positive and Lead with Strength
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Recognize and minimize your faults.
2. Be humble but not to the extent that you set your goals too low.
3. Focus on and take advantage of your inherent strengths.
4. Win by leading with strength.

**Examples from text:**
- The Hawthorne Effect: people who believe good things are happening are motivated to do better.
- Olympic champions mentally rehearse victory because they believe in their practiced abilities.

**Best practices:**
- Balance humility with confidence.
- Use positive psychology to fuel motivation.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Setting goals too low due to excessive humility.
- Focusing on weaknesses to the point of demotivation.

#### 1.6 Plan Self-Improvement Continuously
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Adopt the mindset that you are either "green and growing" or "ripe and rotting."
2. Commit to lifelong learning — as long as you are willing to learn new things, you are growing.
3. Read *How to Win Friends and Influence People* by Dale Carnegie for self-improvement frameworks.
4. Study Lao Tzu's wisdom: "One who knows others is wise. One who knows one's self is enlightened."

**Examples from text:**
- Dale Carnegie: "Improving yourself is a lot more profitable than trying to improve others."
- Tiger Woods studied videotapes of his performance and rebuilt his swing to get better.

**Best practices:**
- Schedule regular self-reflection time.
- View improvement as an ongoing journey, not a destination.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Thinking you have all the answers (the "ripe and rotting" mindset).
- Focusing on improving others instead of yourself.

#### 1.7 Grow Through Relationships
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Take a sincere interest in others.
2. Share personal experiences to build intimacy and camaraderie.
3. When someone needs help, care about that individual at that time.
4. Share values to attract like-minded people who can become a committed team.
5. Build relationships that reflect your best qualities back to you.

**Examples from text:**
- Honest sharing builds "closeness" and fosters intimacy with yourself.
- Strong relationships help overcome setbacks en route to your goals.

**Best practices:**
- Be sincere — if you can't be sincere, don't bother.
- Think of assistance as "caring about one individual at a time."
- Keep alert for people with whom you can share a common purpose.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Offering help at times convenient for you rather than when it's needed.
- Trying to care about every person you know instead of focusing on one at a time.

### Summary
- Be who you are.
- Be enlightened — know who you are.
- Self-knowledge is the foundation of success.

## 2. Have Moral Integrity (Pages 56–60)

### Core Principle
Moral integrity is the bedrock of personal reputation and the most powerful force for success. "Either you have integrity, or you do not have integrity." Integrity without knowledge is weak and useless; knowledge without integrity is dangerous and dreadful.

### Actionable Lessons

#### 2.1 Develop Moral Clarity
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Define what is right and wrong in your core value system.
2. Apply moral clarity to your goals and objectives.
3. Ensure your actions are justified by a moral base, not just power or pity.

**Examples from text:**
- Benjamin Netanyahu's argument for Israel's right to exist was rooted in logic with a moral base, not power or pity.
- Netanyahu's moral imperatives for victory: military victory → political victory → public relations victory → justice.

**Best practices:**
- Start with a sense of justice when building arguments.
- Ensure your moral position is clear and defensible.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Justifying supposedly moral positions at the expense of integrity.
- Perpetrating evils in conjunction with a sense of moral right.

#### 2.2 Build Integrity as the Foundation of Reputation
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Adhere to a moral code in all interactions.
2. Understand that personal reputation is based on others' perceptions of your behavior.
3. Build your success on a base of integrity — it is a much stronger, firmer, and better place to start.

**Examples from text:**
- The Notre Dame football coach who lied about a Master's degree on his resume was forced to resign five days after being hired.
- An executive-search consultant reported that about 1 in 10 people lie about college degrees on their resumes.

**Best practices:**
- View integrity as non-negotiable — there is no "fudging" on integrity.
- Remember: "Although integrity does not guarantee success, lack of integrity is a prescription for failure."

**Common pitfalls:**
- Thinking small lies are acceptable because "everyone does it."
- Building success on a base that lacks integrity.

#### 2.3 Influence Through Win-Win, Not Machiavellian Means
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Aim for mutual benefit in all relationships.
2. Build trust as the backbone of communication.
3. Ask: "Does the communication come from a trusted source?" If not, the entire message is in doubt.

**Examples from text:**
- Decisions about dating, team selection, and other relationships are built on trust.
- The most effective backbone of any communication is trust.

**Best practices:**
- Use influence to achieve win-win outcomes.
- Prioritize trust in all communications.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Using influence in a Machiavellian way.
- Communicating from an untrusted source.

#### 2.4 Manage Crises with Moral Integrity
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Tell it like it is.
2. Admit uncertainties exist.
3. Don't make statements you'll have to retract later.
4. Act on the best estimate of a situation.
5. Refrain from "value judgments."

**Examples from text:**
- Harold Denton's handling of the Three Mile Island incident with calm, professional, reassuring communication.
- President Carter praised Denton for letting the American people know they need have no fear.

**Best practices:**
- Be transparent and honest during crises.
- Maintain composure under pressure.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Making statements that cannot be sustained.
- Passing value judgments that escalate tensions.

### Summary
- Integrity is a powerful force.
- Moral integrity is the bedrock of your reputation.
- Either you have integrity, or you do not have integrity.

## 3. Listen Well (Pages 61–64)

### Core Principle
Listening is learning. Active listening "assures depth in deliberation." The person who listens well gains more information, builds stronger relationships, and exercises greater control in conversations.

### Actionable Lessons

#### 3.1 Practice Active Listening
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Play back what you are hearing using paraphrasing: "What I heard you say was..."
2. Ask frequent, sincere questions to stay engaged and yield new information.
3. Acknowledge the other person's thoughts and feelings with words or body language (e.g., nodding).
4. Focus on the other person's needs to build a complete picture of logic and emotion.
5. Build the other person's self-esteem by smiling, being helpful, and staying positive.
6. Be aware of your own listening "derailers" — know what prevents you from listening well.
7. Stay in the present; don't make assumptions or drift to future implications.
8. Take notes to stay focused on the conversation.

**Examples from text:**
- General Curtis LeMay told a young lieutenant: "No. But it is how I got to be a captain" when the lieutenant interrupted him.
- Acknowledging "baggage" with a nod or comment of understanding can encourage the speaker to move on.

**Best practices:**
- Use active listening to get to deeper issues.
- Convey that you are indeed listening to help clear away emotional baggage.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Making assumptions or drifting to future implications.
- Listening only to people whose values you share.
- Letting a wandering mind prevent good listening.

#### 3.2 Use Questions as a Powerful Tool
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Prepare a list of questions before important meetings.
2. Ask questions to persuade — help people convince themselves of a position.
3. Ask questions to stay engaged — two-way conversations are better than monologues.
4. Ask questions to maintain control — questions guide the discussion and keep it on track.
5. Remember: people are more convinced by what they say than by what you say.

**Examples from text:**
- When executives ask how to prepare for an important visitor, the advice is: "prepare a list of questions."
- "Why is it that we are always thinking of how we are going to respond and what we are going to tell rather than what we are going to ask?"

**Best practices:**
- Don't play defense by responding to the other person's questions.
- A list of questions prepared in advance is your strongest weapon.
- The more questions you ask, the more you listen; the more information you gather, the more you know.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Playing defense by responding to others' questions instead of asking your own.
- Expecting immediate acceptance of your ideas.
- Drifting into monologue instead of two-way conversation.

#### 3.3 Use the Power of Silence
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. After asking a question, wait for the other person to respond.
2. Resist the urge to step into the conversation vacuum first.
3. Give the other person time to think — silence often pressures a positive response.
4. If you talk first, you may give them an excuse for not taking action.

**Examples from text:**
- If you ask a person if they will do something and say, "Perhaps you are too busy," they will often latch on to that excuse.
- Good salespeople know to "shut up" and wait after asking a question.

**Best practices:**
- Wait, and wait, and wait for an answer.
- Use silence to gather information and pressure positive responses.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Filling silence with your own words.
- Offering excuses that the other person can use to decline.

### Summary
- Listening is learning.
- Practice active listening.
- Questions provide the answer.

## 4. Be Considerate (Pages 65–66)

### Core Principle
Pay attention to others' well-being and do not fatigue them. Keep them in high spirits and conserve their energy. Treat captives (and by extension, all people) with civility and magnanimity.

### Actionable Lessons

#### 4.1 Don't Abuse Power
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Recognize the power hierarchy in all relationships.
2. Resist the temptation to make decisions that work to your advantage when you have nothing to gain.
3. Return phone calls even when you have nothing to gain.
4. Consider how your actions affect others' well-being and income.

**Examples from text:**
- A president of a large financial group bawled out a senior executive for making a delivery person wait 25 minutes, recognizing that the waiting time reduced the delivery person's income.
- This incident became a corporate legend and solidified the president's reputation as a considerate person.

**Best practices:**
- Treat people well regardless of their position or your potential gain.
- Be magnanimous when you are in control to set up winning situations when you are not.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Not returning phone calls when you have nothing to gain.
- Making people wait without consideration for their time and income.

#### 4.2 Don't Burn Bridges
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Treat others with respect even when you have power over them.
2. Remember that "it's a small world" — alienated people may rise to be peers or superiors.
3. Avoid burning bridges in any relationship.

**Examples from text:**
- In the new "knowledge worker" information age, we sometimes find ourselves in control of information; at other times we need information.
- Alienated people often appear at professional gatherings, restaurants, or local school events.

**Best practices:**
- Treating people well is a building block for success that sticks.
- Consider the long-term value of every relationship.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Alienating people across an industry.
- Assuming you will never need someone's help again.

### Summary
- Don't abuse power.
- Don't burn your bridges.
- Do unto others as you would have them do unto you.

## 5. Be Courageous (Pages 67–69)

### Core Principle
Courage is taking action to do the right thing. It is balanced confidence and wisdom. While lack of confidence is at one extreme, overconfidence is at the deadly other extreme.

### Actionable Lessons

#### 5.1 Build Self-Confidence
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Develop physical self-confidence through bodily training and skill.
2. Develop mental self-confidence through mental preparation and belief in your abilities.
3. Let physical and mental self-confidence reinforce each other.
4. Take calculated risks — we cannot win if we do not take risks.

**Examples from text:**
- A quarterback who has learned to pass accurately believes in his skill; the mental belief helps him keep a cool head and perform well.
- General Wavell: "A bold general may be lucky, but no general can be lucky unless he is bold."
- General McClellan was "so fearful of losing that he could not win."

**Best practices:**
- Train so that confidence has a sound foundation.
- Use mental rehearsal to build confidence (like Olympic athletes).

**Common pitfalls:**
- Overconfidence creates situations where you will be defeated by the unexpected.
- Being sure everything is going well makes you vulnerable to surprise.

#### 5.2 Take Action to Do the Right Thing
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. When impulse is required, do what you feel has to be done.
2. Don't overthink courage — people who take action seldom claim to be courageous; they say they "just did what anyone else would have done."
3. Understand the circumstances and do what needs to be done.

**Examples from text:**
- Todd Beamer on Flight 93 on September 11, 2001: "Let's roll" — he and fellow passengers became heroes by understanding the circumstances and doing what needed to be done.
- Betty Ford demonstrated true courage by going public with her alcoholism and founding the Betty Ford Center.

**Best practices:**
- Recognize that courage is people taking action on something in which they believe.
- Don't wait for a lifelong ambition to be courageous — act when the situation demands it.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Waiting for the "right" time instead of acting when needed.
- Trying to overtake hijackers without considering the cost (as in the Flight 93 example).

#### 5.3 Balance Confidence with Wisdom
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Develop the ability to make the right decisions.
2. Develop the courage to act on those decisions.
3. Avoid overconfidence — when you begin thinking you are really number one, you begin to go nowhere.
4. Kick away the ladder behind you when you have climbed to success — take risks and don't look back.

**Examples from text:**
- Stevie Wonder: "When you begin thinking you are really number one, that's when you begin to go nowhere."
- Field Marshal Montgomery: "Many qualities go to make a leader, but two are vital — the ability to make the right decisions and the courage to act on the decisions."

**Best practices:**
- Use wisdom to temper courage.
- Take calculated risks, not reckless ones.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Overconfidence leading to defeat by the unexpected.
- Acting without wisdom or judgment.

### Summary
- Balance confidence with wisdom.
- Take action to do the right thing.
- This is courage.

## 6. Practice Discipline (Pages 70–72)

### Core Principle
Discipline is a friend, not an enemy. All great achievements are won through discipline. "It takes discipline to practice self-discipline."

### Actionable Lessons

#### 6.1 Apply Daily Discipline Routines
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Get up early every morning to study how you can improve your profession or exercise to improve physical health.
2. Don't put off discipline until later in the day — it will be put off until the next day, and the next.
3. Practice consistency — without consistency there is no discipline.
4. Eliminate excuses — excuses for not practicing good discipline are the real enemy.

**Examples from text:**
- Henry Aaron: "Discipline" was his one-word answer to the difference between a good team and a great team.
- Tiger Woods is known for being the last to leave the practice tee.
- Tom Brady, the day after winning the Super Bowl: "There's so much room for improvement, I don't know where to start."

**Best practices:**
- View discipline as a friend that keeps you doing the right thing.
- Start with small things that eventually become habits.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Putting discipline off until later in the day.
- Making excuses for not practicing discipline.

#### 6.2 Practice Kaizen (Continuous Improvement)
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Adopt the Japanese concept of kaizen — continuous improvement of processes.
2. Apply kaizen to personal improvement, not just business processes.
3. Rebuild and refine your skills even when you are already excellent.

**Examples from text:**
- Tiger Woods studied videotapes of his performance after dominating the 1997 Masters and went back to his coach to rebuild his swing.
- Kaizen is the discipline of rebuilding a process.

**Best practices:**
- Continuously rebuild and refine your approach.
- Never stop improving, even at the top of your field.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Thinking you have "made it" and don't need to improve.
- Ignoring continuous improvement because current performance is good enough.

#### 6.3 Apply Colin Powell's Seven Laws of Power
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Dare to be the Skunk — tolerate rebels who tell the emperor he has no clothes.
2. To get the real dirt, head for the trenches — the people in the field are closest to the problem and have the real wisdom.
3. Share the power — plans don't accomplish work; people get things done.
4. Know when to ignore your advisors — experts often possess more data than judgment.
5. Develop selective amnesia — never let ego get so close to your position that when your position goes, your ego goes with it.
6. Come up for air — don't log hours to impress others; you are wasting time.
7. Declare victory and quit — know the importance of timing in withdrawing from a position.

**Examples from text:**
- Colin Powell's laws are described as key disciplines for power and success.

**Best practices:**
- Follow these rules or any other set of rules requires discipline.
- Set a realistic number of goals, then move on to bigger challenges.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Following rules without discipline.
- Letting ego become tied to your position.

### Summary
- Discipline is a friend.
- Discipline makes good people great.

## 7. Be Creative (Pages 73–76)

### Core Principle
Creativity is not a mystical thing; it is simply people searching for alternate solutions to problems. "The extraordinary does not necessarily mean the newest and latest or the most expensive. Often the extraordinary lies in the simple, the mundane, the everyday things we look at with a fresh perspective."

### Actionable Lessons

#### 7.1 Brainstorm and Generate Options
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. On a "blank piece of paper," develop a list of options.
2. Construct a "Ben Franklin Balance Sheet" by making two columns: "yes" and "no."
3. Write down the "yes" and "no" reasons, compare them, and make your decision.
4. Set up structured brainstorming sessions with a small group around a flip chart.
5. Follow simple brainstorming rules to generate input without criticism.

**Examples from text:**
- A brainstorming group developed over 2,000 uses for parts of a chicken.
- A small Texas restaurant company hung out a simple sign saying "Drive Thru" in the 1950s, and their business exploded.

**Best practices:**
- Use tools to enhance creative thinking.
- Combine known elements in useful variations — you don't need to invent new "musical notes," "colors," or "flavors."

**Common pitfalls:**
- Thinking creativity is only for "other people."
- Believing great ideas come as "lightning bolts from the sky."

#### 7.2 Think Outside of the Box
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Identify your current box of knowledge (e.g., professional expertise).
2. Map expanding circles of knowledge: suppliers/customers, industry organizations, organizations outside your industry, any organization in the world.
3. Recognize that the farther from your box, the less you know.
4. Task yourself to "think outside of the box" and into new areas that enhance your value and assure future income.
5. Get out of the box and find new opportunities away from the box.
6. Remember: "I'll see it when I know it" — only when you know about something do you see it as a new opportunity.

**Examples from text:**
- A leader drew concentric boxes on a flip chart to show expanding circles of knowledge and challenged his people to think outside the box.
- The "Drive Thru" concept was a creative solution outside the traditional restaurant box.

**Best practices:**
- Systematically expand your knowledge beyond your immediate field.
- Look for opportunities in areas you don't yet understand.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Thinking "I'll know it when I see it" instead of "I'll see it when I know it."
- Staying inside the box of what is known.

#### 7.3 Unleash Creative Energy
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Understand that true creativity is a succession of acts, each dependent on the one before and suggesting the one after.
2. Accept that creativity involves rational advancements, not lightning bolts.
3. Recognize that creativity arises out of what you know and the focus of your interests.
4. Let your passions kindle the fire of creativity.
5. Use Sun Tzu's concept of "energy" — the creativity of human beings tackling problems by looking for the extraordinary.

**Examples from text:**
- Edward H. Land (inventor of the Polaroid camera): "True creativity is characterized by a succession of acts each dependent on the one before and suggesting the one after."
- Charles Darwin: "At no time am I a quick thinker or writer; whatever I have done in science has solely been by long pondering, patience, and industry."

**Best practices:**
- Work hard at creativity — it revolves around "knowing our stuff."
- Use the "attainable creativity" Sun Tzu describes — combining the known in useful variations.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Expecting quick thinking or sudden inspiration.
- Believing creativity is mystical and unattainable.

### Summary
- Brainstorm.
- Think outside of the box.
- Unleash your creative energy.

## 8. Aim for High Standards (Pages 77–79)

### Core Principle
Success is not doing what needs to be done. Success is going "above and beyond" what needs to be done. To succeed, you must break "the ordinary rules" and set new standards of performance.

### Actionable Lessons

#### 8.1 Focus on Process Improvement
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Recognize that improvement in the process creates results.
2. Identify three types of standards in every process:
   - Input standards: Ingredients that go into a process.
   - Operating standards: Process activities.
   - Output standards: The results of the process.
3. Work upstream on input and operating standards to achieve better output standards.
4. Understand that a last-minute scramble to improve on the day of the event is too late.

**Examples from text:**
- Preparing a meal: Input standards (quality of meat, freshness of ingredients) → Operating standards (cooking time and temperature) → Output standards (serving temperature, taste, appearance).
- The high jump evolved through process changes: Scissors → Western Roll → Straddle → Fosbury Flop.

**Best practices:**
- Focus on the process, not just the results.
- Improve preparation and practice standards to improve results.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Focusing only on output standards (results) without improving inputs and operations.
- Trying to improve results at the last minute.

#### 8.2 Set New Standards
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Break "the ordinary rules" and set new standards of performance.
2. Break your own records.
3. Strive for big changes in process that improve performance — they require more effort but produce bigger rewards.
4. Recognize that new standards result from changes in process.

**Examples from text:**
- Dick Fosbury introduced the "Fosbury Flop" in the high jump, and no one could compete effectively with the old methodology afterward.
- Every high jump champion today uses the Fosbury flop.

**Best practices:**
- Aim high — the higher the aim, the greater the stretch required, the more likely you are to achieve improvement.
- View success as going above and beyond what needs to be done.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Settling for doing only what needs to be done.
- Ignoring process changes that could lead to new standards.

### Summary
- Aim high.
- Work upstream.
- Set new standards.

## 9. Seek Sound Counsel (Pages 81–84)

### Core Principle
Achievement is rarely accomplished in solitary practice without the aid of coaching. Feedback from mentors and advisors who can give you ideas, support, and "course corrections" is critical.

### Actionable Lessons

#### 9.1 Build a Counseling Staff: Guru, Coach, and Sponsor
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Get a guru — a wise person not directly involved in your activities who will listen and give good positive input. Gurus are great for exploring new ideas.
2. Get a coach — someone who knows your profession or business and can go beyond your defenses and ego so you can truly "know yourself." A coach highlights your "blind spots."
3. Get a sponsor — someone at a high level in the organization who will act as your mentor. If that person moves on, cultivate another.
4. Listen carefully to your advisors and keep score of the usefulness of their advice.
5. Prioritize a few advisors of high reliability over a large quantity of advisors.

**Examples from text:**
- A young man lost all of his original sponsors when the three next senior people up the chain of command left, so he soon left the organization.
- If asked to sit on a local board, ask the person inviting you to be your mentor in the workings of the team.

**Best practices:**
- Use a coach to get feedback and tweak your performance.
- Cultivate sponsors in both professional and outside activities.
- Mutual candor is the most valuable component of a coaching relationship.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Relying on a single sponsor and being devastated when they leave.
- Having too many advisors and losing the ability to evaluate their advice.

#### 9.2 Beware of Eloquent Ignorance
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Recognize that a room full of well-meaning people who have no knowledge of the situation will often come to the wrong conclusion.
2. Beware of eloquent ignorance — it yields deadly contributions.
3. Remember: good answers come only from a blend of prior experience and local knowledge.
4. Treat opinions with appropriate skepticism — "that is one data point" is a polite way of noting that more than one data point is needed.

**Examples from text:**
- A visiting individual in a consulting meeting participated actively but had little validity — his well-intentioned input was eloquent ignorance.
- General MacArthur had a plaque in his Tokyo headquarters: "In every circle and truly at every table there are people who lead armies into Macedonia. These are great impediments to those who have the management of affairs."

**Best practices:**
- Seek advice from people who know the situation.
- Blend prior experience with local knowledge.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Valuing eloquence over knowledge.
- Letting uninformed people dominate meetings.

#### 9.3 Use Meetings Effectively
**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. Avoid using meetings as a decision-making forum — use meetings for input and then make your decision.
2. Recognize that the ability to carry on a meaningful and intelligent discussion decreases with an increase in the number of people attending.
3. Keep counsel with advisors who know the situation — all advice is not sound advice, and all voices should not be considered equal.
4. Avoid voting — it polarizes people.
5. Form small support groups of like-minded individuals (who may or may not be in your field) for exchanging information and networking.
6. Use noncompetitive relationships as an ideal sounding board for testing ideas and solving problems.

**Examples from text:**
- A 22-person board of directors session was frustrating because the group was too large for interchange and much advice was given by people impressed only with the sound of their own voices.

**Best practices:**
- Poll everyone in a small group to get their opinion, but avoid voting.
- Use small groups for meaningful discussion.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Using large meetings for decision-making.
- Letting people with loud voices but little knowledge dominate discussions.

### Summary
- Quality beats quantity.
- Eloquent ignorance is deadly.

## Cross-Cutting Themes and Integration

### Theme 1: Self-Knowledge as Foundation
Every characteristic in this section begins with knowing yourself. Whether identifying your personality type, understanding your values, or recognizing your listening derailers, self-knowledge is the prerequisite for all other personal characteristics.

### Theme 2: Action Over Theory
Sun Tzu for Success emphasizes doing, not just thinking. Discipline is practicing self-discipline. Courage is taking action. Creativity is searching for alternate solutions. Success requires action.

### Theme 3: Relationships as Mirrors
Relationships are repeatedly cited as vehicles for self-growth. They reflect your strengths and weaknesses, provide feedback, and offer support. Treating others well builds the kind of success that sticks.

### Theme 4: Balance and Wisdom
Each characteristic requires balance:
- Confidence balanced with wisdom (courage).
- Humility balanced with strength (self-knowledge).
- Discipline balanced with flexibility (kaizen).
- Courage balanced with judgment (action).

### Theme 5: Process Over Results
Whether in high standards, discipline, or creativity, the text emphasizes that process improvement creates results. Working upstream on inputs and operations yields better outputs.

## Implementation Roadmap

### Phase 1: Self-Assessment (Weeks 1-4)
1. Take the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator or similar personality assessment.
2. Write down your core values and review past decisions to identify patterns.
3. Seek 360-degree feedback from peers, subordinates, and bosses.
4. Identify one area for self-improvement based on feedback.

### Phase 2: Foundation Building (Weeks 5-8)
1. Practice active listening in every conversation — play back, ask questions, acknowledge.
2. Prepare a list of questions before every important meeting.
3. Practice using silence effectively in conversations.
4. Identify one power imbalance in your life and practice not abusing it.

### Phase 3: Character Development (Weeks 9-12)
1. Define your moral code and test decisions against it.
2. Practice telling it like it is, admitting uncertainties, and refraining from value judgments.
3. Take one courageous action outside your comfort zone.
4. Establish a daily discipline routine (early morning improvement practice).

### Phase 4: Excellence and Growth (Weeks 13-16)
1. Brainstorm creative solutions to one problem using a blank piece of paper and a Ben Franklin Balance Sheet.
2. Identify one process you can improve and work upstream on input and operating standards.
3. Set a new standard for yourself in one area and break your own record.
4. Identify a guru, coach, and sponsor for your personal and professional development.

### Phase 5: Sustained Success (Ongoing)
1. Review and update your values quarterly.
2. Continue 360-degree feedback regularly.
3. Maintain discipline through kaizen — continuous small improvements.
4. Reassess your counseling staff annually and adjust as needed.
5. Practice consideration in every interaction.
6. Review processes monthly and aim for new standards.
