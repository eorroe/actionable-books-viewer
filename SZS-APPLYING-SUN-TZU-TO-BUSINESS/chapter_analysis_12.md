# Chapter Eleven: The Nine Varieties of Ground — Actionable Analysis
**Source:** *Sun Tzu for Success*, Chapter Eleven, pages 33–38  
**Chapter Title:** The Nine Varieties of Ground

---

## Overview

Chapter Eleven is the deepest and most complex chapter in Book One. It maps nine distinct strategic situations (ground types) that every army — and every business leader — will encounter. Each ground type demands a different response, and the wrong response at the wrong time can be fatal. The chapter is organized into three major sections:

1. Choose the Battleground — classify the nine varieties of ground and apply the correct tactic to each
2. Shape Your Opponent's Strategy — divide enemy forces, block alliances, and make yourself invincible
3. Make Victory — study the enemy, conceal your designs, concentrate force, and strike at the decisive moment

---

## Lesson 1: Classify the Ground You're Operating On

### Core Principle
Ground may be classified into nine varieties. Each variety has a specific name, a specific definition, and a specific tactical prescription. Misclassifying the ground leads to applying the wrong tactic and suffering defeat.

### The Nine Varieties of Ground

| # | Variety | Definition | Correct Action |
|---|---------|-----------|----------------|
| 1 | **Dispersive** | A chieftain fighting in his own territory | Unify the determination of the army; do not fight if avoidable |
| 2 | **Frontier** | Shallow penetration into hostile territory | Keep forces closely linked; do not stop |
| 3 | **Key** | Ground equally advantageous for us and the enemy to occupy | Hasten to occupy; do not attack an enemy who has already occupied it |
| 4 | **Open** | Ground equally accessible to both sides | Do not allow your communications to be blocked |
| 5 | **Focal** | Ground contiguous to three other states | Consolidate alliances with neighboring states |
| 6 | **Serious** | Deep penetration into hostile territory, far behind many enemy cities | Ensure a continuous flow of provisions; gather in plunder |
| 7 | **Difficult** | Mountain forests, rugged steeps, marshes, fens, all hard to traverse | Press on; march speedily over the road |
| 8 | **Encircled** | Constricted access, tortuous retreat paths, small enemy force can crush large body | Resort to stratagems; block points of access and egress |
| 9 | **Desperate** | No place of refuge at all; army survives only through desperate fight | Fight courageously; make it evident there is no chance of survival |

### The Depth-Cohesion Rule
> "Generally, when invading hostile territory, the deeper the troops penetrate, the more cohesive they will be; penetrating only a short way causes dispersion."

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Survey your current situation** before any engagement or strategic move.
2. **Classify the ground** into one of the nine varieties using the definitions above.
3. **Apply the corresponding correct action** from the table — do not default to a generic approach.
4. **Reassess continuously** — as you penetrate deeper or circumstances shift, the ground type may change.

### Examples from the Text
- *Dispersive ground*: "When a chieftain is fighting in his own territory, he is in dispersive ground."
- *Frontier ground*: "When he has penetrated into hostile territory, but to no great distance, he is in frontier ground."
- *Serious ground*: "When an army has penetrated deep into hostile territory, leaving far behind many enemy cities and towns, it is in serious ground."
- *Desperate ground*: "Ground on which the army can avoid annihilation only through a desperate fight without delay is called a desperate one."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- **Do** treat ground classification as a required first step in any strategic planning process.
- **Do** remember that deep penetration creates cohesion, while shallow penetration causes dispersion.
- **Do** communicate the ground type and corresponding tactic to your entire team.
- **Don't** fight on dispersive ground if you can avoid it — unify purpose first.
- **Don't** stop on frontier ground — keep momentum and maintain close links.
- **Don't** attack an enemy who has already occupied key ground.
- **Don't** allow your communications to be blocked on open ground.
- **Don't** linger on critical ground without a clear plan.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Defaulting to engagement**: Many leaders feel compelled to "do something" even when the ground type calls for patience, unification, or withdrawal.
- **Ignoring the depth rule**: Assuming that shallow market entry or minor competitive moves will build cohesion, when they actually cause dispersion.
- **Misreading key ground**: Thinking that because you can occupy a position, you should — without checking whether the enemy has already secured it.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Dispersive ground breeds complacency and distraction (team members focused on home concerns rather than the mission).
- Desperate ground creates maximum effort but also maximum risk — use it deliberately, not accidentally.
- Encircled ground can turn a large force into a helpless target; recognize it early and apply stratagems before being trapped.

## Lesson 2: Match Your Strategy to Each Ground Type

### Core Principle
The nine varieties of ground are not just descriptive categories — they are prescriptive frameworks. Each ground type demands a specific strategic response. Applying the wrong response to the wrong ground is a primary cause of strategic failure.

### Strategic Prescriptions by Ground Type

| Ground Type | Prescribed Strategy | Why It Works |
|-------------|---------------------|--------------|
| Dispersive | Unify purpose and determination | Soldiers are close to home and easily distracted; shared purpose overcomes dispersion |
| Frontier | Keep forces closely linked | Shallow penetration means easy retreat; tight cohesion prevents dissolution |
| Key | Hasten — occupy first | Speed determines who controls the advantage |
| Open | Guard communications | Free movement means both sides can maneuver; blocking communications isolates you |
| Focal | Consolidate alliances | Geographic centrality makes you a nexus; allies provide security and leverage |
| Serious | Ensure provisions / gather plunder | Deep penetration severs supply lines; you must live off the enemy |
| Difficult | Press on / march speedily | Slow movement in hazardous terrain invites attack; speed minimizes exposure |
| Encircled | Resort to stratagems | Direct assault is suicide; ingenuity and deception are the only options |
| Desperate | Fight — show no survival option | When escape is impossible, troops fight with maximum commitment |

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Identify the ground type** using the definitions from Lesson 1.
2. **Select the matching strategy** from the table above.
3. **Communicate the strategy** clearly to all relevant stakeholders.
4. **Monitor for ground transitions** — as conditions change, shift strategies accordingly.
5. **Do not apply a strategy suited to one ground type to a different ground type.**

### Examples from the Text
- *Key ground*: "Do not attack an enemy who has occupied key ground; in open ground, do not allow your communication to be blocked."
- *Focal ground*: "In focal ground, form alliances with neighboring states."
- *Serious ground*: "In serious ground, gather in plunder."
- *Difficult ground*: "In difficult ground, press on."
- *Encircled ground*: "In encircled ground, resort to strategems."
- *Desperate ground*: "In desperate ground, fight courageously."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- **Do** create a decision matrix or playbook that maps ground types to strategies for your organization.
- **Do** brief your team on the current ground type and why a specific strategy is being chosen.
- **Do** review ground type and strategy at regular intervals during any extended campaign or project.
- **Don't** treat all competitive situations as the same — the ground determines the rule.
- **Don't** attempt to hold key ground with inferior forces; seize it first or abandon it.
- **Don't** allow your team to become demoralized on serious or difficult ground by failing to ensure provisions or momentum.
- **Don't** waste resources trying to break out of encircled ground with brute force; use stratagems.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Strategy mismatch**: Applying a "form alliances" strategy on open ground where speed and defense matter more than relationships.
- **Premature commitment**: Failing to hasten to key ground and then being forced to attack a prepared enemy.
- **Resource exhaustion**: Entering serious ground without ensuring a continuous flow of provisions — both militarily and in business (cash flow, resources).
- **Desperation by accident**: Allowing a situation to deteriorate into desperate ground without planning for it, rather than deliberately creating commitment.

### Security/Safety Notes
- On open ground, unsecured communications are a critical vulnerability. In business, this means protecting data flows, supply chains, and stakeholder communications.
- In encircled ground, stratagems require careful planning and secrecy. Revealing your plan to escape or counterattack before executing it destroys the advantage.
- Desperate ground, while powerful, can also destroy an organization if misused. Creating "no escape" conditions is a high-stakes leadership decision that requires absolute commitment from the team.

## Lesson 3: Shape Your Opponent's Strategy by Dividing Their Forces

### Core Principle
The highest form of strategy is to shape the enemy's strategy before battle begins. By preventing the enemy from uniting his forces, you make him fight in pieces — and a divided enemy is easily defeated.

### The Five Divisions to Create
1. **Divide van from rear**: Prevent the enemy's vanguard from supporting his rearguard.
2. **Divide large from small divisions**: Prevent coordination between major and minor forces.
3. **Hinder good troops from rescuing the bad**: Let the enemy's weaknesses become fatal.
4. **Prevent officers from rallying their men**: Disrupt command and control.
5. **Prevent allies from joining**: Isolate the enemy from external support.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Study the enemy's organizational structure** — who commands what, and how do the pieces connect?
2. **Identify the critical links** — communications, supply routes, command relationships, alliances.
3. **Design actions to sever those links** before the main engagement.
4. **Exploit the resulting disorder** — strike the separated pieces individually.
5. **Prevent reconstitution** — keep the enemy divided throughout the campaign.

### Examples from the Text
> "In ancient times, those described as skilled in war knew how to make it impossible for the enemy to unite his van and his rear, for his large and small divisions to co-operate, for his good troops to rescue the bad, his officers to rally their men."

> "It follows that one does not need to seek alliances with other neighboring states, nor is there any need to foster the power of other states, but only to pursue one's own strategic designs to overawe his enemy."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- **Do** map the enemy's full organizational structure before planning any attack.
- **Do** prioritize cutting links over destroying units — a disconnected force cannot fight effectively.
- **Do** use diplomacy, economics, and information operations to prevent enemy alliances from forming.
- **Do** create a "dominance strategy" that makes the enemy's alliances irrelevant through superior positioning.
- **Don't** attack a unified, coordinated enemy — wait until divisions have been created.
- **Don't** assume the enemy's allies will remain passive; actively work to neutralize them.
- **Don't** neglect your own internal cohesion while trying to divide the enemy.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Attacking before dividing**: Launching a direct assault on a unified, coordinated enemy.
- **Ignoring the alliance network**: Focusing only on the primary competitor while ignoring the supporting cast of allies, suppliers, and influencers.
- **Overreach**: Trying to divide too many enemy factions simultaneously, spreading your own effort too thin.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Operations designed to divide enemy forces often require intelligence penetration, diplomatic manipulation, or economic pressure — all of which carry operational security risks.
- The enemy will attempt to counter your dividing strategy by reinforcing links and launching counter-alliances. Maintain continuous intelligence on enemy relationship dynamics.

## Lesson 4: Master Intelligence, Terrain, and Local Guides

### Core Principle
Three areas of knowledge are non-negotiable for any commander: the designs of neighboring states, the conditions of terrain, and the use of local guides. Ignorance in any one of these areas makes even the strongest force vulnerable.

### The Three Knowledge Requirements

| Requirement | Consequence of Ignorance |
|-------------|--------------------------|
| **Neighboring states' designs** | Cannot enter into alliance with them; alliances become liabilities |
| **Conditions of terrain** | Cannot conduct the march of an army; forces get lost, ambushed, or delayed |
| **Local guides** | Cannot gain the advantages of the ground; must fight on unfamiliar terms |

### The Invincibility Standard
> "An army does not deserve the title of the invincible Army of the Hegemonic King if its commander is ignorant of even one of these nine varieties of ground."

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Build an intelligence system** that covers neighboring entities (competitors, partners, regulators, markets).
2. **Map terrain thoroughly** — understand mountains, forests, dangerous defiles, swamps, and marshes in your operational environment.
3. **Recruit local guides** — people with on-the-ground knowledge who can navigate complexities you cannot see from headquarters.
4. **Test your knowledge** before engagement: Can you name the ground type? Can you describe the terrain? Do you have reliable guides?
5. **Close knowledge gaps** before any major initiative. Do not proceed with ignorance as an excuse.

### Examples from the Text
> "One ignorant of the designs of neighboring states cannot enter into alliance with them. If ignorant of the conditions of mountains, forests, dangerous defiles, swamps, and marshes, he cannot conduct the march of an army. If he fails to make use of native guides, he cannot gain the advantages of the ground."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- **Do** maintain a systematic intelligence collection program covering competitors, markets, and regulatory environments.
- **Do** conduct terrain walks and site visits — do not rely solely on maps and reports.
- **Do** cultivate relationships with local experts, industry insiders, and operational veterans who can serve as guides.
- **Do** cross-validate intelligence from multiple sources before making decisions.
- **Don't** assume that general market knowledge replaces specific terrain knowledge.
- **Don't** enter a new market, region, or competitive space without a local guide or advisor.
- **Don't** form alliances without understanding the other party's true designs and motivations.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Headquarters arrogance**: Believing that strategic insight from the office can substitute for on-the-ground intelligence.
- **Skipping the guide**: Assuming that because you have data and analysis, you don't need local expertise.
- **Shallow intelligence**: Knowing what competitors are doing but not why — their underlying designs and motivations.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Intelligence on neighboring states/competitors must be collected legally and ethically. Corporate espionage or unethical data gathering creates legal and reputational risk.
- Local guides have access to your plans and dispositions. Screen them for loyalty and protect sensitive information.
- Terrain intelligence (market conditions, regulatory environments, supply chain vulnerabilities) is a competitive asset — protect it accordingly.

## Lesson 5: Build Unity Through Unconventional Command

### Core Principle
When operating on serious, difficult, encircled, or desperate ground, conventional command structures may fail. The leader must be willing to set aside customary practices and issue orders that break convention in order to maintain unity and commitment across the entire force.

### The Four Unconventional Command Principles
1. **Bestow rewards irrespective of customary practice**: Reward based on contribution and need, not just on rank or protocol.
2. **Issue orders irrespective of convention**: Give commands that are right for the situation, even if they break standard procedure.
3. **Command the whole army as though it were but one man**: Achieve such unity that the entire force moves as a single organism.
4. **Conceal your designs from the troops**: Do not reveal the full plan — especially when the task is dangerous.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Assess whether the ground type** (serious, difficult, encircled, desperate) requires unconventional command.
2. **Break customary reward practices** when the situation demands it — recognize extraordinary effort immediately and visibly.
3. **Issue direct, situational orders** even if they contradict standard operating procedures.
4. **Conceal the full strategic design** from the troops; reveal only what they need to know to execute their specific task.
5. **When the task is dangerous, do not tell the troops its advantageous aspect** — focus them on execution, not on potential reward.

### Examples from the Text
> "Bestow rewards irrespective of customary practice and issue orders irrespective of convention and you can command a whole army as though it were but one man."

> "Set the troops to their tasks without revealing your designs. When the task is dangerous, do not tell them its advantageous aspect."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- **Do** use unconventional command when the situation genuinely demands it — not as a routine management style.
- **Do** explain the rationale for breaking convention to senior leaders, even if you cannot explain it to the troops.
- **Do** ensure that unconventional orders still align with the organization's core values and legal boundaries.
- **Don't** break convention arbitrarily — every deviation from standard practice should serve a clear strategic purpose.
- **Don't** reveal dangerous plans to troops in a way that creates fear, hesitation, or mutiny.
- **Don't** confuse secrecy with deception — conceal designs from the troops, but do not lie to them about basic facts.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Reckless deviation**: Breaking rules and conventions without a clear strategic purpose, creating chaos rather than unity.
- **Over-secrecy**: Withholding so much information that the team cannot execute effectively or trust the leader.
- **Inconsistent rewards**: Bestowing rewards arbitrarily rather than based on genuine contribution, creating resentment.
- **Danger disclosure**: Telling troops about the lethal aspects of a mission in a way that paralyzes rather than motivates them.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Unconventional command often operates at the edge of organizational norms and sometimes legal boundaries. Ensure that all actions remain compliant with laws, regulations, and ethical standards.
- Concealing designs from your own team creates information asymmetry. If the enemy captures your troops, they may extract information through interrogation. Plan for this contingency.

## Lesson 6: Create Commitment by Controlling Escape Routes

### Core Principle
Soldiers resist when surrounded, fight hard when there is no alternative, and follow commands implicitly when they have fallen into danger. The same principle applies to teams and organizations: when escape is possible, energy leaks into exit planning; when escape is impossible, all energy goes into the fight.

### The Commitment Mechanics
| Condition | Soldier/Team Response | Energy Allocation |
|-----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Escape possible | Consider retreat | Energy split between fight and exit |
| Surround but not trapped | Resist reluctantly | Partial commitment |
| No alternative | Fight to the death | Full, unified commitment |

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Assess whether your team has too many escape routes** — are they hedging, keeping options open, or maintaining side projects that dilute focus?
2. **Identify which "boats" can be burned** — which exit options, safety nets, or fallback plans are dividing attention?
3. **Remove or deliberately constrain escape routes** to force full commitment to the primary objective.
4. **Create conditions where success is the only acceptable outcome** — not by threatening punishment, but by making the mission so compelling that retreat is unthinkable.
5. **Throw your team into positions whence there is no escape, and they will prefer death to flight** — use this principle deliberately and responsibly.

### Examples from the Text
> "For it is the nature of soldiers to resist when surrounded, to fight hard when there is no alternative, and to follow commands implicitly when they have fallen into danger."

> "Throw them into a perilous situation and they will survive; put them in desperate ground and they will live. For when the army is placed in such a situation, it can snatch victory from defeat."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- **Do** use deadline-driven commitments, public pledges, or resource allocation locks to eliminate escape routes.
- **Do** create a narrative where retreat is not just difficult but unacceptable — mission-critical, identity-defining, or values-aligned.
- **Do** ensure that when you remove escape routes, you also provide the support, resources, and confidence needed to succeed.
- **Don't** create desperate ground conditions without the capacity to win — a team with no escape and no path to victory will mutiny or collapse.
- **Don't** use this principle to trap people through coercion, threats, or exploitation.
- **Don't** remove escape routes that are necessary for safety, ethics, or legal compliance.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Accidental desperation**: Allowing a project or organization to drift into desperate ground without planning or preparation, creating panic rather than commitment.
- **False commitment**: Removing escape routes while still providing inadequate resources — the team will feel trapped, not committed.
- **Misreading readiness**: Applying desperate-ground tactics to a team that is not cohesive, disciplined, or trusting — the result is mutiny, not unity.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Deliberately creating desperate ground is a high-stakes leadership action. If the team perceives the situation as a trap rather than a challenge, trust will be destroyed.
- In organizational contexts, "burning boats" can have legal and ethical implications (e.g., mandatory overtime, non-compete clauses, resource starvation). Ensure all actions are lawful and ethical.
- The principle of desperate ground is about removing OPTIONS, not removing SUPPORT. Always pair constrained options with increased resources, training, and leadership presence.

## Lesson 7: Study the Enemy's Designs Before Acting

### Core Principle
The key to military operations — and to strategic success in any field — lies in cautiously studying the enemy's designs. Acting without understanding the enemy's true intentions, dispositions, and plans is gambling, not strategy.

### The Study Process
1. **Observe**: Gather data on the enemy's movements, communications, resource allocation, and public statements.
2. **Analyze**: Distinguish between genuine actions and deception. What is the enemy's true objective?
3. **Anticipate**: Predict the enemy's next moves based on observed patterns and stated goals.
4. **Plan**: Design your response to counter the enemy's likely actions and exploit his predictable weaknesses.
5. **Reassess**: Continuously update your understanding as new information arrives.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Establish an intelligence collection system** that provides timely, accurate information about the enemy/competitor.
2. **Create analytical frameworks** for distinguishing signal from noise — what does the enemy's behavior reveal about his true strategy?
3. **Conduct red-team exercises** to stress-test your understanding of the enemy's designs.
4. **Document your assessments** and the assumptions behind them.
5. **Update your enemy model** continuously — competitors and adversaries adapt.
6. **Brief your team** on the enemy's likely moves so that they can recognize them when they occur.

### Examples from the Text
> "Now, the key to military operations lies in cautiously studying the enemy's designs."

> "Concentrate your forces in the main direction against the enemy and from a distance of a thousand li you can kill his general. This is called the ability to achieve one's aim in an artful and ingenious manner."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- **Do** study the enemy's past behavior, stated objectives, resource constraints, and decision-making patterns.
- **Do** look for what the enemy values most — this reveals where he will defend most fiercely and where he is most vulnerable to pressure.
- **Do** maintain a current enemy assessment document that is updated regularly.
- **Don't** assume the enemy will behave as you would in the same situation — understand his unique psychology and constraints.
- **Don't** rely on a single source of intelligence about the enemy; triangulate across multiple channels.
- **Don't** confuse enemy deception with genuine intent — build methods to detect feints and bluffs.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Mirror imaging**: Assuming the enemy thinks, values, and decides the same way you do.
- **Static analysis**: Creating an enemy profile once and never updating it as conditions change.
- **Confirmation bias**: Seeking only intelligence that confirms your pre-existing beliefs about the enemy.
- **Analysis paralysis**: Studying the enemy endlessly without making a decision.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Intelligence collection on competitors must comply with laws and ethical standards. Corporate espionage, industrial sabotage, and illegal surveillance carry severe legal consequences.
- The enemy is also studying you. Maintain operational security — conceal your own designs, movements, and vulnerabilities.
- Intelligence about the enemy is a high-value asset. Protect it with the same care you would protect financial or strategic data.

## Lesson 8: Concentrate Force Against the Decisive Point

### Core Principle
Victory is achieved not by spreading force across the entire battlefield, but by concentrating overwhelming strength at the decisive point — the place where the enemy is most vulnerable and where a single blow can collapse his entire position.

### The Concentration Principle
> "Concentrate your forces in the main direction against the enemy and from a distance of a thousand li you can kill his general."

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Identify the enemy's center of gravity** — the source of his strength (a key leader, a critical asset, a vital market position, a core technology).
2. **Identify the decisive point** — the place where a concentrated blow will have maximum effect and cascade through the enemy's system.
3. **Mass your forces** at that point — bring more strength to bear than the enemy can counter.
4. **Strike suddenly and with overwhelming force** — the goal is not just to win the engagement but to collapse the enemy's entire strategy.
5. **Exploit the collapse** — once the decisive point falls, the enemy's remaining forces will be disorganized and vulnerable.

### Examples from the Text
> "Concentrate your forces in the main direction against the enemy and from a distance of a thousand li you can kill his general. This is called the ability to achieve one's aim in an artful and ingenious manner."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- **Do** identify the one or two decisive points that will determine the outcome before dispersing your forces.
- **Do** accept that concentrating force means accepting risk elsewhere — that is the price of decisive action.
- **Do** use economy of force elsewhere: hold with minimal strength so that you can mass at the decisive point.
- **Don't** spread your forces evenly across multiple fronts — this guarantees failure at all points.
- **Don't** attack the enemy's strongest point — concentrate against his weakest critical link.
- **Don't** commit to the decisive point until you have confirmed the enemy's disposition and your own superiority at that point.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Diffusion**: Trying to compete on every front simultaneously because you lack the discipline to choose.
- **Pointless attacks**: Striking at points that are not decisive — winning a battle but losing the war.
- **Premature commitment**: Committing the main force before the enemy's decisive point is confirmed.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Concentrating force at a decisive point creates a temporary vulnerability on your flanks and rear. Maintain security and reserve forces to protect against counterattack.
- Intelligence about your concentration must be protected — if the enemy learns where you are massing, he can strike your dispersed forces or reinforce the decisive point.
- The "kill the general" metaphor illustrates the power of targeting leadership and command. In business, this means targeting the decision-maker, the key relationship, or the critical asset that holds the competitor's strategy together.

## Lesson 9: Execute Decisive Action at the Right Moment

### Core Principle
Strategy is decided in council; victory is won on the ground. Once the plan is finalized, the leader must act with the suddenness of a maiden turning into a running hare — first appearing coy and yielding, then striking with overwhelming speed when the enemy gives an opening.

### The Decision-to-Action Sequence
1. **Close the passes**: Eliminate options and commit to the chosen course.
2. **Destroy the official tallies**: Remove the ability to reverse or reconsider the decision.
3. **Stop the passage of all emissaries**: Cut off outside influences that could dilute or redirect the plan.
4. **Examine the plan in council**: Finalize the strategy with the leadership team.
5. **Make final arrangements**: Ensure all forces are positioned and ready.
6. **Wait for the opening**: Appear coy, yielding, and non-threatening until the enemy reveals vulnerability.
7. **Strike like a running hare**: When the enemy gives an opening, attack with maximum speed — it will be too late for the enemy to oppose you.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Finalize the decision** in a disciplined council process — do not keep options open indefinitely.
2. **Eliminate distractions** — stop new emissaries, new proposals, and new alliance offers that could derail the plan.
3. **Prepare all forces** for execution before revealing your hand.
4. **Signal patience and non-aggression** to the enemy to lower his guard.
5. **Watch for the enemy's opening** — a mistake, a vulnerability, a moment of overconfidence.
6. **Execute immediately and violently** when the opening appears. Do not hesitate.
7. **Maintain momentum** — the strike must be so swift that the enemy cannot reorganize or respond.

### Examples from the Text
> "Therefore, on the day the decision is made to launch war, you should close the passes, destroy the official tallies, and stop the passage of all emissaries. Examine the plan closely in the temple council and make final arrangements."

> "If the enemy leaves a door open, you must rush in."

> "Seize the place the enemy values without making an appointment for battle with him."

> "At first, then, exhibit the coyness of a maiden until the enemy gives you an opening; afterward be swift as a running hare, and it will be too late for the enemy to oppose you."

### Best Practices (Do's)
- **Do** make decisions decisively and close off alternative options once the plan is set.
- **Do** use council meetings to pressure-test the plan, but once decided, execute without second-guessing.
- **Do** practice patience — wait for the enemy to make the first mistake.
- **Do** prepare forces in secret so that the enemy cannot anticipate your strike.
- **Don't** keep plans open-ended — "keeping options open" usually means "never deciding."
- **Don't** reveal your intentions to the enemy before you are ready to strike.
- **Don't** hesitate once the opening appears — speed is the essence of victory.
- **Don't** make an appointment for battle — let the enemy choose the time and place on your terms.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Analysis paralysis**: Continuing to study, plan, and gather intelligence long after the decision should have been made.
- **Premature action**: Striking before the enemy has given an opening, before forces are ready, or before the decisive point is confirmed.
- **Leaking the plan**: Allowing information about the pending strike to reach the enemy through carelessness or over-communication.
- **Hesitation at the moment**: When the opening finally appears, failing to act with sufficient speed and violence.

### Security/Safety Notes
- The decision to "close the passes and destroy the tallies" is irreversible. Ensure that the strategic assessment is thorough and the plan is sound before committing.
- Emissaries and external advisors can dilute or corrupt a sound plan. Control the flow of information into the decision-making process.
- The "coyness of a maiden" phase requires discipline — your own team may become restless and demand action. Maintain morale through confident, quiet leadership.
- Once the strike begins, there is no turning back. Ensure that all forces understand this and are prepared for total commitment.
