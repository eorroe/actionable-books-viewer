## 1. Know Yourself: Self-Assessment and Self-Improvement

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Recognize that you are perceived as three different people: who you think you are, who others think you are, and who you really are. Focus on discovering "who you really are."
2. Take a personality assessment (e.g., Myers-Briggs Type Indicator) to identify your decision-making preferences (thinker vs. feeler).
3. Determine whether you tend to base decisions on logic or personal values/emotions.
4. Identify your closest friends and use them as a "mirror image" of your own personality.
5. Document your observations by writing down what you learn about your preferences, strengths, and weaknesses.
6. Accept feedback from others through tools like 360-degree reviews to understand how you are perceived.
7. Base your arguments on the decision preference of the person you are trying to convince, not your own.
8. Determine your core values and write them down to uncover common links in your behavior.
9. Show commitment to your core values, as they establish your identity and determine your future.
10. Accept help from others who can serve as a sounding board to help you determine your true identity.
11. Think positive—focus on and take advantage of your inherent strengths while recognizing and minimizing your faults.
12. Plan self-improvement by remaining "green and growing" rather than "ripe and rotting." Commit to continuous learning.

### Examples from the Text
- Ben Franklin outlined his own list of personal values at age twenty-six.
- The author visited an organization that administered tests, interviewed him, and provided a written report of his qualifications. Over time, his actions mirrored the written report, developing greater confidence in his strengths.
- The Hawthorne Effect experiment showed that people who believe good things are happening are motivated to do better.

### Best Practices
- Do use personality tests as a starting point for self-discovery.
- Do seek feedback from trusted others to understand blind spots.
- Do write down your observations and values.
- Do maintain humility while focusing on strengths.
- Do use your closest relationships as mirrors for self-understanding.

### Common Pitfalls
- Assuming you already know yourself completely without external feedback.
- Basing arguments on your own decision preferences rather than the audience's.
- Being "ripe and rotting"—thinking you have all the answers and refusing to learn.
- Setting goals too low due to excessive humility.
- "Faking it" instead of developing genuine internal consistency.

### Security/Safety Notes
- No direct security concerns, but integrity in self-assessment is foundational to all other success principles.

## 2. Have Moral Integrity

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Develop moral clarity—be clear about what is right and what is wrong.
2. Establish your position on justice before entering any conflict or negotiation.
3. Build your arguments on logic that has a moral base, not on power, guns, or pity.
4. Maintain adherence to a moral code in all interactions.
5. Understand that lack of integrity destroys "moral influence."
6. Recognize that integrity is the bedrock of personal reputation.
7. Avoid making statements you will have to retract later.
8. Refrain from making "value judgments" during crises.
9. Tell the truth about situations, even when uncomfortable.
10. Admit uncertainties exist rather than pretending to have all the answers.

### Examples from the Text
- Benjamin Netanyahu used moral logic to defend Israel's right to exist by comparing it to Spain's historical reclamation of land from the Moors.
- Harold Denton managed the Three Mile Island crisis by following five rules: tell it like it is; admit uncertainties exist; don't make statements you'll have to retract later; act on the best estimate of a situation; refrain from "value judgments."
- President Carter praised Denton's calm, professional, reassuring voice that let the American people know they need have no fear.
- Senator Alan Simpson encapsulated: "If you have integrity, nothing else matters. If you do not have integrity, nothing else matters."

### Best Practices
- Do build your personal success on a base of integrity.
- Do ensure your communication comes from a trusted source.
- Do aim for win-win outcomes rather than Machiavellian manipulation.
- Do be humble but not to the extent that humility makes you set goals too low.
- Do lead with strength rather than focusing on weaknesses.

### Common Pitfalls
- Justifying supposedly moral positions at the expense of integrity.
- Making "inaccuracies" on resumes or in representations (1 in 10 people lie about college degrees, according to a consultant).
- Believing that successful people with doubtful integrity will not pay a price.
- Using moral positions as justification for evil actions.
- Abusing power in daily relationships.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Integrity failures can result in immediate and severe professional consequences (e.g., Notre Dame football coach forced to resign after five days due to resume lies).
- False positioning is destructive and will eventually be discovered.

## 3. Practice Active Listening

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Play back what you are hearing using paraphrasing comments such as "What I heard you say was..." or by repeating the person's statement.
2. Ask frequent, sincere questions to stay actively engaged, yield new information, and have a positive influence.
3. Acknowledge the other person's thoughts and feelings with words or body language (e.g., occasional nods).
4. Focus on the other person's needs rather than your own response.
5. Build the other person's self-esteem by smiling, being helpful, and staying positive.
6. Be aware of your own listening "derailers"—identify what prevents you from listening well to people with different values or on various topics.
7. Stay in the present; avoid drifting to future implications or making assumptions.
8. Take notes to stay focused on the conversation.
9. Use the power of silence—after asking a question, wait for the other person to respond rather than filling the conversation vacuum.
10. Prepare a list of questions in advance for important meetings rather than preparing responses.
11. Remember: people are more convinced by what they say than by what you say.

### Examples from the Text
- General Curtis LeMay was repeatedly interrupted by a young lieutenant. LeMay gently admonished the officer, who responded that listening quietly was not how LeMay got to be a general. LeMay reportedly answered, "No. But it is how I got to be a captain."
- Good salespeople know that when they ask a question, they should "shut up" and wait for an answer. If they step into the vacuum first by offering an excuse ("Perhaps you are too busy"), the other person often latches onto the excuse.
- The author advises executives preparing for an important visitor to "prepare a list of questions" rather than thinking about how they will respond.

### Best Practices
- Do paraphrase to confirm understanding.
- Do ask questions to guide the discussion and maintain control.
- Do use silence to pressure a positive response.
- Do prepare questions in advance for important conversations.
- Do be sincere in your questions to get more information flowing.

### Common Pitfalls
- Playing defense by responding to the other person's questions instead of asking your own.
- Filling silence and giving the other person an excuse for not taking action.
- Making assumptions or drifting to future implications while the other person is speaking.
- Listening only to people whose values you share.
- Forgetting the power of questions to persuade and engage.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Listening well can help you detect deception or uncover critical information in negotiations and interviews.
- Active listening builds trust, which is essential for secure relationships and information sharing.

## 4. Be Considerate of Others

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Pay attention to others' well-being and do not fatigue them.
2. Keep people in high spirits and conserve their energy.
3. Treat captives (or those in a subordinate position) with civility and care.
4. Avoid abusing power in daily relationships (hiring/firing, returning phone calls, tipping, etc.).
5. Recognize that power dynamics shift—treat others with magnanimity when you are in control to set up future winning situations when you are not.
6. Do not burn bridges—ever.
7. Care about one individual at a time when the need arises.
8. Share values to identify like-minded people who can become committed team members.
9. Recognize that relationships are mirrors of your strengths, weaknesses, values, and desires.
10. Take a sincere interest in others; if you can't be sincere, don't bother.

### Examples from the Text
- The president of a large financial group reprimanded a senior executive for making a delivery person wait 25 minutes in the lobby, noting that the waiting time reduced the delivery person's income. This incident became a corporate legend.
- Sun Tzu advised treating captives well and caring for them to achieve certain victory.
- The text notes that "it's a small world"—alienated people may rise to be peers or superiors, and you may encounter them at professional gatherings, restaurants, or school events.

### Best Practices
- Do treat others well as a building block for success that sticks.
- Do recognize that relationships are complex and span industries and communities.
- Do return phone calls and respond to people even when you have nothing to gain.
- Do follow your gut when opportunities to help arise.
- Do build relationships that reflect your best qualities back to you.

### Common Pitfalls
- Abusing power when you are in a position of control.
- Making decisions that work to your advantage at the expense of others when you have nothing to gain from being fair.
- Burning bridges by alienating people across an industry.
- Missing opportunities to help because the timing is inconvenient.
- Being insincere in your interest in others.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Burning bridges can have long-term negative consequences when former acquaintances become decision-makers who can affect your career.
- Power imbalances create ethical risks; consider how actions taken today may be perceived when power dynamics reverse.

## 5. Build Self-Confidence and Courage

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Understand that confidence is the father of courage—taking action to do the right thing.
2. Build self-confidence through training and practice so you have confidence that you can succeed or be mentally satisfied that you "gave it your best shot."
3. Balance confidence with wisdom—avoid overconfidence, which creates situations where you will be defeated by the unexpected.
4. Take calculated risks; without risk, you cannot win.
5. Recognize that trying to maintain the status quo can be the greatest risk of all.
6. Understand that courage is people taking action on something in which they believe.
7. Balance confidence with wisdom by ensuring boldness is paired with right decision-making.
8. "Kick away the ladder" behind you when you have climbed to success—embrace the risk and change required to advance.

### Examples from the Text
- Todd Beamer on September 11, 2001, uttered "Let's roll" and helped overtake hijackers, becoming a hero. He did not board the plane planning to be a hero; he simply did what he felt had to be done.
- Betty Ford demonstrated courage by going public with her alcoholism and founding the Betty Ford Center after initially seeking treatment confidentially.
- General Wavell stated: "A bold general may be lucky, but no general can be lucky unless he is bold."
- Biographer Stephen Sears wrote of General McClellan at Antietam: "so fearful of losing that he could not win."
- Field Marshal Montgomery wrote: "Many qualities go to make a leader, but two are vital—the ability to make the right decisions and the courage to act on the decisions."
- Stevie Wonder said: "When you begin thinking you are really number one, that's when you begin to go nowhere."

### Best Practices
- Do build physical and mental self-confidence through practice and preparation.
- Do take action on what you believe is right.
- Do recognize that courage often appears as "just doing what anyone else would have done."
- Do balance boldness with wisdom and right decision-making.
- Do understand that maintaining the status quo is often riskier than change.

### Common Pitfalls
- Overconfidence—being sure everything is going well makes you most vulnerable to surprise.
- Fear of losing so great that you cannot win.
- Waiting for perfect conditions before taking action.
- Believing you must profess a lifelong ambition to be courageous; courage often appears in spontaneous action.
- Confusing confidence with competence without underlying skill.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Overconfidence in security assessments or crisis management can lead to catastrophic failures.
- Courage without wisdom can result in reckless action; balance is essential.

## 6. Practice Discipline

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Understand that discipline is a friend, not an enemy. Excuses for not practicing good discipline are the real enemy.
2. Start with small things that eventually become habits.
3. Set a realistic number of goals and prioritize them.
4. Practice discipline in the morning—putting things off until later means they may never get done.
5. Embrace kaizen (continuous improvement) as a discipline for rebuilding processes.
6. Apply discipline to both professional study and physical health.
7. Use discipline to move from small challenges to bigger, more productive self-discipline.
8. Follow rules and principles consistently (e.g., Colin Powell's Seven Laws of Power).
9. Remember that discipline keeps you doing the right thing and puts you on the path to achieving your goals.

### Examples from the Text
- Henry Aaron was asked the difference between a good team and a great team; his answer was one word: "discipline."
- Tiger Woods studied videotapes of his performance after dominating the 1997 Masters and went back to his coach to rebuild his swing. He is known for being the last to leave the practice tee.
- Tom Brady, the day after being the youngest quarterback to win a Super Bowl, said: "There's so much room for improvement, I don't know where to start."
- Colin Powell's Seven Laws of Power include: Dare to be the Skunk; To Get the Real Dirt, Head for the Trenches; Share the Power; Know When to Ignore Your Advisors; Develop Selective Amnesia; Come Up for Air; Declare Victory and Quit.

### Best Practices
- Do set realistic goals and don't select more than three to five strategic initiatives at once.
- Do practice discipline consistently; without consistency, there is no discipline.
- Do view discipline as a path to greatness, not punishment.
- Do apply continuous improvement to all areas of life.
- Do be the last to leave the practice tee—continual improvement sets champions apart.

### Common Pitfalls
- Making excuses for not practicing discipline.
- Attempting too many goals at once.
- Waiting until later in the day to practice discipline—it rarely happens.
- Confusing activity with productivity; discipline means doing the right things consistently.
- Thinking discipline is an enemy rather than a friend.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Lack of discipline in security protocols (e.g., failing to update systems, ignoring training) creates vulnerabilities.
- Discipline in following procedures is often the difference between safe operations and disasters.

## 7. Unleash Creative Energy

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Understand that creativity is not mystical—it is simply people searching for alternate solutions to problems.
2. Recognize that creativity involves rational advancements arising out of what you know and your passions.
3. Develop a list of options on a blank piece of paper.
4. Construct a "Ben Franklin Balance Sheet" with "yes" and "no" columns to compare reasons and make decisions.
5. Set up structured brainstorming sessions with a small group observing simple rules (e.g., no criticism during idea generation).
6. Think "outside of the box"—look beyond your current industry, suppliers, customers, and known organizations for new opportunities.
7. Combine known elements in useful variations rather than trying to invent entirely new things.
8. Focus on the simple, mundane, and everyday with a fresh perspective.
9. Use creativity to find new strategies for personal success and problem-solving.
10. Understand that "I'll see it when I know it"—you must first know about opportunities before you can see them.

### Examples from the Text
- A small Texas restaurant company hung a simple sign saying "Drive Thru" in the 1950s, a novel approach at the time. Their business exploded and others copied the idea.
- Edward H. Land (inventor of the Polaroid camera) described creativity as "a succession of acts each dependent on the one before and suggesting the one after."
- Darwin said: "At no time am I a quick thinker or writer; whatever I have done in science has solely been by long pondering, patience, and industry."
- A brainstorming group developed over 2,000 uses for parts of a chicken.
- Bob Sutherland created the Cherry Republic, a store themed around a fictitious nation, by seeing what everybody had seen (cherries) and thinking what nobody had thought.

### Best Practices
- Do understand that creativity arises from hard work and knowing your subject.
- Do use structured tools like brainstorming and balance sheets.
- Do look outside your industry for inspiration.
- Do combine known elements in new ways.
- Do persist through long pondering and patience.

### Common Pitfalls
- Believing creativity is a mystical gift that only some people possess.
- Looking only within your own field for solutions.
- Dismissing simple or mundane ideas as uncreative.
- Expecting "lightning bolts from the sky" rather than rational advancement.
- Failing to act on creative ideas—implementation is the test for success.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Creative problem-solving can identify novel security threats and solutions.
- Innovation in security practices is necessary to stay ahead of evolving threats.

## 8. Aim for High Standards

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Understand that success is not doing what needs to be done—it is going "above and beyond" and breaking "the ordinary rules."
2. Look at the process, not just the results. Improvement in the process creates better results.
3. Identify three types of standards in every process: input standards (ingredients), operating standards (process activities), and output standards (results).
4. Work upstream on input and operating standards to achieve better output standards.
5. Strive for big changes in process that improve performance; they require more effort but produce bigger rewards.
6. Set new standards of performance and then break your own records.
7. Recognize that methodologies evolve (e.g., high jump techniques from Scissors to Fosbury Flop). Today's standard may be obsolete tomorrow.

### Examples from the Text
- The high jump evolved from the Scissors to the Western Roll to the Straddle to the Fosbury Flop. After the new methodology was introduced, no one could compete effectively with the old methodology.
- In preparing a meal, input standards determine ingredient quality, operating standards determine cooking method and temperature, and output standards determine serving temperature, taste, and appearance.
- Tiger Woods continuously rebuilds his swing to get better, even after dominating major tournaments.

### Best Practices
- Do focus on process improvement rather than just results.
- Do work upstream on inputs and operations rather than just fixing outputs.
- Do set big goals that require significant stretch.
- Do recognize that yesterday's breakthrough is today's baseline.
- Do continuously raise your own bar.

### Common Pitfalls
- Focusing only on results while ignoring the process that creates them.
- Making last-minute scrambles to improve on the day of the event—too late.
- Believing that past success guarantees future success with the same methods.
- Setting goals that don't require significant stretch.
- Failing to update standards as new methods emerge.

### Security/Safety Notes
- High standards in security and quality control prevent disasters.
- Complacency with current processes creates vulnerability to new threats.

## 9. Seek Sound Counsel

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Create a counseling staff with three key roles: a guru, a coach, and a sponsor.
2. Find a guru—a wise person not directly involved in your activities who will listen and give good positive input.
3. Find a coach—someone who knows your profession or business and can go beyond your defenses and ego to help you truly "know yourself."
4. Find a sponsor—someone at a high level in the organization who will act as your mentor.
5. Listen carefully to your advisors and keep score of the usefulness of their advice.
6. Prioritize a few advisors of high reliability over a large quantity of advisors.
7. Beware of "eloquent ignorance"—avoid advice from well-meaning people who have no knowledge of the situation.
8. In meetings, recognize that all advice is not sound advice and all voices should not be considered equal.
9. Avoid using meetings as a decision-making forum; use them for input and then make your own decision.
10. Avoid voting in small groups as it polarizes people.
11. Form small support groups of like-minded individuals for exchanging information and networking.
12. Cultivate new sponsors when original ones move on.

### Examples from the Text
- The author recalls a meeting at a consulting organization where a visitor participated actively but had little validity in his input—an example of "eloquent ignorance."
- General MacArthur had a plaque with a quote from a Roman general warning against people at every table who "lead armies into Macedonia"—great impediments to management.
- The author attended twenty-two-person board of directors sessions where much advice was given by people impressed only with the sound of their own voices.
- A young man lost all his original sponsors when the three next senior people up his chain of command left, and he soon left the organization.

### Best Practices
- Do keep counsel with advisors who know the situation.
- Do seek quality over quantity in advice.
- Do use meetings for input, not decision-making.
- Do be the kind of listener who builds the self-esteem of others.
- Do cultivate noncompetitive relationships as sounding boards.

### Common Pitfalls
- Allowing eloquent ignorance to influence decisions.
- Seeking too many advisors, leading to analysis paralysis.
- Using meetings as decision-making forums, which polarizes participants.
- Failing to replace sponsors when they leave.
- Letting ego get in the way of honest feedback from coaches.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Bad advice from ignorant but eloquent sources can lead to strategic failures.
- In crisis situations, listening to uninformed voices can worsen outcomes.

## 10. Develop Great Strategies

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Understand that strategy is doing the right thing; tactics is doing things right. Strategy comes before tactics—always.
2. Develop a vision of your future success—not just a vision statement, but a discovery of "what we will be."
3. Identify your core competencies by asking trusted advisors what you do best and what you like to do.
4. Determine applications for your core competencies—where you can be most productive.
5. Establish a clear direction and mind-picture of where you want to go.
6. Express your vision as a vivid image—write a single-session free-flow description starting with "Five years from now I will..." and then simplify into a short paragraph.
7. Develop personal strategic initiatives in key areas (career, family, renewal, health) using clear statements.
8. Prioritize the most important three to five initiatives—do not select more than this number.
9. Develop a plan of action for each initiative and be diligent in follow-through.
10. Understand that strategy determines the allocation of resources; tactics deals with the use of resources.

### Examples from the Text
- The author decided to become an expert on military strategy to business, spent time in bookstores and libraries, visited West Point and Fort Benning, and eventually addressed audiences on five continents.
- A friend who headed a consulting organization stated: "Visions are the vehicles that transport us across the boundaries of current reality to the boundless hopes of a future seemingly beyond our grasp."
- Strategic initiative examples: Career: "I will get a degree at night school that makes me eligible for promotion"; Family: "I will send our children to college"; Renewal: "I will go on an educational trip overseas for a week"; Health: "I will train and attempt to run a marathon."

### Best Practices
- Do ensure strategy is right first; then tactics can support it.
- Do limit strategic initiatives to three to five to avoid attempting too much.
- Do use the mental exercise of vision exploration to crystallize thoughts.
- Do cascade strategies from a vision of the future.
- Do recognize that excellent strategy can sustain many tactical failures, but bad strategy with good tactics is a fast route to failure.

### Common Pitfalls
- Confusing strategy with tactics.
- Starting with tactics before strategy is defined.
- Having too many strategic initiatives, spreading resources too thin.
- Believing that tactical brilliance can overcome inadequate strategy.
- Failing to prioritize strategic initiatives.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Strategic clarity prevents resource waste on tactical dead ends.
- Poor strategy allocation can leave critical vulnerabilities unaddressed.

## 11. Build a Personal Network

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Recognize that three types of knowledge are critical to success: what you know, whom you know, and who knows you.
2. Set specific contact goals rather than just attending meetings or conferences.
3. Make a list of the first twenty people you can think of who might be able to help you, regardless of comfort level or time since last contact.
4. When meeting someone new, find out their interests and promise to send something they might like.
5. Get their business card and write down the promised action, date, and place you met.
6. Keep your promise to send something and include a handwritten note for a personal touch.
7. Use the telephone strategically—when calling someone you do not know well, never leave a message. This keeps you in control of your networking.
8. Ask if the person has a moment before launching into conversation.
9. Use the Internet as a networking tool, keeping messages brief and informative.
10. When planning to visit a city, call people in your network and ask to meet them.
11. Actively work your network—the more friendships you cultivate, the more people you will know well.
12. Remember that statisticians say only four contact links separate you from anyone in the world.

### Examples from the Text
- A Finnish manager searched America for a town to build his factory and selected the author's hometown, creating an instant common bond.
- The Transition Team outplacement organization advises making a list of twenty people who might help with a job search.
- The author says he has never been able to walk past a phone in an airport without using it.
- In some cities, the author has had three breakfasts with three different groups of people, demonstrating active network cultivation.

### Best Practices
- Do have specific contact goals when networking.
- Do keep promises and follow up with handwritten notes.
- Do never leave messages when cold-calling; maintain control of the timing.
- Do actively work your network by visiting people when traveling.
- Do understand that quality contacts require a high-quantity network.

### Common Pitfalls
- Being too frugal about whom you call or the time spent developing relationships.
- Leaving messages when cold-calling, which abrogates your right to call back.
- Failing to keep promises to send information, breaking trust.
- Networking only when you need something rather than continuously investing.
- Assuming you can have a high-quality network without a high-quantity network.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Networks are critical sources of "foreknowledge" about opportunities and threats.
- Active networking provides early warning systems about changes in your industry or market.

## 12. Apply Strength Against Weakness

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Avoid the enemy's strength and strike his weakness.
2. Concentrate strength at a decisive point, not just mass numbers.
3. Look for the weakness of your opponent's strength when determining where to concentrate.
4. Obtain relative superiority through organizational strength, technological superiority, or skillful use of existing resources.
5. Focus on reinforcing what is working rather than shoring up what's not working.
6. Spend time, money, and energy on strengths, not weaknesses.
7. Select a niche where you can apply your strengths against weaknesses.
8. Ensure all resources are focused on predetermined strategic initiatives.
9. Remember: "Guns that have not fired, have not attacked, no matter how long they have been in position."

### Examples from the Text
- Peter the Great arrived at Dorpat in 1704 and found his commander firing at the town's strongest bastions. He switched artillery to the most vulnerable walls, and a breach was made for Russian troops to enter. Victory came five weeks after the siege began but only ten days after Peter arrived.
- Dan Poynter earned his reputation as a sky diving expert by jumping from an airplane over the North Pole and writing a book on the subject.
- Jim King parlayed his corporate specialty in analyzing environmental risk into a position of expertise by writing the 1,290-page Environmental Dictionary.
- The Greeks fighting in a phalanx won the Battle of Marathon against the Persians who were fighting one to one—organizational superiority.
- A military adage warns: "Guns that have not fired, have not attacked, no matter how long they have been in position."

### Best Practices
- Do concentrate at a decisive point.
- Do use organizational and technological superiority when available.
- Do select narrow niches with limited competition.
- Do reinforce strengths rather than weaknesses.
- Do focus resources on predetermined strategic initiatives.

### Common Pitfalls
- Spreading resources too thin across many areas.
- Trying to shore up weaknesses instead of leveraging strengths.
- Failing to find the decisive point of concentration.
- Attempting to compete in areas where others have overwhelming strength.
- Allowing managers to get involved in day-to-day operations instead of focusing on strategy.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Concentrating strength at decisive points maximizes defensive and offensive effectiveness.
- Focusing on known strengths reduces exposure to unforeseen threats.

## 13. Avoid the Avoidable

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Take specific action to reduce the possibility of defeat by physical conditions to devote full resources to unpredictable human elements.
2. Schedule regular training that increases professional skills or prepares for an alternate career.
3. Always arrive early for appointments to avoid starting with the negative of an excuse for being late.
4. Pay attention to both form and substance in documents—errors in spelling and language usage can detract from content.
5. Never allow your plan to fail because of quality of product or service, delivery time, or any physical element.
6. Take practical preventive measures: take an aspirin prior to long plane flights to reduce blood clot risk; carry two credit cards in different places.
7. Never send a letter on the day you write it—allow wisdom from passage of time and a second look.
8. Avoid drinking and driving.
9. Correct predictable components first, then devote resources to the unpredictable.

### Examples from the Text
- "Economy class syndrome" (blood clots on long flights) can be reduced by taking an aspirin and walking during the flight.
- Sending a letter immediately after writing it often results in regret; waiting allows wisdom from a second look.
- Carrying two credit cards in different places provides backup if one is lost.
- Errors in spelling and language usage can destroy the content of substance in presentations.

### Best Practices
- Do focus on eliminating predictable errors first.
- Do build redundancy into critical systems (e.g., two credit cards).
- Do review important documents after a time delay.
- Do prepare for physical and logistical contingencies.
- Do take preventive health measures when traveling.

### Common Pitfalls
- Assuming disasters are always unforeseeable—many are avoidable with preparation.
- Sending communications immediately without review.
- Carrying only one critical item (credit card, ID, key) in one location.
- Ignoring physical/health risks because they seem minor.
- Focusing exclusively on unpredictable human elements while neglecting fixable physical problems.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Avoiding the avoidable frees resources to handle truly unpredictable threats.
- Preventive measures (backup cards, review periods, health precautions) provide security against common failures.

## 14. Build a Strong Position

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Recognize that your personal strength is the foundation of your position.
2. Understand that others perceive your position by the way they see you—perception is reality.
3. Change yourself and cause a change in how others perceive you, but ensure actions are "for real"—false positioning is destructive.
4. Act out your part based on who you really are.
5. Develop a personal brand—define your brand identity narrowly so you can own it (e.g., "The author who writes mystery stories involving black Persian cats").
6. Discover your personal brand identity by imagining a good friend introducing you to an audience using a descriptive middle name.
7. Select a particular niche—the narrower the field, the more limited the competition.
8. Support your native expertise by developing expertise in an area that reinforces your current strengths.
9. Be an explorer—get out of town, meet new people, network, go to the source.
10. Build a reputation—experts aren't chosen, they are announced. Be your own announcer.
11. Continue getting more expertise—the quest never ends.
12. Form alliances to help secure your position. Get a coach, empower agents, and find advisors.
13. Answer the phone—you know you are arriving when people start calling you.

### Examples from the Text
- The author decided to become an expert on military strategy to business. He visited bookstores in Cambridge, Massachusetts, the Library of Congress, military libraries at West Point and Fort Benning, and eventually addressed audiences on five continents.
- He narrowed his focus to Sun Tzu and built a personal library containing every English translation of The Art of War.
- When the opportunity arose to consult internationally, he set a goal of developing expertise on service quality around the world and interviewed general managers of finest hotels.
- A personal brand example: "The author who writes mystery stories involving black Persian cats."

### Best Practices
- Do clearly define your position before reinforcing it.
- Do select a narrow niche to limit competition.
- Do be authentic—your best role is acting based on who you really are.
- Do continuously build expertise in your chosen field.
- Do use your network and alliances to strengthen your position.

### Common Pitfalls
- Attempting false positioning—trying to be someone you are not.
- Being too broad in expertise, making it hard to own a position.
- Failing to continuously invest in expertise after initial success.
- Becoming complacent when feeling most secure (greatest danger of surprise).
- Not answering the phone or engaging with those who reach out to you.

### Security/Safety Notes
- "It's an axiom that the more secure you feel about your position, the greater the danger. It is when you feel most secure that you become most vulnerable to surprise."
- False positioning destroys credibility when discovered.

## 15. Organize a Team

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Recognize that you cannot achieve the level of success as an individual that you can as a member of a team.
2. Form teams to solve problems—better results are arrived at by working together.
3. Understand that four brave men who know each other well and are sure of their reliability will attack resolutely, while four brave men who do not know each other will not dare attack a lion.
4. Spend time learning team skills and working together toward a common goal.
5. Share a common, unifying purpose that energizes and excites toward a goal.
6. Build team consistency by integrating "who we are" into your personal style.
7. Commit with your hearts, not just your heads, to the team's purpose.
8. Build mutual trust and reliability through shared experiences working toward a common goal.
9. Remember that successful teams are made up of people who have shared experiences, not those who have merely been lectured about teamwork.

### Examples from the Text
- The "f" Exercise: Individuals counting the letter "f" in a paragraph typically get answers ranging from low-twenties to mid-thirties. When working in teams of three or four, they usually get the correct answer (39) by assigning one person to count "f"s on specific lines.
- Bo Schembechler said: "You will never get the same effort from one man seeking glory as from a group of men pulling for a shared goal."
- Ardant du Picq stated: "Four brave men who do not know each other will not dare attack a lion. Four less brave, knowing each other well, sure of their reliability and consequently of mutual aid, will attack resolutely."

### Best Practices
- Do form teams for complex problems.
- Do build shared experiences to develop trust.
- Do establish a common, unifying purpose.
- Do integrate personal identity with team spirit.
- Do commit with heart and emotion, not just intellectual agreement.

### Common Pitfalls
- Believing that individual effort is more efficient than teamwork.
- Forming teams without shared experiences or trust.
- Focusing on individual glory rather than shared goals.
- Assuming that lecturing about teamwork creates actual teamwork.
- Failing to spend time building the productive interaction and mutual trust of a team.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Teams with mutual trust and reliability can respond more effectively to crises.
- Diverse perspectives in teams help identify blind spots and security vulnerabilities.

## 16. Go for a Breakthrough

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Use the normal force to engage; use the extraordinary to win.
2. Think big and focus your strategy on meaningful gains rather than small goals that don't lead to major results.
3. Plan breakthroughs rather than hoping they happen.
4. Do not renew an attack along the same line after it has once failed—trying harder is not the solution, trying smarter is.
5. Dislocate the enemy and exploit that dislocation.
6. Disrupt the mind to gain attention and mindshare for your idea, service, or product.
7. Define the challenge clearly using words or pictures.
8. Develop "How about..." statements to lead to radical and unusual ideas.
9. Visualize action—select intriguing ideas and imagine putting them into action moment by moment.
10. Diagram input from ideas and look for a new way to fulfill the challenge.
11. Understand that "just a little more" effort is not enough—incremental increases are matched by opponents, creating stalemate.
12. Merge a big responsibility with a big goal to create an energizing reason for effort.

### Examples from the Text
- General MacArthur advised Olympic athletes: "We are here to represent the greatest country on earth. We did not come here to lose and lose gracefully, we came to win and win decisively."
- The author sold a product licensed with the Pink Panther brand by giving away six-foot-high stuffed Pink Panthers as premiums. The sheer size was disruptive, and the intrusion was achieved by appealing to the desire to own this unique item.
- Carl von Clausewitz stated: "Only great battles produce great results." The corollary: "Only the prospect of great results can produce the means to fight great battles."
- Dick Fosbury innovated the Fosbury Flop in the high jump by radically changing how he went over the bar.
- Dell sold computers direct to customers, ignoring traditional distribution channels.

### Best Practices
- Do aim for decisive, breakthrough results rather than incremental improvements.
- Do use disruption to gain mindshare, but ensure it is in good taste.
- Do plan breakthroughs; do not wait for them to happen.
- Do visualize action and moment-by-moment execution.
- Do combine responsibility with big goals to generate enthusiasm.

### Common Pitfalls
- Confusing small goals with steps to larger goals—small goals that don't lead to major results cause loss of interest.
- Renewing attacks along the same line after failure.
- Believing that "just a little more" effort will create a breakthrough—it often creates stalemate.
- Using crass or obnoxious disruption that alienates rather than engages.
- Failing to merge big responsibility with big goals, missing the energizing effect.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Breakthrough thinking can identify novel competitive advantages and defensive strategies.
- Disruptive innovation can change market dynamics and create new security paradigms.

## 17. Know Your Battleground (Research and Knowledge)

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Thoroughly study the subject of your endeavor—knowledge is mandatory.
2. Analyze the enemy's battle plan to understand strong and weak points.
3. Agitate the enemy (or situation) to ascertain patterns of movement.
4. Learn your subject well enough to teach it—this is a simple standard for mastery.
5. Ask "why" five times to get to the root cause of problems.
6. Break your subject into pieces (segmentation) to understand components and how they meld into the whole.
7. Study information close to the time of use to increase retention.
8. Have a networking mindset—develop a network of bright people who can give you information important to your success.
9. Take advantage of learning opportunities (trade shows, industry meetings, volunteer organizations).
10. Spend time with people with different interests to gain new insights.
11. Seek new experiences—don't get stuck in a rut.
12. Learn new questions through observing and wandering around.
13. Compare the five constant factors: moral influence, weather, terrain, commander, and doctrine.

### Examples from the Text
- Sun Tzu said: "War is a matter of vital importance to the state; a matter of life and death, the road either to survival or to ruin. Hence, it is imperative that it be thoroughly studied."
- The "five why's" example: A late delivery was traced not to the driver but to a budget cut that canceled training—five levels deep.
- The author visited four of the five largest grocery retailers during a consulting assignment to gain on-site knowledge, enabling him to know the retail store component better than anyone else.
- Mark Twain said: "I have never let my schooling interfere with my education."

### Best Practices
- Do study subjects thoroughly and close to time of use.
- Do break complex subjects into manageable pieces.
- Do ask "why" five times to reach root causes.
- Do network with bright people inside and outside your field.
- Do seek new experiences and perspectives.

### Common Pitfalls
- Limiting knowledge to a narrow focus.
- Accepting surface-level explanations without drilling to root causes.
- Failing to study information close to time of use, reducing retention.
- Only spending time with people who know the same things you do.
- Using lack of time as an excuse to avoid learning opportunities.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Thorough knowledge of the battleground (market, industry, threat landscape) is essential for strategic security.
- Root cause analysis prevents recurring vulnerabilities and failures.

## 18. Win Without Fighting

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Formulate strategy so well that you avoid battle by intimidating opponents.
2. Be so fundamentally sound and well-prepared that opponents choose to expend their energy elsewhere.
3. Innovate to find new ways to victory without encountering competition.
4. Define the challenge clearly using words or pictures.
5. Develop "How about..." statements to lead to radical and unusual ideas.
6. Visualize action—select intriguing ideas and imagine putting them into action moment by moment.
7. Diagram input from ideas and look for a new way to fulfill the challenge.
8. Recognize that victory before battle is the supreme excellence.
9. Understand that easy wins are not victories—the real victories are where you "conquer an enemy already defeated" by strategy.
10. Use innovation to declare victory before encountering competition.

### Examples from the Text
- Switzerland wins many wars without fighting using the "Porcupine Strategy"—rolling up into a ball and brandishing quills. The Swiss army is composed of civilians who train regularly and keep weapons at home. Switzerland does not have an army; Switzerland is an army.
- Phillip Fulmer coaches his players to be "fundamentally sound and in such good physical condition that they can outlast and outwork their opponents."
- Dick Fosbury innovated the Fosbury Flop.
- Dell sold computers direct to customers, ignoring traditional distribution channels.
- Bob Sutherland created the Cherry Republic by seeing what everybody had seen (cherries) and thinking what nobody had thought.

### Best Practices
- Do aim to subdue the enemy without fighting.
- Do innovate to find new paths to victory.
- Do be so well-prepared that opponents avoid confrontation.
- Do define challenges clearly before seeking solutions.
- Do use "How about..." statements to generate radical ideas.

### Common Pitfalls
- Engaging in battle before establishing strategic superiority.
- Failing to innovate and instead relying on conventional approaches.
- Believing that fierce fighting and public acclaim constitute true victory.
- Confusing incremental improvement with strategic breakthrough.
- Seeking battles to prove strength rather than avoiding them through preparation.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Winning without fighting preserves resources and avoids casualties.
- Strategic innovation can create defensible positions that deter competitors and threats.

## 19. Be Courageous

*(Consolidated with Section 5 above for completeness)*

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Understand that everything involves risk, and there is also risk in doing nothing.
2. Take action to do the right thing—this is courage.
3. Build self-confidence through training and practice.
4. Balance confidence with wisdom; avoid overconfidence.
5. Take calculated risks; without risk, you cannot win.
6. Recognize that courage is people taking action on something in which they believe.
7. Kick away the ladder behind you when you have climbed to success—embrace the risk of change.

### Examples from the Text
- Todd Beamer's "Let's roll" on Flight 93.
- Betty Ford going public with alcoholism.
- General Wavell: "A bold general may be lucky, but no general can be lucky unless he is bold."
- Field Marshal Montgomery: ability to make right decisions and courage to act on them are vital leadership qualities.

### Best Practices
- Do take action based on values.
- Do balance boldness with wisdom.
- Do recognize that courage often appears as ordinary action in extraordinary circumstances.
- Do maintain training and preparation to build confidence.

### Common Pitfalls
- Overconfidence leading to surprise defeat.
- Inaction due to fear of risk.
- Forgetting that maintaining status quo is often the greatest risk.
- Confusing recklessness with courage.

## Summary of Key Themes from the Preface

The "Preface to Book Two" establishes five major sections for applying Sun Tzu's wisdom:

1. **Personal Characteristics for Success** — Knowing yourself, moral integrity, listening, consideration, courage, discipline, creativity, high standards, and sound counsel.
2. **Strategies for Success** — Knowing your battleground, building networks, developing great strategies, and winning without fighting.
3. **Tactics for Success** — Applying strength against weakness, avoiding the avoidable, and building strong positions.
4. **Success in Competitive Situations** — Organizing teams and going for breakthroughs.
5. **Examples of Personal Success** — Learning from those who have applied the Master's strategies.

The foundation of all success, according to this chapter, is **self-knowledge** and **moral integrity**. From these flow the practical disciplines of listening, networking, strategy development, and courageous action.

**Final admonition from the text:** "Be who you are. Be enlightened—know who you are. Self-knowledge is the foundation of success."
