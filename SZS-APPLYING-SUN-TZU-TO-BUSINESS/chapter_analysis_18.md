# Chapter Analysis: "Section Three: Tactics for Success"
## Sun Tzu for Success — Pages 127–153

---

## Chapter Overview

Section Three of *Sun Tzu for Success* bridges strategic planning and tactical execution, demonstrating how the master plan becomes increasingly focused as action cascades through an organization. The section teaches that strategy at one level is tactics at the next, and that separating planning from implementation separates thinking from doing. Seven subsections break down the essential tactics for achieving success: taking the offensive, moving rapidly, gaining momentum, seizing opportunities, persisting, occupying the high ground, and building on success.

**Source text:** Pages 127–153 of *Sun Tzu for Success*

## Actionable Lessons

---

### 1. Coordinate Tactics with Strategy

**Core Principle:** Strategy at one level becomes tactics at the next. Separating planning from implementation separates thinking from doing and diffuses responsibility.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Align tactical actions with the overarching strategy at every level of the hierarchy.
2. Weave planning and implementation together rather than treating them as separate phases.
3. Ensure that responsibility for outcomes is not diffused across disconnected teams.
4. Select the right people and exploit the situation by matching tactics to strategic objectives.

**Example from the Text:**
Francisco Pizarro coordinated strategy with tactics when he conquered the Inca Empire with four dozen horses and a few hundred men:
- **Strategy:** Establish a method of communication. **Tactic:** Got an interpreter (Felipillo).
- **Strategy:** Communicate effectively. **Tactic:** Through Felipillo, delivered winning speeches to the Indians.
- **Strategy:** Implement activities to support communication. **Tactic:** Exchanged trinkets for gold and food, smiled, ordered his men to "Touch nothing, respect the inhabitants."
- **Strategy:** Play off one tribe against another and fuel civil war. **Tactic:** Attacked the Incas' enemies so they wouldn't see the Spaniards as enemies.
- **Strategy:** Select the place and time for the decisive battle. **Tactic:** Chose the Battle of Cajamarca as the "Waterloo."

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Weave planning and implementation together so thinking and doing are inseparable.
- **Do:** Match tactical actions to strategic objectives at every level.
- **Don't:** Allow strategy to exist only in planning documents while tactics operate independently.
- **Don't:** Diffuse responsibility by separating planning from implementation teams.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Strategic plans that are never translated into actionable tactics.
- Tactical actions that contradict or undermine strategic objectives.
- Teams that optimize locally at the expense of organizational goals.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In security operations, strategy (risk management policy) must translate into tactics (incident response, access controls). Gaps between the two create exploitable vulnerabilities.

### 2. Initiate Action by Taking the Offensive

**Core Principle:** The possibility of victory lies in the attack. Being on the offensive puts you in control and forces the foe to react rather than act.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Seize, retain, and exploit the initiative in pursuit of your goal.
2. Choose a mental approach to launching your offensive:
   - Plan everything in detail, then get going (but don't let preparation become procrastination).
   - Determine general objectives, then get going (fill in details as momentum builds).
3. Make the mental leap at gut level; mental action must precede physical action.
4. Recognize that your greatest odds for success lie on the side of action, not analysis.

**Example from the Text:**
> "The first blow is as much as two." — Ancient proverb cited in the text.

A dean of a prestigious law school noted that training to "look at both sides of the situation" is not good training for business; people are better off deciding to do something rather than spending excessive time analyzing.

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Act on the offensive to preserve freedom of action.
- **Do:** Accept that not making a decision is itself a decision.
- **Don't:** Let preparation become an excuse for procrastination.
- **Don't:** Let opponents dictate the timing and direction of your actions.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Analysis paralysis — waiting for perfect information before acting.
- Over-preparation that delays decisive action.
- Reacting to competitors instead of setting the agenda.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In competitive intelligence and market positioning, being on the offensive means shaping the narrative before adversaries define it. Delay allows opponents to establish facts on the ground.

### 3. Keep on the Move (Avoid Inactivity)

**Core Principle:** Inactivity causes more loss of opportunity than mistakes in the choice of methodology. Field-generated initiatives outperform office-generated ones.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Prioritize field-generated initiatives over office-generated ones.
2. Visit your market and customers directly rather than relying on filtered reports.
3. Get out from behind the desk and generate activity in the field.
4. Recognize that being in the office for a week generates enough activity to keep you in the office another week — break that cycle.

**Example from the Text:**
A Fortune 500 company president spent weeks managing from his desk, making decisions based on historical experience. When he did visit customers, he returned with new ideas — but his new actions were often misguided because they were based on conversations with only a few customers in the same market.

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Spend time "where the action is" to get unfiltered, timely information.
- **Do:** Generate field activity rather than relying on secondhand reports.
- **Don't:** Let bureaucracy filter information before it reaches you.
- **Don't:** Assume office-generated initiatives have the same value as field-generated ones.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Management by desk: relying on reports instead of direct observation.
- Acting on stale information filtered through organizational layers.
- Mistaking activity for productivity.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- Physical security assessments must involve on-site inspections, not just remote monitoring. In cybersecurity, threat intelligence must be gathered from the field (network sensors, incident reports) rather than relying solely on theoretical models.

### 4. Develop a Spirit of the Offensive

**Core Principle:** An offensive spirit must permeate everything you do. Opportunity rarely comes to those who wait; it is found outside the office.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Schedule face time with people who are on the route to your goals.
2. Position yourself where action happens, not where it is reported.
3. Get timely information before it is filtered through bureaucracy.
4. Make decisions a matter of course by spending time in the field.

**Example from the Text:**
> "I've seldom found opportunity calling on me; instead, I've found opportunity outside the office. Even more important is the fact that being on the offensive visiting my market provides me with the timely information that keeps me on the cutting edge. When I've waited for information to be filtered through the bureaucracy, the offensive has been launched too late."

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Build relationships with people on your path to success before you need them.
- **Do:** Be proactive in seeking information and opportunities.
- **Don't:** Wait for opportunity to come to you.
- **Don't:** Allow bureaucracy to delay your response to emerging situations.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Reactive posture: waiting for problems to arise before acting.
- Isolation from the field: missing early signals of change.
- Complacency: believing past success guarantees future opportunity.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In threat hunting and competitive intelligence, the offensive posture means actively seeking signals before incidents occur. Waiting for alerts after the fact is defensive and reactive.

### 5. Consider Alternative Routes to Your Goal

**Core Principle:** Sun Tzu says, "The army must know the five different attack situations and wait for appropriate times." The best route depends on your relative strength and the situation.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Head-on attack:** Use only if you have overwhelming strength; otherwise, avoid.
2. **Indirect approach:** Instead of telling someone what to do, present facts, ask questions, and let the other person suggest a solution. Help them think the solution is their idea.
3. **Guerrilla approach:** Carefully select a point of attack where you can have an advantage. Win with a series of minor coups that tip the scales.

**Example from the Text:**
Fundraising organizations schedule events to raise money instead of directly asking for donations — an indirect approach that engages supporters without pressure.

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Assess your relative strength before choosing an approach.
- **Do:** Use the indirect approach when building consensus or influencing stakeholders.
- **Do:** Use guerrilla tactics when resources are limited and you need incremental advantages.
- **Don't:** Use a head-on attack without overwhelming strength.
- **Don't:** Assume one approach works in all situations.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Confronting competitors directly when a flanking maneuver would be more effective.
- Using direct commands where collaboration would yield better results.
- Underestimating the cumulative power of small, strategic wins.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In penetration testing and red team exercises, guerrilla tactics (small, incremental probes) are often more effective than a single brute-force attack. Indirect social engineering may bypass technical controls more efficiently than direct exploits.

### 6. Heed the Rules for a Successful Offensive

**Core Principle:** Seven foundational rules sustain a successful offensive over time.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Be Professional:** Understand and apply scientific knowledge in your area of expertise. Knowledge is the heartbeat of success.
2. **Circulate to Percolate:** Network often and with people who have different interests.
3. **Be Disciplined:** Plan your work and work your plan.
4. **Be a Difference Maker:** Focus on making a positive difference; cause good things to happen.
5. **Keep on the Leading Edge:** Use the newest technology (e.g., digital video cameras to show visual images that excite people).
6. **Extend Every Contact:** Send handwritten thank-you notes; follow up with copies of articles that interest the other person.
7. **Be Action-Oriented:** Develop ongoing action plans and involve others in the planning process.

**Example from the Text:**
The text cites Levinson's *The Exceptional Executive*: "A professional is a person who can understand and apply scientific knowledge. Given knowledge, the professional can choose a course of action; he remains in charge of himself and his work."

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Treat knowledge as the heartbeat of success in your area of expertise.
- **Do:** Network broadly across disciplines and industries.
- **Do:** Document and share action plans with stakeholders.
- **Don't:** Rely on charisma or connections alone without substantive expertise.
- **Don't:** Let relationships lapse without follow-up.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Complacency after achieving professional status.
- Networking only within familiar circles.
- Allowing action plans to become shelfware.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In cybersecurity leadership, professionals must continuously update knowledge as threats evolve. Handwritten follow-up notes (or their digital equivalent) maintain relationship security with stakeholders across the organization.

### 7. Move Rapidly to Achieve Speed Advantage

**Core Principle:** Speed is of the essence in war and winning. A partially developed plan violently executed is superior to being late with a perfect plan.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Reduce the time required for initiating action; aim for a 50% reduction if possible.
2. Avoid paralysis by analysis — Napoleon said two-thirds of decision-making is based on analysis and one-third is a leap in the dark.
3. Recognize that not making a decision is itself a decision — and often a poor one.
4. Act before opponents are ready and before opportunities disappear.
5. Remember: "Even if you are on the right track, you'll get run over if you just sit there." — Will Rogers

**Example from the Text:**
Napoleon's troops marched at 120 paces per minute while his opponents marched at the more orthodox 70 paces — a fundamental advantage that contributed to his success. The Mongol hordes' speed invariably gave them superiority of force at the decisive point.

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Accept that you will never have enough data; winners get going and fight through.
- **Do:** Set timetables for action to create urgency.
- **Do:** Make gut-level decisions when time is critical.
- **Don't:** Let the search for "more" data delay execution.
- **Don't:** Allow opportunities to disappear while waiting for perfect conditions.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Waiting for perfect information before acting.
- Mistaking thoroughness for effectiveness.
- Letting competitors seize the initiative while you deliberate.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In incident response, speed of detection and containment is critical. A partially developed containment executed immediately often prevents more damage than a perfect plan executed hours later.

### 8. Solve Differences Rapidly

**Core Principle:** Every disagreement has two dimensions: time and depth. The longer the disagreement, the deeper the resulting emotional depth.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Get started solving the problem immediately — do not let disputes fester.
2. Recognize that delayed decisions inevitably lose their positive quality.
3. Understand that hatred grows over time; "the more I thought about it, the madder I got" is a common pattern.
4. Advise all parties to "get over it and go on with your life."

**Example from the Text:**
The historic feud between the Hatfields and McCoys is cited as a classic example of carrying an altercation to the extreme.

Sun Tzu advises:
> "While we have heard of stupid haste in war, we have not yet seen a clever operation that was prolonged. There has never been a case in which a prolonged war has benefited a country."

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Address conflicts at the earliest possible moment.
- **Do:** Focus on resolution, not winning the argument.
- **Don't:** Let personal disputes deepen over time.
- **Don't:** Prolong disagreements in hopes of achieving a better outcome.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Allowing grievances to simmer and grow.
- Using time as a weapon in negotiations, which destroys relationships.
- Letting ego override the objective of resolution.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In workplace conflict resolution, prolonged disputes can lead to security risks (insider threats, sabotage). Rapid mediation prevents escalation. In international diplomacy, prolonged conflicts create conditions for radicalization and instability.

### 9. Gain Momentum Through Rapid Decision and Execution

**Core Principle:** Success requires not only the initial speed of attack but also continuation of the initiative — momentum. Decision time is the enemy.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Recognize that any positive action or degree of success generates positive emotions and protects you from falling.
2. Shorten decision time to accelerate communication and execution.
3. Coordinate all actions into simultaneous thrusts when swiftness is at a maximum.
4. Keep on the move — the pursuit is as important as the attack.

**Example from the Text:**
> "When torrential water tosses boulders, it is because of momentum. The energy is similar to a fully drawn crossbow. The momentum of one skilled in war is overwhelming."

The text notes: "When you wait too long, your opponents have had time to prepare and your friends have lost patience."

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Make decisions rapidly and communicate them immediately.
- **Do:** Coordinate actions into simultaneous thrusts for maximum impact.
- **Do:** Treat momentum as a strategic asset that preserves freedom of action.
- **Don't:** Let decisions linger until their value is lost.
- **Don't:** Allow opponents time to prepare countermeasures.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Delayed decisions that lose their positive quality.
- Failure to coordinate actions, resulting in sequential rather than simultaneous impact.
- Letting momentum stall by pausing to celebrate intermediate wins.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In crisis management, momentum determines whether an organization controls the narrative or reacts to it. Rapid, coordinated communication preserves stakeholder confidence.

### 10. Get Leverage by Giving Leverage

**Core Principle:** The first rule of good leveraging is that you must give leverage in order to get leverage. One-sided leverage destroys relationships.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Identify where you can give leverage before seeking it in return.
2. Choose opportunities that support your personal and professional objectives.
3. Volunteer in organizations to gain knowledge, contacts, and influence.
4. Raise your hand and volunteer — that is how you become a leader in any organization.
5. Leverage every relationship for mutual benefit, not one-sided gain.

**Example from the Text:**
Airline frequent-flyer programs succeed because they give customers an opportunity to leverage their relationship — the more the airline gets what they want (the flier on their routes), the more benefits the flier accrues.

David Thiel, founder of Auragen Communications, participates in charitable organizations like the United Way to aid in pursuing political interests. By helping his community, he leverages his time to gain knowledge and contacts that advance business and personal goals.

When the new director of a hospital stood at the employee entrance on the first day to meet everyone and shake their hand, he established the first link in communications. A video camera during this event helped him learn names and faces, signaling the beginning of building relationships he needed to leverage his leadership position.

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Give before you get; create value for others before seeking favors.
- **Do:** Volunteer for leadership roles in organizations aligned with your goals.
- **Do:** Use every relationship as a two-way street.
- **Don't:** Extract value without contributing in return.
- **Don't:** Assume leadership without serving first.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Transactional relationships that focus only on what you can extract.
- Joining organizations without a clear objective, wasting limited time resources.
- Failing to follow up after initial contact.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In corporate and cybersecurity leadership, influence is built on trust and reciprocity. One-sided demands for compliance without relationship-building lead to workarounds and shadow IT.

### 11. Develop an Opportunity List

**Core Principle:** The opportunities we lose are often due to circumstances beyond our control. We need ten sure things for one to work.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Maintain a running list of at least ten prospective opportunities.
2. When one opportunity fails, move to the next one on your list rather than becoming discouraged.
3. Continuously search for new opportunities to replace those that are lost.
4. Use the list as evidence that alternatives exist, keeping you on the offensive.

**Example from the Text:**
A business partner, after losing a sale, observed: "The opportunities we lose are often due to circumstances beyond our control like loss of funding or the buyer changing jobs. It seems that we need ten sure things for one to work."

The author has maintained a "ten sure things" list ever since. Sometimes he does not have ten prospective opportunities, but the search for additional opportunities keeps him on the offensive and prevents discouragement.

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Keep your opportunity list current and actively replenish it.
- **Do:** Use setbacks as prompts to add new alternatives rather than retreat.
- **Don't:** Put all your focus on a single opportunity.
- **Don't:** Allow one loss to demoralize you when alternatives exist.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Over-reliance on a single sales prospect, deal, or relationship.
- Discouragement after a single loss, leading to inaction.
- Failing to replenish the pipeline after wins.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In business development and sales, pipeline diversification is analogous to risk diversification. A single point of failure (one client, one deal) creates existential vulnerability.

### 12. Do It Now — Set Timetables for Action

**Core Principle:** Timing is of great importance. The problem is often not that time spans are short, but that things that can be done either this year or next never get done.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Set a lifelong goal and timetable for accomplishment.
2. Establish clear deadlines for opportunities that could otherwise slide indefinitely.
3. Convert "someday" intentions into scheduled actions.
4. Recognize that opportunities appear and disappear in careers, relationships, and all endeavors.

**Example from the Text:**
> "It's things that can be done either this year or next that never get done."

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Create explicit timetables for action.
- **Do:** Treat deadlines as commitments to yourself and others.
- **Don't:** Allow tasks to drift from "this year" to "next year" indefinitely.
- **Don't:** Mistake planning for action.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Chronic postponement of non-urgent but important tasks.
- Mistaking busywork for progress toward goals.
- Assuming there will always be more time.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In compliance and risk management, delays in implementing controls create windows of exposure. Regulatory deadlines are not suggestions; missed timelines create legal and operational risk.

### 13. Choose the Right Implementation Methodology

**Core Principle:** All implementation is goal-oriented. The process you choose must fit the situation — sequential, cumulative, or simultaneous.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Sequential Implementation:** Use for step-by-step improvement where each success sets the stage for the next. Ideal for skill-building and phased rollouts.
   - Example: MacArthur's island-hopping campaign, athlete training for an event.
2. **Cumulative Implementation:** Use for multi-pronged approaches with unconnected activities that eventually reach critical mass. Ideal for broad capability-building.
   - Example: American submarine force attacks in the Pacific, an exercise program combining diet and multiple exercise regimes.
3. **Simultaneous Implementation:** Use for mass attack across a wide front in a limited time span. Ideal when speed is critical.
   - Example: German blitzkrieg across the Low Countries, a phone blitz to get job interviews, direct mail and phone blitzes before election day.

**Example from the Text:**
A cumulative job search involves inquiries at a trade show, sending out resumes, checking with friends, and calling headhunters — testing the waters to see what's available.

A manager trying to build a stronger organization might use a cumulative approach (continually looking for good people), while an athlete training for an event uses sequential implementation (step-by-step improvement).

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Match the implementation methodology to your specific goal and timeline.
- **Do:** Recognize that your career path is a series of sequential steps, each making the next possible.
- **Don't:** Use a sequential approach when simultaneous action is required.
- **Don't:** Use a blitz approach when sustained, cumulative effort is needed.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Applying the wrong methodology to a situation (e.g., sequential job hunt: send one resume and wait for a response before sending the next).
- Failing to recognize when a cumulative approach has reached critical mass.
- Burning out teams with simultaneous blitzes when sustained effort is needed.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In incident response, a simultaneous blitz (activating all response teams at once) may be necessary for a major breach, while a sequential approach works for minor issues. Misjudging severity and applying the wrong methodology can worsen outcomes.

### 14. Be Persistent — Drive Good Ideas Into Practice

**Core Principle:** Good ideas are not adopted automatically. They must be driven into practice with courageous patience.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. After deciding on a course of action, stick to it — consistency of action gives your plan strength.
2. When knocked down, get up.
3. When your ideas fall flat, get up.
4. When you are dead wrong, get up.
5. Storm the next barricade.

**Example from the Text:**
Admiral Rickover found that getting backing for a nuclear submarine was almost as difficult as designing and building it. Sports coaches state simply: "When you are knocked down, get up!"

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Treat setbacks as temporary, not terminal.
- **Do:** Maintain courage and patience when driving ideas into practice.
- **Do:** Recognize that persistence is a choice, not a trait.
- **Don't:** Abandon a course of action at the first sign of resistance.
- **Don't:** Let one failure define your trajectory.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Mistaking early failure for evidence that the idea is wrong.
- Confusing persistence with stubbornness — know when to pivot.
- Letting criticism derail implementation before it gains traction.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In cybersecurity program building, persistent advocacy is often required to secure budget and executive support. Rickover's experience building the nuclear submarine program demonstrates that paradigm shifts require years of relentless push against institutional inertia.

### 15. Establish a Mental High Ground

**Core Principle:** In any endeavor, the high ground is both mental and physical. The foundation of your mental high ground is moral beliefs, personal integrity, and motivation toward success.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Build moral beliefs:** Establish a belief in the rightness of your position. The battle for the moral high ground is waged in minds — emotions play a powerful role in determining courses of action.
2. **Maintain personal integrity:** Take positions but remain open-minded. Recognize that truths change and facts can be quicksand. Finding "the best" is a continuous search.
3. **Cultivate motivation:** Know where you are going. As Peter Drucker said, whenever anything is being accomplished, it is being done by "a monomaniac with a mission."
4. **Use psychocybernetics:** Steering the mind toward goals produces stability — like a bicycle that achieves stability by pedaling, not by standing still. Being motivated toward success is what keeps us successful.

**Example from the Text:**
Management expert Peter Drucker: "Whenever anything is being accomplished, it is being done, I have learned, by a monomaniac with a mission."

Maxwell Maltz's *Psychocybernetics* discovered that people who are goal-oriented and moving toward that goal have more stability, just as a person on a bicycle achieves stability by pedaling — and has none while standing still.

When interviewing candidates, the author asks: "What are the last five books you have read?" Looking for evidence of a thirst for self-improvement. When asking salespeople "How did you learn how to sell?" one candidate replied, "I'm a natural-born salesman." The author's response: "We are all 'natural-born' and everything else is learned. I want to work with people who are willing to learn how to get better."

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Read continuously to maintain mental sharpness and motivation.
- **Do:** Set clear goals and maintain relentless focus.
- **Do:** Take positions while remaining open to new evidence.
- **Don't:** Claim to be a "natural" at anything; treat all skills as learnable.
- **Don't:** Stand still mentally — stability comes from movement toward goals.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Assuming talent is innate rather than developed.
- Rigidity in beliefs that prevents adaptation.
- Lack of direction leading to lack of motivation.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In organizational leadership, moral authority is a force multiplier. Leaders who lose moral high ground (through dishonesty, hypocrisy, or inconsistency) lose the trust that makes influence possible.

### 16. Secure Physical High Ground

**Core Principle:** Without being able to see ourselves progressing toward or reaching the high ground, we lose confidence in our compass. Physical high ground supports mental high ground, and vice versa.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Improve position strength:** Continuously improve your position in all areas (nuclear family, religious community, career, interests). Position is a source of identity and reward.
2. **Protect career security:** When leaving a company, do not burn bridges. Consider exit interviewers as potential future employers. Politely decline competing offers by stating loyalty to your new commitment, but leave the door open.
3. **Build physical fitness:** Discipline is the primary ingredient of any exercise program. Get a schedule and find the right types of exercise that work for your body.
4. **Train smarter, not just harder:** Sport-specific training means working the right muscles and training in the right movement patterns to stimulate the neuromuscular systems needed to excel.

**Example from the Text:**
A friend whose company was acquired received an excellent job offer and accepted it. When counseled not to burn bridges, he declined to interview with another division of his old company the next day, stating he had already accepted an offer and could consider other opportunities only after spending time in his new position. This strengthened his career security — he is now in the "catbird seat" with a great new job and potential future opportunities with his previous employer.

Bobsled racers practice sprinting in a shuffling style to learn how to impart take-off speed while keeping feet on the ice. Cross-country skiers work on upper body strength because upper body muscles are important for propelling racers forward. Luge racers work on torso strength, control, and balance. Winners train smarter, not just harder.

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** Continuously improve your position in all areas of life.
- **Do:** Orchestrate career transitions carefully to strengthen long-term security.
- **Do:** Follow a disciplined exercise schedule tailored to your body.
- **Don't:** Burn bridges when leaving an employer.
- **Don't:** Accept counter-offers that undermine your integrity or new commitments.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Burning professional relationships during exit.
- Treating all exercise the same regardless of goal.
- Focusing on mental high ground while neglecting physical health.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- Physical security (access controls, facility design) and cybersecurity are complementary components of organizational high ground. A breach in either undermines the other.

### 17. Build on Your Success — Consolidate Achievements

**Core Principle:** The greatest cause of defeat is victory. Success breeds complacency — we tend to stop doing the things that made us successful. Consolidating achievements means using current strengths as weapons to achieve the next level.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Take inventory of your personal strengths and resources.
2. Determine how to use these "weapons" to achieve the next level of success.
3. Project your vision to the end — what do you want to accomplish long term?
4. Transition from short-term success to long-term success deliberately.
5. Put a plan of action in writing; mental exercise alone is insufficient.

**Example from the Text:**
95 percent of people who lose weight gain it all back — and often more. They won the battle but became complacent and lost the war.

Sports teams that vanquish a very tough opponent often suffer defeat in the next game against a less able opponent because the exhilaration of winning dulls their fighting edge.

Argentina was a rich nation a century ago thanks to cattle and beef production, but that prosperity bred a social-welfare system that eventually brought it to financial ruin. The oil-rich states of the Middle East have never weaned themselves from dependence on oil income. Later generations of wealthy families often experience similar decline.

Business acquisitions can be a victory that leads to defeat because different cultures fail to mesh.

**Best Practices:**
- **Do:** After every win, immediately plan how to leverage that success into the next level.
- **Do:** Treat success as a springboard, not a destination.
- **Do:** Write down your consolidation plan — make it concrete.
- **Don't:** Assume past success guarantees future success.
- **Don't:** Let the exhilaration of victory dull your fighting edge.
- **Don't:** Build systems that depend on a single resource (the "curse of resources").

**Common Pitfalls:**
- Complacency after a win — stopping the behaviors that produced success.
- Treating consolidation as a mental exercise rather than an action plan.
- Dependency on a single resource, relationship, or market.

**Security/Safety Notes:**
- In information security, a "success" (passing an audit, surviving a penetration test) can create complacency that opens the door to motivated opponents. Continuous reinforcement of security practices is essential.
