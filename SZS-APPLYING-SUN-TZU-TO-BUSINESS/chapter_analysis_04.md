# Chapter Three: Attack by Strategem — Actionable Lessons Analysis

**Source:** *Sun Tzu for Success* — Chapter Three (pages 9–11)  
**Section Highlighted:** "Seek Circumstances Which Assure Victory" (page 11)  
**Date:** 2026-09-08

---

## Overview

Chapter Three teaches that the supreme excellence in competition (war or business) is to achieve objectives **without fighting** — through strategic superiority, preparation, and intelligence rather than brute force. The chapter culminates in the famous principle: *"Know the enemy and know yourself, and you can fight a hundred battles with no danger of defeat."*

---

## Actionable Lesson 1: Prioritize Winning Without Fighting

### Core Principle
The best outcomes are achieved by subduing competition intact rather than through destructive conflict. In business, this means capturing market position, partnerships, or deals through strategy, negotiation, and positioning — not through price wars, litigation, or aggressive takedowns.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Map the full spectrum of engagement options** before entering any competitive situation:
   - Best: Attack the competitor's strategy (e.g., render their business model obsolete through innovation).
   - Second: Disrupt alliances (e.g., poach key partners, suppliers, or distribution channels).
   - Third: Attack their operational capabilities in the field (e.g., out-execute them in their core market).
   - Last resort: Direct confrontation (e.g., price cuts, legal battles, or market share grabs that damage both sides).
2. **Evaluate whether a direct attack is truly necessary.** If attacking a competitor's "walled city" (a deeply entrenched position), calculate the real cost in time, resources, and morale.
3. **Choose the highest-leverage option** that achieves your objective with minimum destruction to your own resources.

### Examples from the Text
- *"To capture the enemy's entire army is better than to destroy it; to take intact a battalion, a company, or a five-man squad is better than destroy them."*
- *"Hence, to win one hundred victories in one hundred battles is not the acme of skill. To subdue the enemy without fighting is the supreme excellence."*
- *"The worst policy is to attack walled cities. Attacking cities is the last resort when there is no alternative."*

### Best Practices
- **Do:** Reconfigure the market so your competitor voluntarily yields position (e.g., platform shifts, standard-setting, ecosystem plays).
- **Do:** Use diplomatic and alliance tools before resorting to competitive force.
- **Don't:** Rush into direct confrontation because it feels decisive. Sun Tzu warns that impatience leads to needless casualties and untaken objectives.
- **Don't:** Treat a competitor's stronghold as a simple conquest problem. It will drain you.

### Common Pitfalls
- **The attrition trap:** Leaders confuse "fighting hard" with "winning." Sustained direct conflict exhausts both sides and erodes competitive advantage.
- **The last-resort fallacy:** Teams default to the most visible lever (price cuts, feature parity) because it's familiar, not because it's optimal.

### Security / Safety Notes
- **Reputational risk:** Aggressive competitive tactics (e.g., hiring away key personnel, litigation) can trigger legal retaliation, industry blacklisting, or customer backlash.
- **Resource depletion:** Direct attacks on entrenched positions consume capital, talent, and time that could be deployed elsewhere.

## Actionable Lesson 2: Attain Strategic Superiority Before Engaging

### Core Principle
Engage only when you have a clear quantitative or qualitative advantage. Sun Tzu provides explicit force-ratio guidelines for when to surround, attack, engage, divide, defend, or elude.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Assess the relative strength** of your position versus the competitor's across key dimensions (capital, talent, distribution, brand, technology).
2. **Apply the strategic ratio framework:**
   - **10:1 advantage** → Surround (completely encircle the market or opportunity; make competition irrelevant).
   - **5:1 advantage** → Attack (commit substantial force to secure a decisive win).
   - **2:1 advantage** → Engage (fight, but maintain caution and reserves).
   - **Equal strength** → Capability to divide (create internal fault lines, split their focus, or fragment their coalition).
   - **Inferior strength** → Defend (fortify your position; avoid being flanked or encircled).
   - **Unfavorable in all respects** → Elude (withdraw, reposition, or wait for conditions to shift).
3. **If you are weak,** do not simply hold ground and conduct a desperate defense. Seek to change the geometry of the battle by dividing the enemy, creating multiple fronts, or escaping to more favorable terrain.

### Examples from the Text
- *"When ten to the enemy's one, surround him. When five times his strength, attack him. If double his strength, engage him. If equally matched, be capable of dividing him. If less in number, be capable of defending yourself. And, if in all respects unfavorable, be capable of eluding him."*
- *"Hence, a weak force will eventually fall captive to a strong one if it simply holds ground and conducts a desperate defense."*

### Best Practices
- **Do:** Quantify advantage before committing resources. Use objective metrics (ARR, market share, runway, talent density, IP portfolio).
- **Do:** If outmatched, reframe the battle by attacking a subordinate objective, creating a new front, or changing the timeline.
- **Don't:** Engage in battles where you cannot achieve at least a 2:1 advantage without extreme risk.
- **Don't:** Let pride or sunk costs trap you in a desperate defensive posture.

### Common Pitfalls
- **The equality illusion:** Leaders overestimate their strength and engage at parity, failing to account for hidden competitor reserves or external support.
- **The siege mentality:** Weak players dig in and wait to be rescued rather than actively seeking to divide the enemy or change the battlefield.

### Security / Safety Notes
- **Escalation risk:** Attacking a stronger or equal competitor can provoke a disproportionate counterattack, especially if you misjudge their hidden capabilities.
- **Strategic overextension:** Committing to a 5:1 attack requires significant resources; if the ratio is miscalculated, you may find yourself overextended with no reserve.

## Actionable Lesson 3: Protect Your Organization From "High-Level Dumb" (Command Interference)

### Core Principle
Disastrous outcomes often stem not from the competitor, but from leadership interference that ignores ground realities. In business, this is the C-suite or board micromanaging operational teams without understanding constraints.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Audit your decision-making flows** for the three classic failure modes:
   - **Hobbling the army:** Leadership sets strategic targets (aggressive revenue goals, impossible launch dates) without understanding operational capacity.
   - **Administrative interference:** Leadership restructures teams, changes reporting lines, or imposes process changes without understanding internal workflow realities.
   - **Operational interference:** Leadership dictates tactical moves (pricing, campaign messaging, feature priorities) without understanding market dynamics or competitive response patterns.
2. **Establish clear decision rights:**
   - Sovereign/board sets the mission and allocates resources.
   - General/executive team determines how to execute.
   - Front-line teams provide ground intelligence.
3. **Create formal escalation channels** for when operational leaders believe a directive is impossible or dangerous.
4. **Institute a "no-interference" protocol** for active competitive engagements once the general has taken the field.

### Examples from the Text
- *"There are three ways in which a sovereign can bring misfortune upon his army:*
  1. *By ordering an advance while ignorant of the fact that the army cannot go forward, or by ordering a retreat while ignorant of the fact that the army cannot fall back. This is described as 'hobbling the army.'*
  2. *By interfering with the army's administration without knowledge of the internal affairs of the army. This causes officers and soldiers to be perplexed.*
  3. *By interfering with direction of fighting, while ignorant of the military principle of adaptation to circumstances. This sows doubts and misgivings in the minds of his officers and soldiers.*
- *"If the army is confused and suspicious, neighboring rulers will take advantage of this and cause trouble. This is simply bringing anarchy into the army and flinging victory away."*

### Best Practices
- **Do:** Build robust internal communication systems so leadership has accurate, timely ground intelligence.
- **Do:** Define and defend the "decision zone" for operational leaders once strategy is set.
- **Do:** Conduct pre-engagement briefings where leadership explicitly confirms resource constraints and rules of engagement.
- **Don't:** Allow strategic imperatives to be set by committees without operational input.
- **Don't:** Change tactics mid-engagement based on political pressure or external perception.
- **Don't:** Penalize leaders for delivering bad news about feasibility; you want truth, not optimism.

### Common Pitfalls
- **The strategic vacuum:** Boards set revenue targets without understanding the competitive terrain required to achieve them.
- **The turf war:** Functional leaders (marketing vs. product vs. sales) lobby the C-suite to override tactical decisions, fragmenting execution.
- **The panic override:** During competitive pressure, leadership imposes short-term fixes that destroy long-term positioning.

### Security / Safety Notes
- **Information warfare risk:** If your organization is internally divided and suspicious, competitors, partners, or investors may exploit those divisions.
- **Talent flight:** High-performing operators leave when they are chronically overruled by uninformed leadership, draining institutional capability.

## Actionable Lesson 4: Seek Circumstances Which Assure Victory (The Five Predictors)

### Core Principle
Victory is not luck; it is the predictable outcome of five conditions being met. Conversely, defeat is predictable when these conditions are absent. Assess your situation against these five criteria before committing to any competitive engagement.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Evaluate Condition 1 — Timing:**
   - Do you know when to fight and when not to fight?
   - If you engage when the market, your product, or your team is not ready, you will lose.
   - If you decline to engage when victory is certain, you waste opportunity.
   - *Action:* Build a decision framework with explicit "go/no-go" criteria for competitive engagements.

2. **Evaluate Condition 2 — Force Handling:**
   - Do you understand how to operate with both superior and inferior forces?
   - If you are stronger, do not simply crush — divide and encircle efficiently.
   - If you are weaker, do not simply retreat — defend, elude, or attack vulnerabilities.
   - *Action:* Train leadership and teams on asymmetric tactics for both strength and weakness scenarios.

3. **Evaluate Condition 3 — Unity:**
   - Are your ranks united in purpose?
   - Misalignment across functions, regions, or hierarchies creates exploitable gaps.
   - *Action:* Align teams around a single objective before any major initiative. Eliminate conflicting incentives.

4. **Evaluate Condition 4 — Preparation:**
   - Are you well prepared while the enemy is not?
   - In business: Do you have the capital, product, talent, and operational readiness when competitors are scrambling?
   - *Action:* Build reserves and strategic optionality during calm periods so you can exploit competitor weakness when it appears.

5. **Evaluate Condition 5 — Leadership Autonomy:**
   - Are your generals (operational leaders) able and free from sovereign (board/C-suite) interference?
   - *Action:* Appoint capable leaders and give them clear mandates with protected decision authority.

### Examples from the Text
- *"Thus, there are five points in which victory may be predicted:*
  1. *He who knows when to fight and when not to fight will win.*
  2. *He who understands how to handle both superior and inferior forces will win.*
  3. *He whose ranks are united in purpose will win.*
  4. *He who is well prepared and lies in wait for an enemy who is not well prepared will win.*
  5. *He whose generals are able and not interfered with by the sovereign will win.*
- *"It is in these five points that the way to victory is known."*

### Best Practices
- **Do:** Run a "Five Predictors" assessment before any major competitive initiative, M&A, product launch, or market entry.
- **Do:** Document which conditions are weak and build mitigation plans.
- **Do:** Use Condition 4 (preparation) as a peacetime priority — competitive readiness is built during stability, not crisis.
- **Don't:** Proceed with an initiative if two or more conditions are clearly absent. Rethink timing or approach.
- **Don't:** Allow unity (Condition 3) to be sacrificed for speed. Speed without alignment creates disorder.

### Common Pitfalls
- **The premature engagement:** Companies launch products or enter markets without preparation, hoping momentum will carry them.
- **The divided army:** Functional silos, regional rivalries, or misaligned incentives sabotage coordinated execution.
- **The puppet general:** Operational leaders are given responsibility but stripped of authority, ensuring failure.

### Security / Safety Notes
- **Predictability exposure:** If you consistently violate these five conditions, competitors will learn to anticipate and exploit your patterns.
- **Intelligence vulnerability:** Poor internal alignment and leadership interference create signals that competitors can read and exploit.

## Actionable Lesson 5: Master the Dual Knowledge — Know Yourself and Know the Enemy

### Core Principle
This is the most famous passage in the chapter and arguably in the entire text. Knowledge is the foundation of all strategic calculation. Without it, you are gambling.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Know yourself:**
   - Conduct a rigorous internal audit of your strengths, weaknesses, resources, culture, and decision-making velocity.
   - Map your asymmetric advantages (what you can do that competitors cannot easily copy).
   - Identify your fatal vulnerabilities (what competitors can exploit if they concentrate force).
   - *Action:* Maintain a living competitive self-assessment updated quarterly.

2. **Know the enemy:**
   - Gather intelligence on competitors' strategy (not just current products, but their 12–24 month roadmap).
   - Map their alliances, partnerships, and dependency chains.
   - Understand their organizational dynamics: Where are their decision bottlenecks? Where are they weak?
   - *Action:* Build a dedicated competitive intelligence function or assign cross-functional ownership.

3. **Apply the knowledge gradient:**
   - **Know both self and enemy** → Fight a hundred battles with no danger of defeat.
   - **Know only self** → Chances of winning and losing are equal (gambling).
   - **Know neither** → Sure to be defeated in every battle.

### Examples from the Text
- *"Know the enemy and know yourself, and you can fight a hundred battles with no danger of defeat. When you are ignorant of the enemy but know yourself, your chances of winning and losing are equal. If ignorant both of your enemy and of yourself, you are sure to be defeated in every battle."*

### Best Practices
- **Do:** Invest in structured competitive intelligence (public filings, job postings, patent filings, conference talks, partner interviews, customer win/loss analysis).
- **Do:** Run red-team exercises where your team plays the role of your fiercest competitor to uncover blind spots.
- **Do:** Update self-assessments after every major initiative, win or lose.
- **Don't:** Rely on anecdotes or headline news for competitor intelligence. Go to primary sources.
- **Don't:** Assume competitor behavior today will be the same tomorrow. Map their strategic trajectory, not just their current state.

### Common Pitfalls
- **The mirror-image trap:** Assuming competitors think and act like you do. They do not.
- **The static assessment:** Treating a competitor's capabilities as fixed when they are raising capital, hiring, or pivoting.
- **The arrogance of self-knowledge:** Knowing your own strengths but underestimating a scrappy or hungry competitor.

### Security / Safety Notes
- **Espionage ethics:** Gather only publicly available intelligence or information obtained through legitimate channels. Do not misrepresent yourself to obtain confidential competitor data.
- **Information leakage:** Your own competitive intelligence activities can be discovered. Operate discreetly and comply with all applicable laws (e.g., Economic Espionage Act, trade secret protections).
