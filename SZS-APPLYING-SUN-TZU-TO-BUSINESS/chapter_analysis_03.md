# Chapter Two: Waging War — Actionable Analysis

**Source:** Sun Tzu for Success, Chapter Two: Waging War (pages 6–8)

---

## Overview

This chapter teaches the strategic discipline of resource management, operational tempo, and asymmetric leverage in competitive engagements. The four actionable lessons below translate Sun Tzu's ancient military wisdom into modern business and competitive practice.

---

## Lesson 1: Marshal Adequate Resources Before Committing

### Core Principle
Before engaging in any significant campaign, calculate the full cost of operations and ensure funding is in hand. Do not proceed without a clear resource budget.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Enumerate every cost category**: personnel, logistics, materials, maintenance, diplomacy, and contingency reserves.
2. **Quantify daily burn rate**: measure ongoing expenses per unit time (e.g., per day, per sprint, per quarter).
3. **Set a minimum funding threshold**: determine the capital required before launch.
4. **Validate readiness**: confirm that the full required amount is available before beginning operations.
5. **Document assumptions**: record unit costs and quantities so they can be audited and revised.

### Examples from the Text
- "the expenditure at home and in the field, the stipends for the entertainment of state guests and diplomatic envoys, the cost of materials such as glue and lacquer, and the expense for care and maintenance of chariots and armor, will amount to one thousand pieces of gold a day."
- "An army of one hundred thousand men can be raised only when this money is in hand."

### Best Practices
- **Do** build a line-item budget covering direct and indirect costs.
- **Do** include maintenance and replacement costs, not just initial deployment.
- **Don't** underestimate diplomatic or relationship overhead.
- **Don't** assume funding can be sourced mid-campaign.

### Common Pitfalls
- Forgetting hidden operational costs (support staff, tooling, third-party services).
- Optimistic budgeting that excludes buffer for delays.
- Launching before the full war chest is assembled, hoping revenue will cover shortfalls.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Sensitive budget data should be restricted to authorized decision-makers. Leaking campaign budgets to competitors can undermine strategic positioning and invite preemptive countermeasures.

## Lesson 2: Make Speed Your Primary Weapon

### Core Principle
The main objective in any engagement is a quick victory. Protracted conflict exhausts weapons, dampens morale, drains state resources, and creates opportunities for opportunistic third parties.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Define a clear victory condition**: specify what "win" looks like and when the campaign can end.
2. **Set a hard deadline**: allocate a fixed timebox after which the operation must conclude or be reassessed.
3. **Sequence operations for minimum duration**: optimize the critical path to reduce total elapsed time.
4. **Monitor wear indicators**: track morale, attrition, and resource depletion continuously.
5. **Escalate or exit decisively**: if the timeline slips, choose between concentrated escalation or disciplined withdrawal.

### Examples from the Text
- "In directing such an enormous army, a speedy victory is the main object."
- "If the war is long delayed, the men's weapons will be blunted and their ardor will be dampened."
- "There has never been a case in which a prolonged war has benefited a country."
- "When your weapons are blunted, your ardor dampened, your strength exhausted, and your treasure spent, neighboring rulers will take advantage of your distress."

### Best Practices
- **Do** prioritize operational velocity over exhaustive perfection.
- **Do** build slack into schedules so delays do not cascade into open-ended campaigns.
- **Don't** let sunk costs keep you in a losing engagement.
- **Don't** ignore warning signs that the conflict is dragging beyond its useful window.

### Common Pitfalls
- Getting locked into a long siege or feature war that bleeds resources.
- Assuming more time will improve odds; instead, it usually weakens position.
- Underestimating how competitors exploit your distraction.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Prolonged engagements increase exposure to intelligence leaks, insider threats, and supply-chain disruptions. Short campaigns reduce attack surface and limit the window for adversarial counter-intelligence.

## Lesson 3: Profit from Victories — Convert Enemy Strength Into Your Own

### Core Principle
Victory should make you stronger, not merely consume your existing reserves. Capture and reuse enemy resources, reward your forces from enemy spoils, and avoid forcing your home population to bear the full cost of distant operations.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Target enemy logistics first**: identify the adversary's supply chain, equipment, and provisions as primary objectives.
2. **Capture, don't destroy**: preserve enemy materiel for your own use whenever possible.
3. **Reward from spoils**: use captured wealth and assets to compensate your team, reinforcing motivation without depleting domestic reserves.
4. **Convert prisoners and assets**: integrate captured equipment, talent, or market share into your own operations.
5. **Calculate opportunity cost**: quantify what you save by sourcing locally versus shipping from home base.

### Examples from the Text
- "Those adept in employing troops do not require a second levy of conscripts or more than two provisionings. They carry military supplies from the homeland and make up for their provisions relying on the enemy."
- "One zhong of grains obtained from the local area is equal to twenty zhong shipped from the home country; one dan of fodder in the conquered area is equal to twenty dan from the domestic store."
- "When more than ten chariots have been captured, those who took the enemy chariot first should be rewarded. Then, the enemy's flags and banners should be replaced with ours; the captured chariots mixed with ours and mounted by our men."
- "The prisoners of war should be kindly treated and kept. This is called 'becoming stronger in the course of defeating the enemy.'"

### Best Practices
- **Do** treat captured assets, customers, or talent as force multipliers, not just trophies.
- **Do** design operations to preserve enemy infrastructure for reuse.
- **Don't** destroy value that could be captured and integrated.
- **Don't** finance victories entirely from internal budgets when external spoils are available.

### Common Pitfalls
- Sabotaging competitor assets in ways that eliminate their reuse value.
- Failing to reward contributors from captured gains, demotivating future effort.
- Allowing domestic financial strain to grow because no effort was made to tap adversary resources.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Captured assets may carry hidden risks: embedded malware, compromised credentials, legal encumbrances, or poisoned data. Always inspect and sanitize before integration. Treat captured personnel with fairness to avoid retaliation and reputational damage.

## Lesson 4: Know Your Craft — Competence Is the Guarantee of Security

### Core Principle
The general who understands the art of war becomes the controller of his people's fate and the guarantor of his nation's security. Technical and strategic mastery is non-negotiable.

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Master the fundamentals**: understand the mechanics, economics, and physics of your domain.
2. **Study historical cases**: learn from both successful and failed campaigns.
3. **Practice decision-making under uncertainty**: simulate scenarios and rehearse responses.
4. **Build tacit judgment**: develop intuition through repeated exposure to real conditions.
5. **Continuously update knowledge**: tactics and environments evolve; refresh your skill set.

### Examples from the Text
- "the general who understands war is the controller of his people's fate and the guarantor of the security of the nation."
- "Only those who understand the dangers inherent in employing troops know how to conduct war in the most profitable way."
- "Thus, while we have heard of stupid haste in war, we have not yet seen a clever operation that was prolonged."

### Best Practices
- **Do** invest in continuous learning and professional development.
- **Do** seek mentorship and review from senior practitioners.
- **Don't** let confidence outpace competence; avoid overextension into unfamiliar terrain.
- **Don't** delegate strategic understanding entirely to subordinates without maintaining personal mastery.

### Common Pitfalls
- Mistaking activity for competence — working long hours without strategic insight.
- Relying on outdated playbooks in changed environments.
- Ignoring edge cases and rare-event risks because they seem improbable.

### Security/Safety Notes
- In competitive or adversarial environments, strategic ignorance creates existential risk. An uninformed leader may misallocate resources, expose the organization to preventable harm, or trigger cascading failures. Maintain operational security by ensuring only trusted, competent individuals hold decision authority.
