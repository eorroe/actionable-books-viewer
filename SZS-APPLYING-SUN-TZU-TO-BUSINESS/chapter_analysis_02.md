# Chapter Analysis: Chapter One – Laying Plans
**Source:** *Sun Tzu for Success* by Gerald Michaelson with Steven Michaelson  
**Pages:** 3–5  
**Chapter Focus:** Pre-engagement assessment, strategic advantage, and the art of deception

---

## Overview

Chapter One establishes the foundational principle that success in any competitive endeavor requires thorough planning and assessment before action. Sun Tzu argues that war (or any significant challenge) is a matter of vital importance that must be studied systematically. The chapter introduces five constant factors and seven comparative attributes for evaluating competitive positions, then outlines the strategic use of deception and surprise.

---

## Actionable Lesson 1: Conduct a Thorough Pre-Engagement Assessment

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Identify the five constant factors relevant to your situation: **moral influence**, **weather**, **terrain**, **commander**, and **doctrine**.
2. Compare your standing in each factor against the competitor's standing.
3. Conduct a seven-point comparative analysis between your side and the opposing side.
4. Use the comparison to forecast the likely outcome before committing resources.
5. Only proceed if your assessment shows favorable conditions; otherwise, withdraw or adjust your strategy.

### Examples from the Text
- *"War is a matter of vital importance to the state; a matter of life and death, the road either to survival or to ruin. Hence, it is imperative that it be thoroughly studied."*
- *"These five constant factors should be familiar to every general. He who masters them wins, he who does not is defeated."*
- *"By means of these seven elements, I can forecast victory or defeat."*

### Best Practices
- **Do:** Make the five factors familiar to every leader and key team member.
- **Do:** Systematically document the seven comparisons in writing before making decisions.
- **Do:** Reassess conditions regularly as circumstances change.
- **Don't:** Treat assessment as a one-time activity at the start of a campaign.
- **Don't:** Skip any of the five constant factors because they seem less relevant.
- **Don't:** Allow personal bias to skew your evaluation of your own position.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Incomplete assessment:** Missing one or more of the five constant factors leads to blind spots.
- **Overconfidence in favorable factors:** Assuming victory without verifying all conditions.
- **Static analysis:** Failing to update assessments as the competitive environment evolves.
- **Emotional override:** Letting hope or fear replace objective evaluation.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Thorough assessment reduces risk by preventing unnecessary engagement.
- Understanding moral influence (ethics, reputation, stakeholder trust) prevents strategic decisions that could damage long-term credibility.

## Actionable Lesson 2: Evaluate the Seven Comparative Attributes

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **Moral Influence:** Determine which side possesses greater moral authority, legitimacy, or stakeholder support.
2. **Commander Capability:** Objectively assess the competence of leadership on both sides.
3. **Weather and Terrain:** Analyze external environmental conditions (market conditions, timing, physical/logistical environment) favoring each side.
4. **Implementation of Decrees:** Measure how effectively each side executes its strategies and policies.
5. **Arms Superiority:** Compare resources, technology, and tools available to each side.
6. **Training Quality:** Evaluate the preparedness and skill level of personnel.
7. **Rewards and Punishments:** Assess the strictness and impartiality of accountability systems.

### Examples from the Text
The seven comparisons are listed explicitly:
1. *"Which sovereign possesses greater moral influence?"*
2. *"Which commander is more capable?"*
3. *"Which side holds more favorable conditions in weather and terrain?"*
4. *"On which side are decrees better implemented?"*
5. *"Which side is superior in arms?"*
6. *"On which side are officers and men better trained?"*
7. *"Which side is stricter and more impartial in meting out rewards and punishments?"*

### Best Practices
- **Do:** Gather objective data for each attribute rather than relying on perception.
- **Do:** Benchmark your performance against the competitor in each category.
- **Do:** Prioritize improving the attributes where you are weakest.
- **Don't:** Focus exclusively on material advantages (arms) while neglecting moral or organizational factors.
- **Don't:** Assume that past performance guarantees future results in any category.
- **Don't:** Ignore the competitor's strengths because they are uncomfortable to acknowledge.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Attribute neglect:** Failing to evaluate one or more of the seven attributes.
- **Subjective bias:** Overrating your own side's capabilities and underrating the competitor's.
- **Single-factor reliance:** Believing that superiority in one area (e.g., arms) compensates for weaknesses in others.
- **Static benchmarking:** Not updating comparative assessments as conditions change.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Weaknesses in rewards/punishments (impartiality) can lead to internal discipline failures, creating vulnerability.
- Decrees that are poorly implemented indicate organizational dysfunction that competitors can exploit.

## Actionable Lesson 3: Create Strategic Advantage Beyond Ordinary Rules

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Study the standard approaches and rules governing your field.
2. Identify where conventional thinking limits potential outcomes.
3. Develop unconventional methods that create advantage "over and beyond the ordinary rules."
4. Act expediently—adapt your response to the specific conditions of the field at the moment.
5. Prepare to meet any exigency (unexpected event or emergency) with flexible, situation-specific responses.

### Examples from the Text
- *"Having paid attention to the advantages of my strategems, the commander must create a helpful situation over and beyond the ordinary rules. By 'situation,' I mean he should act expediently in accordance with what is advantageous in the field and so meet any exigency."*

### Best Practices
- **Do:** Encourage creative thinking and challenge "the way things have always been done."
- **Do:** Build organizational flexibility so you can exploit unexpected opportunities.
- **Do:** Train your team to respond to exigencies without waiting for permission.
- **Don't:** Break rules unnecessarily—strategic advantage comes from effectiveness, not rule-breaking for its own sake.
- **Don't:** Be unpredictable for the sake of unpredictability; your own team must understand your intent.
- **Don't:** Sacrifice long-term principles for short-term situational gains.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Rigid adherence to doctrine:** Following established procedures even when they no longer fit the situation.
- **Paralysis by analysis:** Waiting for perfect information instead of acting expediently.
- **Reactive posture:** Waiting for the competitor to dictate the situation rather than shaping it proactively.
- **Lack of preparation:** Failing to build the capability to handle exigencies before they occur.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Operating beyond ordinary rules requires strong ethical guardrails to prevent reckless or harmful behavior.
- Expedient action should not bypass critical safety checks or compliance requirements.

## Actionable Lesson 4: Master the Art of Deception

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. **When able to attack, pretend to be unable.** Mask your true capability to strike.
2. **When employing your forces, seem inactive.** Conceal movement and deployment.
3. **When near, make the enemy believe you are far away.** Use misdirection to mask proximity.
4. **When far away, make the enemy believe you are near.** Create the perception of threat from unexpected directions.
5. **Offer bait to allure the enemy** when he covets small advantages.
6. **Strike the enemy when he is in disorder.** Target moments of confusion or disorganization.
7. **Take double precautions** against an enemy well prepared with substantial strength.
8. **Evade** an enemy powerful in action.
9. **Seek to discourage** an angry enemy.
10. **Make him arrogant** if he appears humble (exploit underestimation).
11. **Wear them down** if his forces have taken a good rest.
12. **Divide them** if his forces are united.
13. **Launch the attack where he is unprepared.**
14. **Take action when it is unexpected.**

### Examples from the Text
The text explicitly lists all fourteen deception principles as a continuous passage:
- *"All warfare is based on deception. Therefore, when able to attack, we must pretend to be unable; when employing our forces, we must seem inactive; when we are near, we must make the enemy believe we are far away; when far away, we must make him believe we are near. Offer a bait to allure the enemy, when he covets small advantages; strike the enemy when he is in disorder. If he is well prepared with substantial strength, take double precautions against him. If he is powerful in action, evade him. If he is angry, seek to discourage him. If he appears humble, make him arrogant. If his forces have taken a good rest, wear them down. If his forces are united, divide them. Launch the attack where he is unprepared; take action when it is unexpected."*

### Best Practices
- **Do:** Ensure your own forces understand the real plan (deception is for the enemy, not your team).
- **Do:** Use deception to create favorable conditions, not to trap your own forces.
- **Do:** Make deception believable—implausible misdirection will be seen through.
- **Don't:** Rely on a single deception tactic; combine multiple methods for greater effect.
- **Don't:** Let deception become habit—use it selectively when it creates real advantage.
- **Don't:** Deceive stakeholders who have a right to accurate information (e.g., investors, regulators).

### Common Pitfalls
- **Predictable patterns:** Using the same deceptive tactics repeatedly allows the enemy to learn your patterns.
- **Self-deception:** Believing your own deception or confusing your team about real intentions.
- **Over-complexity:** Creating so many layers of deception that your own forces cannot execute the real plan.
- **Backfire:** Deception discovered early can damage credibility and strengthen the enemy's resolve.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Deception in business contexts must comply with laws against fraud, misrepresentation, and market manipulation.
- Ethical boundary: Deception in competitive intelligence or marketing must not cross into false advertising, securities fraud, or industrial espionage.
- Clear internal communication is essential—your own team must know when deception is being used to avoid dangerous confusion.

## Actionable Lesson 5: Attack Where the Enemy Is Unprepared and Act When It Is Unexpected

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Gather intelligence on where the competitor is weak or unguarded.
2. Identify areas where the competitor does not expect action from you.
3. Time your engagement for moments when the competitor is least prepared.
4. Avoid predictable patterns in your own behavior that would allow the competitor to anticipate your moves.
5. Strike quickly before the competitor can adjust defenses.

### Examples from the Text
- *"Launch the attack where he is unprepared; take action when it is unexpected. These are the keys to victory for a strategist."*

### Best Practices
- **Do:** Invest in intelligence gathering to identify genuine unpreparedness.
- **Do:** Use unpredictability in timing and location to keep the competitor off balance.
- **Do:** Maintain operational security so your plans cannot be anticipated.
- **Don't:** Attack randomly; unpreparedness must be confirmed, not assumed.
- **Don't:** Mistake competitor passivity for unpreparedness—they may be setting a trap.
- **Don't:** Reveal your pattern of attacking unprepared points, or the competitor will begin to prepare.

### Common Pitfalls
- **False assumption of unpreparedness:** Mistaking competitor confidence or secrecy for actual weakness.
- **Reckless surprise:** Attacking without sufficient force because you assume the target is soft.
- **Pattern exposure:** Repeating the same type of surprise attack, allowing the competitor to adapt.
- **Underestimation:** Assuming the competitor cannot respond to an unexpected attack.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Surprise attacks can provoke severe retaliation if the competitor feels cornered or humiliated.
- Ensure you have contingency plans if the surprise attack fails or triggers an escalation.
- Legal and ethical constraints apply—surprise should not involve illegal or unethical tactics.

## Actionable Lesson 6: Conduct Thorough Pre-War Calculations

### Step-by-Step Instructions
1. Before any major commitment, conduct a comprehensive calculation of all factors.
2. Assign weighted scores to favorable and unfavorable conditions.
3. Seek a high number of favorable scores ("many scores") before proceeding.
4. If your calculation yields few or no favorable scores, withdraw or redesign your approach.
5. Treat the calculation as a disciplined process, not a formality.

### Examples from the Text
- *"Now, the commander who gets many scores during the calculations in the temple before the war will have more likelihood of winning. The commander who gets few scores during the calculations in the temple before the war will have less chance of success. With many scores, one can win; with few scores, one cannot. How much less chance of victory has one who gets no scores at all!"*
- *"By examining the situation through these aspects, I can foresee who is likely to win or lose."*

### Best Practices
- **Do:** Formalize your assessment process with checklists and scoring systems.
- **Do:** Involve multiple perspectives to reduce individual bias.
- **Do:** Recalculate as new information becomes available.
- **Don't:** Proceed with action if your assessment shows unfavorable odds.
- **Don't:** Manipulate calculations to justify a decision you have already made emotionally.
- **Don't:** Treat calculation as a guarantee—it improves odds but does not ensure victory.

### Common Pitfalls
- **Skipping calculation:** Acting impulsively without structured analysis.
- **Confirmation bias:** Interpreting data to support a pre-existing decision.
- **Analysis paralysis:** Getting stuck in calculation and missing the window for action.
- **False precision:** Treating subjective judgments as objective numerical scores.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Thorough calculation prevents catastrophic failures from uninformed action.
- Document your assessments to enable post-action review and learning.
- Recognize that even favorable calculations carry risk; have contingency plans for unexpected developments.

## Summary

Chapter One provides a complete pre-engagement framework: **assess thoroughly** using the five constant factors and seven comparative attributes; **build superiority** in moral influence, command, implementation, arms, training, and accountability; **create strategic advantage** beyond conventional rules; **master deception** to control the competitor's perception; **strike where unprepared** and act when unexpected; and **calculate rigorously** before committing. The chapter teaches that victory is won before the battle begins—through superior planning, assessment, and strategic positioning.
