# Chapter Twelve: Attack By Fire — Actionable Analysis
**Source:** *Sun Tzu for Success*, Chapter Twelve: Attack By Fire (pages 39–40)

---

## Overview

Chapter Twelve, "Attack By Fire," translates Sun Tzu's ancient military principle of using fire as a weapon into modern competitive strategy. The chapter teaches that disruption, decisive follow-through, and disciplined restraint are the three pillars of successful attack. Each pillar contains specific, actionable rules for when and how to act.

---

## Lesson 1: Be Disruptive and Intrusive — Apply Targeted Pressure

### Core Principle
Disruption creates openings. The most effective attacks target the enemy's (competitor's) critical support systems rather than their main force directly.

### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Identify the five target categories for disruption:**
   - Primary operational base or workforce
   - Supply chain and inventory
   - Logistics and distribution channels
   - Infrastructure, data centers, or core systems
   - Communication or transportation networks

2. **Maintain readiness at all times:**
   - Keep disruption resources (intelligence, rapid-response teams, alternative suppliers) prepared before a crisis or competitive move arises.
   - Do not wait until a conflict begins to gather materials.

3. **Time your actions to environmental conditions:**
   - Launch disruptive actions when market or industry conditions are most favorable (e.g., during seasonal slowdowns, when a competitor is financially strained, or during regulatory changes).
   - Recognize that favorable conditions are temporary; act while the "wind" is favorable.

4. **Assess the five changing situations before acting:**
   - If a competitor is already destabilized (fire in their camp), coordinate your move immediately from your position.
   - If a competitor appears calm despite internal trouble, wait; premature attack wastes resources.
   - When a competitor's weakness peaks (flames at their height), follow up with your attack if feasible; otherwise hold position.
   - If you can initiate disruption externally, do not wait for internal problems to appear.
   - Only act when the moment is truly suitable.

5. **Consider directional factors:**
   - Do not attack from a disadvantaged position (e.g., do not launch a marketing campaign against a competitor from a position of lower brand trust if they control the narrative).
   - Recognize cyclical patterns; if momentum is strong during a period, anticipate a coming lull and plan accordingly.

### Examples from the Text
- Sun Tzu lists five fire targets: soldiers in camp, provisions, baggage-trains, arsenals, and transportation lines. In business, these map to disrupting employees/operations, supply of goods, shipping/delivery, technology/data infrastructure, and communication channels.
- The text warns: "If the fire starts from up-wind, do not launch attack from down-wind," meaning do not engage from a position where the advantage flows against you.

### Best Practices (Do's and Don'ts)

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Prepare disruption capabilities in advance | Launch attacks without intelligence or preparation |
| Target support systems, not just front-line operations | Attack when the enemy is calm and prepared |
| Act when conditions are clearly favorable | Act out of impulse or anger |
| Maintain flexibility to hold position if conditions shift | Assume favorable conditions will last indefinitely |
| Coordinate internal and external actions for maximum effect | Ignore environmental or market signals |

### Common Pitfalls
- **Attacking too early:** Disrupting a competitor before they are weakened wastes resources and can trigger defensive retaliation.
- **Attacking too late:** Missing the window when a competitor is most vulnerable allows them to recover and fortify.
- **Ignoring the "wind direction":** Entering a market or launching a competitive move from a position of structural weakness (down-wind) leads to predictable failure.
- **Single-point disruption:** Relying on only one tactic instead of understanding all five dimensions of attack.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Disruption tactics can damage relationships, brand reputation, and invite legal or regulatory scrutiny. Ensure actions comply with antitrust, competitive intelligence, and industry-specific regulations.
- Maintain ethical boundaries: targeting a competitor's employees or sabotaging infrastructure crosses into illegal territory. The "fire" metaphor in business should be interpreted as aggressive but lawful competitive strategy.

## Lesson 2: Consolidate Your Gains — Secure What You Win

### Core Principle
Victory is incomplete without consolidation. Capturing market share, winning clients, or outperforming a competitor means nothing if the gains are not institutionalized and defended.

### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Plan consolidation before the attack:**
   - Do not initiate a competitive move, market expansion, or major business initiative without a post-victory integration plan.
   - Define what "holding the ground" looks like operationally.

2. **Execute the consolidation immediately after victory:**
   - Transfer winning momentum into lasting systems: onboarding new clients, integrating acquired assets, standardizing new processes.
   - Do not assume victory is self-sustaining.

3. **Align leadership on the long-term plan:**
   - Enlightened leaders must deliberate thoroughly before committing resources.
   - Generals (executives) must execute consolidation plans carefully and without deviation.

4. **Measure consolidation success:**
   - Track retention, integration timelines, and operational stability after a win.
   - If gains are eroding, treat it as an ominous sign requiring immediate corrective action.

### Examples from the Text
- "To win battles and capture lands and cities, but to fail to consolidate these achievements is ominous and may be described as a waste of resources and time."
- The text explicitly links deliberation before battle and careful execution after victory to state security and army preservation.

### Best Practices (Do's and Don'ts)

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Build integration and retention plans before launching initiatives | Declare victory and move on without securing gains |
| Involve leadership in planning and oversight | Leave consolidation to ad hoc or uncoordinated efforts |
| Treat unconsolidated gains as a critical failure | Assume market share or client relationships are permanent |
| Audit post-victory operations for stability | Ignore early warning signs of gain erosion |

### Common Pitfalls
- **Victory complacency:** Assuming that winning a deal or outperforming a competitor eliminates the need for follow-up action.
- **Resource drain:** Winning a battle but expending all resources in the process, leaving no capacity to consolidate.
- **Cultural mismatch:** Integrating new assets, teams, or clients without aligning them to existing systems and values.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Rapid expansion without consolidation can expose the organization to operational risk, compliance gaps, and client attrition. Ensure that growth does not outpace governance, security controls, or quality assurance.

## Lesson 3: Exercise Restraint — Fight Only the Right Battles

### Core Principle
Not all conflicts deserve engagement. Discipline, patience, and strategic selectivity prevent costly mistakes and preserve long-term capability.

### Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Apply the three gates of restraint before any major action:**
   - Gate 1: Is this in the interest of the organization/state? If no, do not act.
   - Gate 2: Am I confident of success? If no, do not commit resources or personnel.
   - Gate 3: Is there genuine danger or necessity? If no, do not engage.

2. **Separate emotion from strategy:**
   - Do not initiate competitive or aggressive action out of anger, frustration, or ego.
   - Do not continue a losing battle out of resentment or pride.

3. **Evaluate actions on advantage, not emotion:**
   - Take action only if it creates measurable, lasting advantage.
   - Cancel or withdraw if the cost exceeds the benefit, even if significant resources have already been invested (sunk-cost awareness).

4. **Accept reversibility:**
   - Recognize that emotional states (anger, resentment) are temporary, but organizational destruction is permanent.
   - Prioritize organizational survival and long-term health over short-term victories.

5. **Lead with prudence and caution:**
   - Enlightened leaders remain prudent; capable generals remain cautious.
   - Build a culture where restraint is viewed as strength, not weakness.

### Examples from the Text
- "If not in the interests of the state, do not act. If you are not sure of success, do not use troops. If you are not in danger, do not fight a battle."
- "A sovereign should not launch a war simply out of anger, nor should a general fight a war simply out of resentment."
- "An angered man can be happy again, just as a resentful one can feel pleased again, but a state that has perished can never revive, nor can a dead man be brought back to life."

### Best Practices (Do's and Don'ts)

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply a formal decision gate before major initiatives | React to competitor provocations impulsively |
| Measure every action against organizational interest and probability of success | Fight battles to satisfy ego or past grievances |
| Withdraw from losing positions quickly | Throw good resources after bad due to pride |
| Communicate restraint as strategic discipline | Treat caution as cowardice or weakness |
| Preserve organizational health over short-term wins | Sacrifice long-term stability for temporary advantage |

### Common Pitfalls
- **Emotional escalation:** Allowing pride, anger, or competitive fervor to override objective analysis.
- **Sunk-cost fallacy:** Continuing to invest in a failing initiative because of past investment rather than future potential.
- **Overextension:** Engaging in too many conflicts simultaneously, diluting focus and resources.
- **Misreading danger:** Underestimating real threats while overreacting to minor provocations.

### Security/Safety Notes
- Premature or unnecessary engagement exposes the organization to financial, legal, and reputational risk. Restraint is both a defensive and offensive security measure: it preserves capital, reduces attack surface, and maintains optionality.
- Document decision-making criteria for major actions to ensure consistency and accountability, and to protect against hindsight bias.

## Final Note

Chapter Twelve teaches that success in competitive situations requires a balanced triad: the courage to disrupt, the discipline to consolidate, and the wisdom to restrain. Mastering all three — and knowing when to apply each — is the foundation of sustainable competitive advantage.
