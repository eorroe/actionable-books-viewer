# Chapter Analysis: Section Four — Competitive Success
**Source:** *Sun Tzu for Success*, pages 155–170  
**Subsections:** Pick Your Battles Carefully | Know Your Opponent | Skillful Attacks Win | Timing Is Everything

## 1. Use the Hierarchy of Engagement (Attack Strategy Before Armies)
**Source text:** Pages 155–157 (Introduction)

### Step-by-step instructions
1. Rank your competitive options before choosing a course of action.
2. First choice: Attack the opponent's **strategy** (win without fighting).
3. Second choice: Disrupt the opponent's **alliances** through diplomacy/negotiation.
4. Third choice: Attack the opponent's **army in the field** (indirect approach or blitz).
5. Last resort: Attack **walled cities** (head-on conflict, high resource cost).

### Examples from the text
- "Kill 'em with kindness" or achieving excellence that discourages opposition = attacking strategy.
- "Let's sit down and talk" = disrupting alliances.
- "Taking the case to court" or expending many resources to win = attacking walled cities.

### Best practices
- **Do:** Seek psychological or strategic advantages that make direct conflict unnecessary.
- **Do:** Invest in diplomacy and relationship management to avoid costly battles.
- **Don't:** Rush to direct confrontation when a strategic or diplomatic solution is available.

### Common pitfalls
- Mistaking activity for progress—fighting hard but not winning strategically.
- Overlooking the high cost and low payoff of attacking "walled cities" (e.g., litigation, entrenched market positions).

## 2. Evaluate Odds Before Engaging (Sun Tzu's Math Lesson)
**Source text:** Pages 158–160 ("Pick Your Battles Carefully")

### Step-by-step instructions
1. Assess relative strength before committing troops or resources.
2. 10:1 superiority → Surround the opponent.
3. 5:1 superiority → Attack.
4. 2:1 superiority → Engage.
5. Equally matched → Divide the opponent's forces.
6. Outnumbered → Defend yourself.
7. Overwhelmingly inferior → Elude/avoid engagement.
8. If you cannot achieve favorable odds, seek a different battle or objective.

### Examples from the text
- Allied island-hopping invasions in the Pacific required a minimum of 3-to-1 superiority.
- The planned invasion of Japan was 1-to-1; the Atom Bomb made the invasion unnecessary (and avoided unfavorable odds).

### Best practices
- **Do:** Gather intelligence on relative strengths (numerical, technological, financial) before engaging.
- **Do:** Treat technological strength as a force multiplier that can substitute for numerical strength.
- **Don't:** Hold ground and conduct desperate defense when outmatched—elude instead.

### Common pitfalls
- Overestimating your own strength or underestimating the opponent's.
- Sunk-cost fallacy—continuing a battle because you've already invested resources, not because you can win.

## 3. Avoid the Perils of Winning Too Many Political Battles
**Source text:** Pages 158–160 ("Pick Your Battles Carefully" — "Avoid Political Battles")

### Step-by-step instructions
1. Recognize that winning many battles creates jealous enemies.
2. Assess whether a political fight is necessary before engaging.
3. If you do not have to fight, do not fight.
4. Preserve your resources and relationships for essential conflicts.

### Examples from the text
- Ancient Chinese warlord quote: "One who gains one victory becomes the Emperor; one who gains two, a King; one who gains three, Lord Protector; one who gains four is exhausted; one who gains five victories suffers calamity."

### Best practices
- **Do:** Choose political battles selectively; prioritize long-term success over short-term victories.
- **Do:** Build coalitions and goodwill to neutralize future enemies.
- **Don't:** Seek victory in every office or organizational conflict.

### Common pitfalls
- Winning battles but losing the war by exhausting allies and inflaming rivals.
- Becoming known as a relentless competitor who must be opposed at every turn.

## 4. Apply the Five Points for Predicting Victory
**Source text:** Pages 159–160 ("Know How to Win")

### Step-by-step instructions
1. **Know when to fight and when not to fight** — Control your temper and approach problems clinically.
2. **Understand how to handle both superior and inferior forces** — Use relative superiority (disruption, surprise, concentration) to offset inferiority.
3. **Ensure ranks are united in purpose** — Believe in what you are doing and align your team.
4. **Be well prepared and lie in wait for an unprepared enemy** — Do your homework before the event.
5. **Have able generals/executors and avoid interference** — Let capable people execute without micromanagement.

### Examples from the text
- Vietnam cited as an example of what happens when leadership interferes with military decision-making (President Johnson picking bombing targets).

### Best practices
- **Do:** Conduct pre-battle analysis to identify why losers lost and apply those lessons proactively.
- **Do:** Delegate execution to capable team members and avoid second-guessing.
- **Don't:** React emotionally to competitive threats; use clinical analysis instead.

### Common pitfalls
- Skipping pre-engagement analysis and hoping for the best.
- Allowing organizational politics to override professional judgment during execution.

## 5. Build a Creative Information System (Know Your Opponent)
**Source text:** Pages 162–163 ("Build a Creative Information System")

### Step-by-step instructions
1. **Cultivate contacts** — Develop an informal, organized information flow. Nurture relationships with messages of appreciation.
2. **Take snapshots** — Gather small pieces of current intelligence from multiple contacts to build a big picture.
3. **Validate your sources** — Identify which contacts provide raw data versus analysis and insights. Prioritize reliable, insightful sources.
4. **Put it all together** — Develop a network, evaluate usefulness of information, then cultivate the best sources.

### Examples from the text
- Emailing a golf contact in Hawaii to learn about business conditions in another industry.
- A retailer noticing his business was slow, then seeking other snapshots to build a financial picture of an impending downturn and adjusting his stock portfolio accordingly.

### Best practices
- **Do:** Maintain reciprocity—match outgoing information flow with incoming flow.
- **Do:** Use diverse contacts to cross-validate information.
- **Don't:** Rely on a single source or filtered reports; seek close personal contact.

### Common pitfalls
- Collecting data without analyzing it.
- Taking sources for granted without validating their reliability over time.

## 6. Correctly Identify the Enemy
**Source text:** Pages 163–164 ("Identify the Enemy")

### Step-by-step instructions
1. Define who has the power to keep you from winning.
2. Clearly distinguish between allies, neutrals, and enemies.
3. In business, the enemy is the competitor; in sports, the opponent; in education, systemic blockers.
4. Never label close friends, marriage partners, or allies as enemies.
5. Recognize when **you** are your own worst enemy (personal weaknesses, lack of confidence).

### Examples from the text
- "The enemy is never a close friend or marriage partner. The mental set of viewing someone who can be an ally as an enemy leads to actions that destroy the relationship."
- Struggles with oneself often require overcoming personal weakness or lack of confidence.

### Best practices
- **Do:** Write down who is and is not the enemy to avoid misallocation of competitive energy.
- **Do:** Seek personal coaches/gurus to overcome self-imposed limitations.
- **Don't:** Attack allies or waste resources fighting the wrong target.

### Common pitfalls
- Internal conflict—fighting teammates instead of external competitors.
- Projecting internal weaknesses onto external relationships.

## 7. Study Opponent Style and History
**Source text:** Pages 163–164 ("Understand Style")

### Step-by-step instructions
1. Learn what the opponent is doing now and what they have done in the past.
2. Analyze personalities and their backgrounds of experience.
3. Look at where they acquired expertise and how they handled similar situations.
4. Anticipate that new leaders will clone the style of their previous organizations.

### Examples from the text
- If you want to know the style of a new employee, supervisor, or leader, examine where they were previously—they will bring their personal strengths, weaknesses, and preferred systems with them.
- Frederick the Great: "A great advantage is drawn from the knowledge of your adversary, and when you know his intelligence and character you can use it to play to his weakness."

### Best practices
- **Do:** Build a historical profile of the opponent, not just a snapshot of current behavior.
- **Do:** Accept and rally around predictable style changes from new leaders rather than resisting them blindly.
- **Don't:** Assume the opponent will deviate from their established patterns without evidence.

### Common pitfalls
- Focusing only on current moves without understanding the underlying style and history.
- Underestimating a new leader because they seem unfamiliar (they are likely replicating a known system).

## 8. Concentrate on a Focal Point (Critical Mass of Resources)
**Source text:** Pages 165–167 ("Concentrate on a Focal Point")

### Step-by-step instructions
1. Determine your primary objective (the one thing worth winning).
2. Put yourself in your opponent's shoes to anticipate how they would organize an attack against you.
3. Allocate more than enough resources to the objective—seek an excess, not merely the minimum.
4. Distinguish between good ideas, important ideas, and critical ideas; focus only on the critical ones.

### Examples from the text
- Seminar exercise: Imagine you work for your strongest competitor; identify the strategies they would use to win. This reveals your own vulnerabilities and optimal attack plan.
- General Mark S. Watson (1950): "The efficient commander does not seek to use just enough means, but an excess of means."

### Best practices
- **Do:** Apply overwhelming strength across moral, mental, and physical dimensions.
- **Do:** Ruthlessly prioritize—only the critical mass gets resources.
- **Don't:** Spread resources thin across many good-but-non-critical initiatives.

### Common pitfalls
- Under-resourcing the main effort due to competing priorities.
- Mistaking activity (many initiatives) for effectiveness.

## 9. Plan Surprise and Unpredictability
**Source text:** Pages 167–168 ("Plan Surprise")

### Step-by-step instructions
1. Avoid standing still—keep moving toward your objective (standing still maximizes vulnerability).
2. Identify what your opponent least expects and design your attack around it.
3. Make your attack unpredictable in competitive situations (while remaining predictable to your own team so they can execute without hesitation).
4. Achieve timing such that the opponent becomes aware of the attack too late to react effectively.
5. Aim for the best surprise: the opponent does not even realize they were surprised.

### Examples from the text
- Frederick the Great (1747): "Everything which the enemy least expects will succeed the best."
- Sports examples: Quarterback sneak, fake pass, two-point conversion instead of field goal.
- Military examples: U.S. destroying Taliban tanks before they could fire in Afghanistan; Iraqi tank turrets blown off before crews knew Americans were present in the Gulf War.
- Office politics: Most common arena for undetected surprise attacks.

### Best practices
- **Do:** Use unpredictability against opponents; use predictability within your own organization.
- **Do:** Strike before the opponent completes their OODA loop (see Lesson 11).
- **Don't:** Tip your hand in advance through premature announcements or predictable patterns.

### Common pitfalls
- Over-surprising your own team, causing confusion and paralysis.
- Announcing strategy in meetings that opponents attend or monitor.

## 10. Follow Through and Exploit Weaknesses
**Source text:** Pages 168 ("Follow Through")

### Step-by-step instructions
1. Identify your opponent's weaknesses during planning.
2. Continue executing the action that works.
3. Monitor for the moment when the opponent adapts and begins to counter your approach.
4. Innovate before the opponent's counter becomes effective.
5. Treat follow-through as a continuous component of the attack, not a separate step.

### Examples from the text
- Throwing a ball: follow-through is not a separate step after release; it is part of the entire motion.

### Best practices
- **Do:** Sustain momentum while it is effective.
- **Do:** Plan for the end of an action's effectiveness—schedule innovation proactively.
- **Don't:** Continue a tactic past its useful life because "it worked before."

### Common pitfalls
- Complacency—assuming a winning tactic will work indefinitely.
- Failure to innovate until after the opponent has already neutralized your advantage.

## 11. Get Started Immediately (Earliest Is Best)
**Source text:** Pages 169–170 ("Get Started")

### Step-by-step instructions
1. Make a decision and begin action as early as possible.
2. Recognize that delays increase the speed and resources required later.
3. If approval processes cause delay, act first and seek forgiveness afterward rather than waiting for permission.
4. Build momentum early to compound advantages.

### Examples from the text
- "If you wait for approval, you will be late. It is often better to seek forgiveness, than to ask permission."

### Best practices
- **Do:** Bias toward action; minimize deliberation once the minimum viable analysis is complete.
- **Do:** Document decisions to support post-hoc justification when seeking forgiveness.
- **Don't:** Use perfectionism or excessive caution as an excuse for delay.

### Common pitfalls
- Analysis paralysis—waiting for perfect information that never arrives.
- Being late to a fast-moving opportunity because of bureaucratic inertia.

## 12. Generate a Mismatch by Getting Inside the Opponent's Time Cycle (OODA Loop)
**Source text:** Pages 169–170 ("Keep Ahead of Your Opponent")

### Step-by-step instructions
1. Understand the opponent's **OODA loop**: Observation → Orientation → Decision → Action.
2. Move faster than the opponent through each cycle.
3. Act repeatedly so that by the time the opponent observes your move, orients to it, decides, and acts, their response is too late.
4. Use both physical speed (fast movement, rapid iteration) and mental speed (quick decision-making).
5. Preempt the opponent's actions before they can execute.

### Examples from the text
- Fighter pilot John Boyd's theory: getting inside an opponent's OODA time cycle generates a mismatch that works in your favor.
- Sports: Teams presenting "different looks" and continually changing plays to stay ahead.
- Superior fighter parries an adversary's thrust while simultaneously launching a counter-attack within the opponent's response window.

### Best practices
- **Do:** Operate inside the opponent's decision cycle through continuous action and adaptation.
- **Do:** Use speed and maneuver as the primary generators of initial mismatch.
- **Don't:** Allow the opponent to complete their OODA loop without disruption.

### Common pitfalls
- Matching the opponent's speed instead of exceeding it.
- Focusing only on physical speed while neglecting mental agility and decision-making.

## 13. Use Speed and Maneuver to Sustain Advantage
**Source text:** Pages 169–170 ("Keep Ahead of Your Opponent" — Summary)

### Step-by-step instructions
1. Treat speed and movement as the first two essentials of any successful attack.
2. Use speed to generate the initial mismatch.
3. Use continuous maneuver to create cascading circumstances that keep you ahead.
4. Even if the opponent appears to have every advantage, a well-timed attack can uncover weakness.

### Examples from the text
- "Anytime you can preempt an opponent's action, you have a real possibility of generating a mismatch that works in your favor."
- Even against a superior opponent, operating within their response time can reveal exploitable gaps.

### Best practices
- **Do:** Prioritize agility and responsiveness over brute force when possible.
- **Do:** Design systems and processes that enable rapid iteration and reaction.
- **Don't:** Assume that having every advantage guarantees victory—timing and speed can offset material superiority.

### Common pitfalls
- Over-reliance on static plans that cannot adapt to a moving target.
- Underestimating the power of a well-timed, fast action against a slower, better-resourced opponent.
