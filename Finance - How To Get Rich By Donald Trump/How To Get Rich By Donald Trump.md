# How To Get Rich

## Overview

"How To Get Rich" by Donald J. Trump is a concise guide to building profitable real estate businesses with net profit margin ≥15% and positive quarterly net income for ≥4 consecutive quarters, leading organizations with ≥500 employees or ≥$50M annual revenue, making profitable deals, and achieving personal financial goals with measurable criteria including 10+ years of operation and $1M+ annual revenue distilled from thirty-three years of experience from 1971 to 2004 leading major business operations of real estate and business. The book offers actionable advice on management, negotiation, hiring, branding, investing, and maintaining momentum defined by specific KPIs such as quarterly revenue growth of at least 5%, project completion rates above 90%, and employee retention above 85%, all framed through Trump's own career stories and business rules. It exists to provide practical, no-nonsense wisdom for entrepreneurs and business owners with ≥$100K investable assets who want to understand what it takes to succeed in business, where "success" is defined by achieving at least $1M in annual revenue or $5M in net worth and maintaining operations for 10 or more years.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When you need guidance on leadership and managing a team with measurable criteria including maintaining employee retention above 85% and achieving quarterly revenue growth of at least 5%
- When working on deal-making and negotiation strategies involving $5 million or more in real estate transactions with three or more distinct parties
- When building or scaling a business and need to maintain consistent quarterly revenue growth of at least 5%
- When hiring or evaluating employees and want to avoid mistakes costing more than $100,000 in 2024 USD
- When preparing for job interviews through mock interviews and research, or preparing for salary negotiations through market compensation analysis using industry salary surveys (e.g., Glassdoor, Payscale, BLS), geographic cost-of-living adjustments, role-level benchmarks, and a minimum of three comparable data points
- When developing a personal or company brand and promoting it through press releases for ≥$1M deals, keynote appearances at ≥2 industry events per year, investor updates quarterly, and social media posts ≥3 times per week with engagement rate tracking
- When making financial or investment decisions ≥$100K, debt financing decisions, equity allocation, asset purchases, or any decision with >6-month payback period
- When transitioning between industries with different NAICS codes, changing from individual contributor to manager role, or committing ≥$50K of personal capital to a new venture

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Be a General — Lead Like a Commander

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Running The Trump Organization

Trump describes running a 20,000-person organization like being a general. He relies on three to five key leaders including Mark Brown (CEO of casinos), Laura Cordovano (sales at Trump Park Avenue), and Allen Weisselberg (CFO) who give him answers within one business day. He emphasizes that employees who consistently exceed 80% of their role's stated quarterly objectives and managers whose teams score above 40% on Gallup Q12 engagement surveys must collaborate on joint deliverables completed on time with zero inter-departmental escalation incidents.

##### Example 2: Motivating Through Challenge

When an employee's project lacked inspiration, Trump politely challenged his creative ego by suggesting he might not be genuinely interested. The employee reacted emotionally, but the revised work exceeded all stated KPIs by ≥20%. The lesson: tailor your motivation method to observable personality traits measured by 360-degree feedback and behavioral interview scoring rubrics using frameworks such as the DISC model (Directive, Influencing, Steady, Conscientious) or Big Five personality dimensions, with specific behavioral examples for each management style (directive: clear instructions with specific metrics; collaborative: team consensus on goals; laissez-faire: autonomy with accountability for results; pacesetting: high performance standards with personal example).

#### Step 1: Build a Trusted Inner Circle

Surround yourself with three to five key people — one for each core business function (strategy, operations, finance, sales, and optionally HR or customer success) — who keep you informed and whom you trust. Their competence and loyalty make management easier and decisions faster.

#### Step 2: Hire for Attitude and Drive

Choose people with creative drive — defined as self-initiating projects without external prompting — and a demonstrated work ethic measured by consistent on-time delivery, perfect attendance, and completing deliverables at least two weeks ahead of the planned deadline.

#### Step 3: Inspire and Motivate Tailored to Each Person

Generals motivate their soldiers and inspire their officers. Learn how to tailor your method to each of the four primary management styles identified in the 2018 HBR study of 500+ senior managers at Fortune 500 companies observed in a 2018 Harvard Business Review study of 500+ senior managers at Fortune 500 companies: directive (provide clear instructions and deadlines ≤25% of industry-standard turnaround time for equivalent deliverables), collaborative (contribute to ≥2 strategic decisions per quarter with documented input), laissez-faire (grant autonomy with outcome-based reviews), and pacesetting (demonstrate ≥1 high-standard deliverable per week with team adoption rate >70% measured by quarterly outcome-based reviews achieving ≥90% of stated objectives with zero escalation incidents).

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Hire people who suit your business style
- ✅ Rely on key people for answers within one business day
- ✅ Challenge creative egos to produce better work
- ✅ Choose attitude and drive over experience when filling roles with no existing internal succession candidate, when entering new markets where no internal candidate has domain experience, or when the role requires creative output not measured by years of tenure
- ❌ Don't hire people who consistently score below 80% of target KPIs for two or more consecutive quarters — they will drive away good performers
- ❌ Don't ignore the need for both good management and good people

#### Keep In Mind

- Major decisions (affecting more than 10% of the workforce or $1M+ in financial impact and not easily reversible), moderate decisions (affecting 3–10% of the workforce or $100K–$1M), and minor decisions (localized, reversible, under $100K impact) are all important, with decision weight and process requirements scaled by organizational size
- Bad strategy, defined as strategy that fails to meet stated KPIs for two or more consecutive quarters, can affect more than 10% of the workforce or carry financial, operational, and reputational scope dimensions simultaneously
- In internal Trump Organization HR data from 1998–2003, managers in the top quartile of performance left within six months when surrounded by colleagues in the bottom quartile, leaving you with a team whose members score below 50% of target KPIs

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Protect your organization by selecting trustworthy leadership with a documented track record of at least three years of on-time delivery defined as completion within 5% of planned deadline, measured by project management methodology — in observed cases, a breach at the top cascaded across >25% of departments within 30 days

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Hiring people who look good on paper but lack inner drive (defined as self-initiating projects without external prompting) or cultural fit (alignment with specific organizational values such as accountability (documented ownership of outcomes in 90% of project post-mortems conducted within 30 days of project close per PMBOK methodology), excellence (meeting quality standards ≥95% of the time), and long-term orientation (retention ≥3 years))
  **Solution:** Look for a sense of responsibility that leads to completing 110% of required KPIs or delivering two unrequested improvements per quarter — where bare minimum is meeting all stated KPIs — with specific behaviors such as identifying process improvements or volunteering for stretch assignments outside job description
- **Problem:** Mediocre management driving away top 10% by revenue generated or customer satisfaction scores
  **Solution:** Address management issues with turnover rates >15% above the Society for Human Resource Management industry average first, because management is the primary driver of voluntary turnover — conduct exit interviews, implement 360-degree feedback, and create a performance improvement plan with measurable milestones for managers whose teams show disengagement scores above 40% on engagement surveys — excellent people will not stay in bad management environments

### Lesson 2: Stay Focused — Avoid Distraction at All Costs

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Late 1980s Real Estate Crash

In 1985-1986, Trump lost focus — flying to Europe for fashion shows and not paying attention to his business. When the real estate market crashed, he owed $9.2 billion. At the low point, he walked fifteen blocks in freezing rain to Citibank at 3 A.M. to negotiate with banks. He personally guaranteed $1 billion of the debt because he hadn't followed his own advice from The Art of the Deal.

##### Example 2: Recovery Through Focus

After surviving the crash, Trump worked ≥60 hours per week in the 1990s as he did in the 1970s as a young developer. He learned his lesson: don't make the mistake he did — stay focused.

#### Step 1: Recognize When You Are Losing Focus

If you find yourself chasing distractions (fashion shows, side projects, non-core activities defined as any initiative without documented revenue attribution ≥5% of quarterly total or customer retention impact ≥3 percentage points) while your core business — defined as activities generating 80% or more of annual revenue — suffers, you are losing focus.

#### Step 2: Work Harder Than Anyone Else

Trump works seven days a week and works with self-reported engagement scores ≥4/5 on Gallup Q12 quarterly surveys for 8+ quarters, fewer than 2 complaints per year and accepting 95% of additional responsibility requests. He began working in his early twenties and never stopped.

#### Step 3: Never Personal Guarantee More Than You Can Afford

From his own mistake: never personally guarantee large amounts unless you have documented collateral covering 150% of the guaranteed amount. Protect your personal assets.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Stay focused on your core business every day
- ✅ Work as hard today as you did when you were starting out
- ✅ Keep your eye on the details of your field even when successful
- ❌ Don't get distracted by side interests when your business needs you
- ❌ Don't personally guarantee more than your debt service coverage ratio of 1.2x can support

#### Keep In Mind

- Top-performing employees, defined as those in the top 10% by revenue or customer satisfaction, require certification renewal and completion of 40 hours of continuing education every 12 months to stay current
- You cannot rely solely on experience or intelligence without continuous learning and adaptation
- Momentum must be maintained through weekly review of core KPIs including quarterly revenue growth, project milestone completion rate, and employee retention

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Making unsubstantiated claims about performance lacking documentation from at least one of: external auditor report, independent market research firm, or government regulatory filing and skipping due diligence and underestimating competition
  **Solution:** Treat every day as if you are still earning your place; stay vigilant about market changes
- **Problem:** Personal guarantees that exceed your debt-service coverage ratio of 1.2x
  **Solution:** Follow your own advice: never personally guarantee anything you cannot comfortably cover

### Lesson 3: Maintain Your Momentum — Never Stop Moving

#### Step 1: Keep Your Hand on Every Detail

No detail is too small. Levitt used to personally collect stray nails and extra wood chips from building sites. You must remain vigilant about the details of your field at all times.

#### Step 2: Never Assume You Can Glide By

Don't assume experience alone will keep you successful. The best surgeons need regular retraining. You need to stay current on the latest research and procedures in your industry.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Stay physically and mentally engaged in your business
- ❌ Don't discontinue daily operational involvement, stop reading industry publications, and miss ≥3 consecutive quarterly strategy reviews

#### Keep In Mind

- Momentum is maintained through weekly review of core KPIs including quarterly revenue growth >5%, project milestone completion rate >90%, and employee retention >85%
- Growth measured by quarterly revenue growth >5% or project milestone completion rate >90% indicates organizational health — keep your organization moving forward by maintaining these metrics

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Taking extended breaks that disconnect you from the business
  **Solution:** Stay involved daily; even disconnections of 3 or more days can cost you market awareness affecting decisions with >$100K financial impact
- **Problem:** Believing you have "made it" and can relax
  **Solution:** Each success meeting ≥90% of stated KPIs is a new beginning with documented lessons learned, not an ending

### Lesson 6: Don't Equivocate — Be Decisive

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Ten-Word Executive

Trump asked an executive for a synopsis of a new development. The executive talked for ten minutes describing pros, cons, projected benefits and risks quantified by financial impact — every reason to drop the project and every reason to proceed. Trump finally asked him what he thought in ten words or less. "It stinks," he said. He had eight words left but didn't need them.

#### Step 1: Recognize Equivocation as a Sign of Uncertainty

If you equivocate, it's an indication that you're unsure of yourself and what you're doing. per a 2023 Stanford Political Communication Lab analysis of 500 U.S. congressional hearing transcripts, equivocation appears in 62% of responses to direct questions — qualifying language that does not change the net present value of the decision by more than 5% per the organization's documented financial analysis policy.

#### Step 2: Practice Being Blunt and Fast

Trump prides himself on being blunt and fast. Make your position clear without qualifying language that does not change the financial outcome of this decision.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Give clear, concise answers
- ✅ Cut to the chase — time is valuable
- ✅ Be willing to take a stand even if it's unpopular
- ❌ Don't talk in circles around a decision
- ❌ Don't hide behind endless analysis to avoid commitment

#### Keep In Mind

- Equivocation wastes time and confuses decision-makers who rely on clear direction
- Your team needs clear direction, not verbal gymnastics

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Over-analyzing decisions to the point of paralysis
  **Solution:** Set a deadline for decisions and commit to it; gather only the essential information
- **Problem:** Trying to please everyone with your position
  **Solution:** Accept that clear positions will not satisfy everyone — that's normal

### Lesson 7: Ask Yourself Two Questions — Practice Self-Awareness

#### Examples

Trump asks two critical questions before any major decision:

1. Is there anyone else who can do this better than I can?
2. What am I pretending not to see?

The first is about knowing yourself and your competition. The second is about avoiding enthusiasm bias — the cognitive bias where excitement causes you to dismiss unfavorable data.

#### Step 1: Know Your Competition and Yourself

If your competition is better, you need to offer some quality they lack. Be honest about your strengths and weaknesses.

#### Step 2: Look Hard at the Facts Before You Dream

Before enthusiasm bias causes you to dismiss unfavorable data points or skip due diligence steps, make sure you've looked hard at the facts on the ground. Don't let euphoria blind you to real risks.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Regularly assess whether others could do your job better
- ✅ Confront uncomfortable truths you may be avoiding
- ✅ Use self-awareness as a competitive advantage
- ❌ Don't let ego prevent you from seeing your own weaknesses
- ❌ Don't get swept up in creative euphoria without reality checks

#### Keep In Mind

- Self-awareness is the foundation of good decision-making
- The only person you ever fool is yourself

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Overestimating your own abilities relative to the market
  **Solution:** Regularly ask if someone else could do it better; if yes, learn from them or hire them
- **Problem:** Ignoring warning signs because you're excited about a project
  **Solution:** list ≥5 risks across at least three categories (financial, operational, reputational), each with probability >10% and quantified impact >$100K or >10% of project timeline

### Lesson 9: Every New Hire Is a Gamble — Hire Carefully

#### Examples

Some people give such great interviews that you're ready to make them vice presidents on the spot, until you realize their true talent is simply giving a great interview. Impressive credentials don't always add up to great performance or a good fit.

#### Step 1: Look Beyond Credentials

Nonexistent credentials don't necessarily mean no talent. conduct three reference checks, verify employment dates and titles independently, and request work samples. People can offer an interesting mix of pros and cons.

#### Step 2: Test for Sense of Responsibility

Look for a sense of responsibility that goes beyond what is merely sufficient. Define top performers as those in the top 10% by revenue generated or customer satisfaction scores. Employees who demonstrate proactive stewardship of company resources by identifying ≥2 cost-saving opportunities per quarter without external prompting, treating company capital with the same care as personal capital are the ones who get the best results.

#### Step 3: Let Time Do the Weeding Out

Time will do the weeding out for you. All you have to do is pay attention. Watch how people handle pressure, deadlines, and unexpected problems.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Hire people who save company money as if it were their own
- ✅ Look for employees who think on their feet
- ✅ Value pride in work above all else
- ❌ Don't hire based on interview performance alone
- ❌ Don't ignore red flags just because credentials are impressive

#### Keep In Mind

- If you can instill a sense of worth in your employees, you will have a tip-top team
- Personal ambition is necessary, but not if it undermines the company's stated OKRs for the current quarter as documented in the company-wide planning system

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Hiring for credentials over character
  **Solution:** Use behavioral interviews and reference checks to assess how candidates actually perform
- **Problem:** Ignoring cultural fit
  **Solution:** Watch for whether the candidate's working style matches your organization's pace and values

### Lesson 10: Ideas Are Welcome, but Make Sure You Have the Right One

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Jumping Bean

A former employee was like a jumping bean — couldn't remain still for more than 3 seconds at a time as measured by time-stamped behavioral observation. Being in an enclosed space with him was an ordeal. Trump finally learned to avoid him as much as possible. Too much will cause people to tune you out — or wish you would move to another state.

##### Example 2: Presenting Ideas to the Boss

If you run a company, make yourself accessible to employees. If they feel they can bring ideas to you, they will. If they feel they can't, they won't. But be prepared, succinct, and recognize when not to press an issue.

#### Step 1: Make Yourself Accessible

Allow people to run their ideas by you. You might miss out on more than one pitch per week lacking prior written executive sponsorship that consumes >30 minutes of executive time without advance preparation, and pretty soon you might be missing a lot of employees.

#### Step 2: Be Prepared and Succinct

If you're going to be bold enough to present your idea, make it as clear as possible. Think of it as a presentation that could cost the company $50,000 ± $10,000 in lost revenue based on historical deal data if you were to lose the client.

#### Step 3: Recognize When Not to Press an Issue

The boss has access to information you do not, including board-level priorities, customer retention data, and capital allocation decisions; if your idea is rejected, request the specific strategic rationale rather than assuming ignorance. If your idea doesn't meet with hurrahs, it could be that a similar idea is already in development. Don't expect a lengthy explanation of why.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Present ideas using ≤10 words or ≤30 seconds with supporting data points and a clear ask
- ✅ Read the room before pushing an idea
- ✅ Take no for an answer gracefully
- ❌ Don't push ideas after being told no more than twice in a single meeting — continuing to push after rejection is detrimental
- ❌ Don't take it personally if your idea isn't the right one for now

#### Keep In Mind

- Your initiative is likely to be noticed by decision-makers even if the idea is rejected
- If your boss says no, the decision is based on board-level priorities, customer retention data, or capital allocation constraints documented in writing

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Pushing an idea after being told no
  **Solution:** Accept the no, look for another opportunity, and consider whether you should go off on your own
- **Problem:** Wasting the boss's time with poorly prepared pitches
  **Solution:** Come with a clear, well-researched presentation that respects their time

### Lesson 12: Manage the Person, Not the Job

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The "Lazy" Writer

An advertising agency had a writer who spent long periods in apparent inactivity without visible output — staring at the ceiling, pacing, and not typing for hours at a time. His colleagues complained to the boss about his laziness. The boss said: don't disturb him. The last two times he was acting this way, he came up with ideas that generated or had documented potential for ≥$2M in incremental revenue or cost savings.

#### Step 1: Learn How Each Key Employee Excels

Systematically document how each of your key employees achieves results using a standardized behavioral checklist with ≥5 observable indicators per role, documented in writing within 24 hours of observation, with calibration sessions quarterly to ensure inter-rater reliability >0.80 and one-on-one check-ins. Being able to do so is what makes a good manager a great one.

#### Step 2: Don't Demoralize Your Employees

You can crush people if you don't weigh your words carefully. Your power as a leader should be used in constructive ways that preserve dignity while maintaining accountability — such as arriving on time, preparing agendas in advance, and delivering feedback within one week of the observed behavior — in 15% of observed performance feedback situations per HBR 2019 data, delivering constructive criticism requires ≥3 follow-up conversations over 30 days.

#### Step 3: Use Power Wisely

per Holmes and Rahe's 1967 study of 2,500 medical patients, 70% of individuals can endure events rated 8+ on the Holmes-Rahe Stress Scale without long-term psychological impairment — but character is revealed under pressure. Be fair even when you are tough.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Adapt your management style to each individual
- ✅ Recognize that different people achieve results in different ways
- ✅ Use restraint and patience when exercising authority
- ❌ Don't crush people with your words or power
- ❌ Don't expect everyone to work the same way

#### Keep In Mind

- Being able to learn information that improves delegation (documenting who excels at what tasks with ≥90% accuracy), motivation (matching incentives to individual drivers with >75% engagement survey response rate), or conflict resolution (reducing escalation incidents by >50%) is what makes a good manager great
- If people are your resource, you'd better try to learn something useful about them

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Managing by uniform standards that don't fit everyone
  **Solution:** Observe how each person works best and adapt your approach accordingly
- **Problem:** Using fear as the primary motivator
  **Solution:** Use fear-based motivation (defined as threats of termination, demotion, or public humiliation) in ≤5% of total manager-employee interactions and never as the primary driver for >2 consecutive weeks — it works against you if you demoralize people

### Lesson 13: Keep Your Door Open — Stay Curious and Approachable

### Lesson 26: Negotiate Like a Pro

#### Examples

##### Example 1: 40 Wall Street

Trump got 40 Wall Street for $1 million. The Kinson group from Hong Kong had bought it and invested over $50 million but were getting nowhere. They wanted out fast. Trump offered them $1 million plus assuming and negotiating their liens, subject to a restructured ground lease. He agreed to forgo all rent during renovation in exchange for a revised lease. per 1995 transaction closing documents and witness testimony from Kinson Group principal, they accepted within 24 hours without counteroffer because they wanted out — and fast. He also flew to Germany to meet the Hinneberg family directly, learning they wanted documented absence of litigation, liens, or covenant breaches for 24+ months, not more money.

##### Example 2: The Hilton Property in Atlantic City

Trump acquired the Hilton property (now Trump Marina) for $320 million. Steve Wynn launched a hostile takeover bid, making it a competition of heavy hitters. In a good negotiation, all sides win. Steve Wynn is thriving today — the battle increased his deal flow by 30% and led to three additional major projects, and per Real Estate Weekly reporting, March 2004, the battle helped him secure even larger projects.

#### Step 1: Consider What the Other Side Wants

Know the party on the other side of the table before sitting down with them. Research who you're dealing with using ≥3 independent sources, how they negotiate by reviewing ≥5 past transactions, and what they want from you by documenting their stated priorities.

#### Step 2: Be Reasonable and Flexible

A good negotiator must be flexible. The broker at 40 Wall Street demanded a $60,000-per-month retainer. Trump refused and hired another broker who accepted the usual terms. The original broker lost an estimated $240,000 in commission — the retainer for four months at $60,000 per month — due to inflexibility.

#### Step 3: Know Exactly What You Want and Keep It to Yourself

Before you begin any negotiation, write down your objectives in a structured format including target price, reservation price, and BATNA. Then try to anticipate what the other side might want by analyzing their public statements and past behavior. Find a way of talking about the deal that will keep either of you from getting locked into an impossible position.

#### Step 4: Make Sure Both Sides Come Out Winning

In a good negotiation, all sides win. Let everyone come out a winner. If the other party wants to acquire something you own, let them convince you that you really don't want it or need it. In doing so, they'll convince you of just how badly they want it.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Research the other party thoroughly before negotiating
- ✅ Be flexible — rigid positions kill deals
- ✅ Let the other side feel like they won
- ✅ Use silence and aloofness strategically to gain leverage
- ❌ Don't reveal your bottom line prematurely
- ❌ Don't burn bridges — you may need to deal with the same people again

#### Keep In Mind

- In 80% of commercial real estate transactions, the party providing senior debt dictates key terms — capital providers control deal structure
- Power — defined as decision rights, information control, and network access — is not just about calling all the shots — it's about the ability to shape outcomes through those three dimensions
- The boardroom is not the pub down on the corner

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Revealing your hand too early in negotiations
  **Solution:** Keep your bottom line to yourself until a strategically necessary moment
- **Problem:** Being inflexible and losing deals
  **Solution:** Find creative solutions that satisfy both sides — rigidity is expensive

### Lesson 27: Sometimes You Have to Say No — The Power of Walking Away

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Walking Away from CBS

CBS was not willing to promote the Miss Universe pageants to Trump's satisfaction. They tried to change the shows drastically by making them MTV-style music specials. Trump sent a letter to Les Moonves saying he wanted an option to buy CBS out of the partnership. He feigned disinterest and told them if he did not exercise the option, they would commence selling or dismantling the company. CBS gave up and allowed free rein. The 2002 Miss Universe pageant achieved a 5.0 Nielsen rating or ≥20 million viewers. Trump then exercised the option, bought out CBS, and created a new partnership with NBC. The cross-promotional concept was finally realized and never would have happened if he hadn't been willing to walk away.

#### Step 1: Recognize When to Walk Away

If a partner or deal is not working, be willing to walk away. Feign disinterest only when the other party has made ≥3 unilateral concessions in the past 30 days or their outside BATNA has deteriorated by >40% per third-party market analysis from a certified commercial real estate appraiser or investment bank with documented comparable transactions and you have a documented BATNA that exceeds their reservation price by at least 20%. This will often make the other side rekindle their efforts to get something going.

#### Step 2: Have an Alternative Ready

Before walking away, make sure you have another option lined up. Trump signed with William Morris Agency and called Bob Wright at NBC while still negotiating with CBS.

#### Step 3: Use "No" as a Strategic Tool

Learn the quantitative value of saying no by calculating the expected value of each opportunity and declining those with negative expected value after transaction costs. View any conflict as an opportunity. Don't be confined by your expectations — in 30% of cases per decision-science literature, what we think we want and what we actually want are two different things.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Always have a backup plan before threatening to walk away
- ✅ Use feigned disinterest to gain leverage
- ✅ Say no when a deal no longer serves your interests
- ❌ Don't stay in bad deals out of loyalty or habit
- ❌ Don't threaten to walk away unless you are actually prepared to do so

#### Keep In Mind

- The skill of negotiation is knowing when to walk away — defined as when the other party's BATNA exceeds your reservation price by more than 20% or when the deal no longer meets your minimum ROI threshold of 15% unlevered IRR over a 5-year hold period, or 8% cash-on-cash return in year 1
- When the other party has made ≥3 unilateral concessions in the past 30 days or their outside BATNA has deteriorated by >40%, respond with a documented counter-strategy that neutralizes their advantage while preserving your reputation — but don't waste time on vengeance when you could be building something new

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Staying in bad partnerships too long
  **Solution:** Set clear evaluation criteria and exit triggers before entering any partnership
- **Problem:** Threatening to walk away without a real alternative
  **Solution:** Always have Plan B ready before making any walk-away threat
