# The Art of Communicating

## Overview

The Art of Communicating by Thich Nhat Hanh teaches that mindful communication grounded in deep listening, loving speech, and self-awareness is a foundation for personal well-being, healthy relationships, and peaceful communities. Drawing on Buddhist principles and practical exercises, the book reveals how every thought, word, and action builds trust and understanding or erodes them. Through deep listening, loving speech, and inner mindfulness, readers learn to transform suffering, build authentic connection, and create compassionate communities across personal, family, workplace, and community contexts.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When you need to communicate mindfully during difficult conversations, conflicts, or high-emotion situations
- When working to repair or strengthen personal relationships, family dynamics, or long-term partnerships
- When building mindful communication practices in the workplace, meetings, or professional environments
- When seeking to create compassionate community, resolve group conflicts, or contribute to social and political dialogue
- When you want to understand how your thoughts, speech, and actions affect yourself and others over time

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Communication Is Essential Food

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Mindful Consumption of Information

Everything you consume through your eyes, ears, nose, tongue, and body acts as food for your mind. Communication containing persistent insults, threats, or deliberate humiliation is toxic, much like how excessive consumption of unhealthy food affects the body. Just as moderate computer use is beneficial while excessive use harms your health, moderation in consuming information and conversation supports well-being.

##### Example 2: The Cow Without Skin (Buddha's Teaching)

A cow without skin is attacked by insects and microorganisms from soil, trees, and water. Mindfulness is your skin. Without it, you absorb toxic thoughts, speech, and actions from those around you. With mindfulness, you can protect yourself from harmful influences.

##### Example 3: The Relationship Flowers

Relationships are like flowers that need watering with love and communication. Every thought you produce feeds the relationship. When you produce thoughts of suspicion, anger, fear, or irritation, you are not nourishing the relationship. A practitioner found that focusing on nurturing positive thoughts during a one-hour drive helped improve a relationship.

#### Step 1: Evaluate Your Communication Consumption

Before consuming any content—social media, news, conversations—pause and ask whether it is growing your understanding and compassion or making you feel bad, insecure, or judgmental. If the content is toxic, limit or stop consuming it.

#### Step 2: Write with Compassion

When writing emails, letters, or notes, ensure the content is full of understanding and compassion. Even short notes can nourish both you and the recipient. During the act of writing compassionate communication, you nourish yourself.

#### Step 3: Notice Thoughts Before Speaking

Before speaking, notice whether the thought you produced is rooted in understanding (healthy) or in reactivity (unhealthy), and whether it aims to connect (compassionate) or to harm (unkind). This pause is an important foundation of mindful speech.

#### Step 4: Listen with Compassion, Not Toxins

When listening, instead of absorbing hate, anger, or frustration, actively produce compassion in yourself. Use compassion as your protection when listening to toxic conversation.

#### Step 5: Nourish Relationships Intentionally

Treat relationships as flowers needing water. Look into the nature of suffering in your relationship to identify what kinds of words and behaviors you have been offering. Stop engaging in the patterns of speech and behavior that worsen suffering, allowing it to fade.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Evaluate your communication consumption daily—assess whether what you consume grows compassion or fuels toxicity
- ✅ Write mindfully—every email or letter should be an opportunity for nourishment
- ✅ Notice your thoughts before speaking—pause to determine if a thought is healthy or unhealthy
- ✅ Listen mindfully—when others speak, don't absorb toxins; actively generate compassion
- ✅ Nourish relationships intentionally—treat loved ones like flowers needing regular watering with loving communication
- ✅ Transform in small moments—even one hour of focused loving communication can transform a relationship
- ✅ Limit technology mindfully—don't over-rely on phones and devices; they cannot fully replace authentic communication
- ✅ Protect your mind—use mindfulness as a shield against toxic external influences
- ✅ Cut off toxic nourishment—identify and stop feeding the sources of your suffering

#### Keep In Mind

- Love, respect, and friendship require ongoing acts of kindness and attention—without these, they fade
- In Buddhist teaching, all mental states are sustained by continued attention: love, hate, and suffering all require continued attention to persist
- According to Buddhist teaching, suffering is sustained by unkind thoughts, speech, and actions
- Authentic communication requires authentic content; technology cannot compensate for a blocked mind or inauthentic speech
- The mind is the most fundamental instrument for communication—when the mind is blocked, devices alone cannot restore authentic connection

#### Security & Safety Notes

- No explicit security or safety warnings are present in this lesson. The content focuses on mindful consumption of communication, using mindfulness as protective skin against toxic influences, and nourishing relationships through loving words and intentional attention.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Ingesting toxic communication unconsciously consuming hate, anger, frustration from others without protection
  **Solution:** Practice mindfulness as protective skin; evaluate what you consume and limit toxic inputs

- **Problem:** Over-reliance on technology believing phones and devices will solve loneliness or improve communication
  **Solution:** Use technology mindfully but don't put too much faith in devices; they cannot fully replace authentic communication

- **Problem:** Speaking without mindful awareness—every unkind thought or utterance feeds suffering in yourself and others
  **Solution:** Before speaking, notice whether the thought is healthy or unhealthy, compassionate or unkind

- **Problem:** Confusing connectivity with communication—checking email, texting, and posting frequently doesn't mean you're truly communicating
  **Solution:** Focus on authentic content and presence, not just frequency of contact

- **Problem:** Letting relationships die by failing to nourish love with communication, allowing judgment and anger to take over
  **Solution:** Treat relationships as flowers needing regular watering with loving communication

### Lesson 2: Self-Communication Is the Foundation of All Communication

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Disconnected Life

We walk but don't know we are walking. We are physically present but mentally elsewhere. We are alive but don't know we are alive. This illustrates how disconnected we are from ourselves, making authentic communication with others very difficult.

##### Example 2: The Body-Mind Reunion

When you breathe in mindfully, there is a happy reunion between body and mind. In some people's experience, the mind dwells on past or future thoughts while the body remains in the present, but mindful breathing brings them together.

##### Example 3: Nelson Mandela's Wish

When Nelson Mandela was freed from prison and came to France, a journalist asked what he would most like to do. Mandela replied, "Sit down and do nothing." This illustrates the value of simply sitting and being with yourself.

##### Example 4: The Woman from Washington, D.C.

A woman planned to commit suicide because of suffering in her relationship. After learning to listen to her own suffering, she discovered her own responsibility, and used loving speech to transform her marriage.

#### Step 1: Stop and Communicate with Yourself

When you feel disconnected from yourself, stop whatever you are doing, sit down, and connect with yourself. In as little as 30 seconds of sitting and breathing, you can connect with your body, feelings, emotions, and perceptions.

#### Step 2: Pause Before Sending or Speaking

Before pressing send on a text or email, or before picking up the phone, take at least one full in-breath and out-breath. This pause increases the chance that your next conversation will be more compassionate and understanding.

#### Step 3: Go Home to Yourself Through Mindful Breathing

In this teaching, "home" means a state of inner awareness. Returning to it means pausing to notice your breath, acknowledging the present moment without resistance. Even if your inner state feels cluttered, accept it, because you may have been disconnected from yourself for a while. Begin to tidy up your inner state with your in-breath and out-breath.

#### Step 4: Practice the Breathing Gatha

Silently say to yourself while breathing:
- "Breathing in, I know I'm breathing in."
- "Breathing out, I know I'm breathing out."

Follow the air entering and leaving your body throughout the entire breath.

#### Step 5: Turn Off Your Phone for Self-Communication

To communicate with yourself, leave your telephone behind. Turn off your phone when attending a meeting or event, and do the same when communicating with yourself. Using a phone to browse, text, or check notifications during self-reflection can interfere with sitting mindfully. The mindful phone practices taught in this book—such as hearing the phone as a bell of mindfulness—are intentional uses, not distractions.

#### Step 6: Practice Nonthinking and Nontalking

Stop the internal dialogue and refrain from speaking, as the Zen tradition teaches. When you reduce discursive thinking and talking and listen mindfully to yourself, you notice your greater capacity for joy and suffering present in your life that you had hidden under constant talking and texting.

#### Step 7: Don't Run Away from Suffering

When feelings of suffering come up, don't try to run away from them. Continue to follow your in-breath and out-breath. Don't tell fear or anger to go away; recognize them, acknowledge them. Turn your attention toward the feeling with the same care you would give a distressed child.

#### Step 8: Make Time to Sit Daily

Make time to sit down and do nothing, even if only for 5 minutes a day. When you are restless and don't know what to do, that is a good time to sit down. When you are peaceful, sitting is also good as a way of nurturing the practice.

#### Step 9: Practice Communicating with Your Body

When you sit and breathe mindfully, your mind and body become aligned in awareness. Say to yourself:
- "Breathing in, I'm aware of my body."
- "Breathing out, I release the tension I notice in my body."

#### Step 10: Practice Mindful Walking

When you walk mindfully, integrate your breath with each step and focus on your foot connecting with the ground. Be aware that you are making a step, and reduce discursive thinking. While walking, you can say to yourself: "I have arrived. I am home."

#### Step 11: Listen Deeply to Your Own Suffering

Suffering figuratively tries to communicate with you; listen to it. When you feel loneliness, sadness, fear, or anxiety, continue following your in-breath and out-breath. Don't push these feelings away; embrace them with mindfulness.

#### Step 12: Cultivate Self-Understanding Before Understanding Others

Ask yourself: "Do I understand myself enough? Do I understand my suffering and its roots?" Difficulty accepting yourself can make it harder to extend genuine care to others. Once you understand your own suffering, you suffer less and can more easily understand another person's suffering.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Stop and breathe before communicating—take at least one full in-breath and out-breath before sending a digital message or making a call
- ✅ Practice mindful awareness daily—sit down and connect with yourself for just a few seconds or minutes
- ✅ Write mindfully—use emails, texts, and letters as opportunities for nourishing communication
- ✅ Turn off your phone for inner communication—don't try to communicate with yourself through a device
- ✅ Practice mindful breathing throughout the day—follow your in-breath and out-breath; use the gatha to remind yourself
- ✅ Make time to sit—even a few minutes a day of sitting and doing nothing nurtures the practice
- ✅ Practice mindful walking—integrate breath with each step, reduce discursive thinking, and say "I have arrived. I am home."
- ✅ Don't run from suffering—when suffering arises, stay with your breath and acknowledge it without judgment
- ✅ Hold suffering tenderly—turn your attention toward the feeling with the same care you would give a distressed child
- ✅ Generate mindfulness energy—practice breathing and walking to generate enough energy to be with your suffering
- ✅ Listen deeply to your own suffering before trying to help others—understanding your own suffering is one important foundation for understanding others
- ✅ Communicate with your body—pause to release tension and bring mind and body together
- ✅ Connect with the earth while walking—feel the ground beneath your feet as part of healing alienation

#### Keep In Mind

- In this teaching, "home" refers to a state of inner awareness; returning to it means pausing to notice your breath, acknowledging the present moment without resistance
- Freedom here means release from reactive patterns, available each time you notice your breath; this kind of freedom comes when we release our suffering and return to ourselves
- Suffering figuratively tries to communicate with you—listen to it rather than running from it
- In Buddhist teaching, personal suffering is understood as connected to generational patterns from your parents and ancestors; if you transform your suffering, you help break these generational patterns for yourself and those who follow
- A misperception can arise very quickly, and decisions made from misperception can lead to harm for yourself or others
- If you haven't understood your own suffering and your interpretations of events, which may or may not match reality, you will find it very difficult to understand another person's suffering

#### Security & Safety Notes

- No explicit security or safety warnings are present in this lesson. The content focuses on listening to your own suffering, practicing inner mindfulness through breathing and sitting, overcoming overthinking and device addiction, and understanding yourself before seeking to understand others.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Living disconnected from yourself—walking, being here, and being alive without knowing it; losing yourself throughout the day
  **Solution:** Practice mindful awareness by stopping and connecting with your breath and body throughout the day

- **Problem:** Rushing communication—sending emails and texts within a few minutes of writing them without taking a mindful breath
  **Solution:** Before pressing send or picking up the phone, take at least one full in-breath and out-breath

- **Problem:** Using technology for self-communication—believing you can communicate with yourself through a phone or device
  **Solution:** Turn off your phone; self-communication requires being present with yourself, not through a device

- **Problem:** Overthinking and overtalking—dwelling on the past, trying to control the future, generating misperceptions, and worrying about what others are thinking
  **Solution:** Practice nonthinking and nontalking; reduce discursive thinking and talking to listen to yourself

- **Problem:** Covering up suffering—using shopping, binge-eating, watching distressing content, and constantly checking devices to avoid encountering inner suffering
  **Solution:** When suffering arises, stay with your breath and acknowledge it without judgment

- **Problem:** Running from suffering—trying to get away from or avoid suffering instead of listening to and understanding it
  **Solution:** Continue following your in-breath and out-breath; turn your attention toward feelings with care

- **Problem:** Judging or pushing away feelings—telling fear or anger to go away instead of acknowledging them
  **Solution:** Recognize and acknowledge feelings without judging them or pushing them away

- **Problem:** Neglecting the body—spending more than three consecutive hours at the computer and forgetting you have a body until bodily discomfort disrupts your focus
  **Solution:** Take breaks and return to your body; practice mindful breathing with awareness of your body

- **Problem:** Believing you understand others without self-understanding—thinking you know and understand loved ones without having understood your own suffering and perceptions
  **Solution:** Ask yourself "Do I understand myself enough?" before trying to understand others

- **Problem:** Hating yourself—if you cannot accept yourself, you may find it very difficult to love another person
  **Solution:** Practice self-understanding and self-love as the foundation for loving others

### Lesson 3: The Two Keys to Compassionate Communication

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Vietnamese Couple Tragedy

A husband returns from war and his wife brings their son. The boy says the man is not his daddy because he saw another man visiting at night (the wife was talking to her shadow to comfort her son). Pride prevented both from asking questions. The wife drowned herself. The tragedy may have been averted if either had asked for help or practiced loving speech.

##### Example 2: The Man Reconciling with His Father

At a retreat, a man was given a midnight deadline to reconcile with his father with whom he had been angry for years. When he called his father the next morning, he naturally used loving speech without trying. His father told him about his suffering for the first time.

##### Example 3: The Times of India Editors

During the 2008 Mumbai terrorist attacks, the author advises journalists to report truthfully but in a way that avoids inflammatory language, sensational headlines, and unverified claims that intensify fear and anger in readers. They should help people understand why things happen.

##### Example 4: The Israeli-Palestinian Retreat

Groups of Israelis and Palestinians at Plum Village practice mindful communication. Initially they carry fear, anger, and mistrust. Through deep listening and loving speech, they recognize each other's suffering and many report feeling greater compassion for one another.

#### Step 1: Establish Your Own Home Through Self-Communication

Reserve time alone each day for communicating with yourself. Practice nonthinking and nontalking to listen to yourself. Practice mindful breathing and mindful walking. Be aware of your in-breath and out-breath before communicating with others.

#### Step 2: Begin Every Communication with the Buddha Nature Practice

Remember at the beginning of every communication that there is a Buddha inside each person. Join your palms and bow with respect (or look them in the eye and smile). Breathe in and out mindfully while bringing your hands together. Form a lotus flower with your hands as an offering. Silently say: "A lotus for you. A Buddha to be."

#### Step 3: Practice Deep Listening (First Key)

Listen first, speak second. Practice mindfulness of suffering—recognizing and embracing suffering in yourself and the other person. Listen with the primary intention of helping the other person feel understood rather than judged. Listen without interrupting, even if the person says things that are factually incorrect.

Say to yourself: "I am listening to this person with only one purpose: to give this person a chance to feel understood."

#### Step 4: Practice Loving Speech (Second Key)

Speak in a way that helps others recognize the suffering inside themselves and in you. Be skillful with your words so the listener is not caught in wrong perceptions. Words have consequences—what you say should aim to reduce suffering and promote understanding.

#### Step 5: Check If You Understand the Other Person Enough

Ask yourself: "Can I summarize this person's perspective accurately?" Ask the other person: "Do you feel I understand your situation?" Don't wait until the other person shows signs of anger, such as a raised voice or tense body language, to ask this question.

#### Step 6: Practice the Four Elements of Right Speech

1. **Tell the truth** — Don't lie or deliberately distort facts
2. **Don't exaggerate** — Refrain from inflating the significance of events beyond what the facts support
3. **Be consistent** — No double-talk or saying different things to different people about the same situation for personal gain
4. **Use peaceful language** — Refrain from words that insult, threaten, demean, or condemn others

#### Step 7: Apply the Four Criteria to Your Speech

1. **Speak the language of the world** — Use terms your listener can understand, based on their daily experience and background
2. **Speak according to the understanding of the listener** — Keep truthful content the same but adapt your phrasing and pacing to the person's perspective and current capacity to receive it
3. **Prescribe the right communication strategy for the specific difficulty** — Adapt your words to the other person's background and the nature of the conflict, as a doctor chooses a treatment for a specific condition
4. **Reflect the absolute truth** — Be humble and examine the situation carefully to discover how to talk about difficult truths

#### Step 8: Handle Anger Skillfully Before Communicating

Don't communicate when you are angry—you are not thinking clearly. Don't suppress anger—it will lead to an explosive outburst if ignored. Don't express anger physically—this rehearses and nourishes anger. Go home to yourself and take care of your anger. Use mindful breathing and mindful walking.

#### Step 9: Reconcile in Families and Relationships

Don't wait for the other person to change—change yourself first. Release the habit of judging others and return to your own inner dialogue. Don't try to force the other person to change. Give yourself a timeframe for making a start. Practice mindful breathing, mindful walking, and mindfulness in daily activities.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Reserve time for self-communication daily—communicate with yourself before communicating with others
- ✅ Begin every encounter with a mindful breath—breathe before speaking, recognize the Buddha nature in others
- ✅ Deep listening first—listen before you speak. Deep listening comes first in conflicts.
- ✅ Listen without interrupting—even if the person says things that are factually incorrect, continue to listen. After both parties are calm, correct misperceptions at a suitable moment rather than during the listening process.
- ✅ Listen with one purpose—to help the other person feel understood. Say this as a reminder to yourself.
- ✅ Pause if too agitated to listen—"I want to listen to you when I'm at my best. Would it be all right if we continued later?"
- ✅ Practice the 24-hour rule—within 24 hours of feeling hurt, practice the fourth mantra with the person who caused the hurt by honestly sharing your suffering using the mantra's words
- ✅ Check your understanding periodically—ask "Do I understand you enough?" and "Do you think I understand me enough?"
- ✅ Adapt speech to the listener—speak according to the person's background, ability, and wisdom level
- ✅ Tell the truth skillfully—there are multiple ways to convey the truth. Find an honest and respectful way.
- ✅ Don't exaggerate—stick to the truth, don't inflate small matters beyond their actual significance
- ✅ Be consistent—don't use forked tongue or double-talk
- ✅ Use peaceful language—refrain from violent, condemning, abusive, humiliating, or judgmental words
- ✅ Practice mindful breathing when angry—don't communicate when angry. Calm yourself first before responding.
- ✅ Give yourself a timeframe for reconciliation—set a timeframe, such as 24 hours, to begin reconciling with someone with whom you have difficulty
- ✅ Change yourself, not the other person—don't try to force the other person to change. Begin with your own inner transformation.
- ✅ Don't interrupt during conflict resolution—let the other person finish speaking before you respond

#### Keep In Mind

- Love is born from understanding—each of us is hungry for understanding. Compassion and love are born from understanding. In this tradition, happiness is defined as the capacity to understand and to love. Without understanding and love, no lasting happiness is possible.
- Wrong perception can cause great suffering. We must ask ourselves, "Are you sure your perception is right?"
- According to Buddhist teaching, suffering is passed down from ancestors and parents. Unless you understand and transform your own suffering, it tends to be passed to future generations. Absolute truth refers to universal truths not dependent on perspective, while relative truth refers to common language based on perspective, such as up and down. Absolute truths must be conveyed skillfully based on the listener's capacity.
- Deep listening means examining the nature of suffering closely. The listener must be careful not to get caught in the details of words alone.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- **Violent or abusive situations:** "This is true if we're in a violent or abusive situation. It's important that we be in a place where we feel safe and not vulnerable." The content acknowledges that separation or divorce may be necessary in abusive situations, but emphasizes that even then, compassionate communication practices can help manage ongoing interactions (especially when children or shared matters connect you).

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Communicating when angry—you aren't thinking clearly when angry. Acting while angry escalates suffering.
  **Solution:** Don't communicate when angry. Practice mindful breathing and return to yourself to take care of your anger first.

- **Problem:** Suppressing anger—suppressed anger will lead to an explosive outburst if ignored. You need to get genuinely in touch with it.
  **Solution:** Handle anger with tenderness through mindfulness. Treat your anger as yourself, with compassion rather than violence.

- **Problem:** Expressing anger physically—shouting or hitting a pillow rehearses and nourishes anger rather than transforming it.
  **Solution:** Use the woodstove analogy: opening windows doesn't fix a defective woodstove. You must address the root causes of anger, not just release the immediate emotion.

- **Problem:** Interrupting during deep listening—interrupting transforms the session into a debate and ruins everything.
  **Solution:** Listen without interrupting, even if the person says things that are factually incorrect. After the person has fully expressed themselves and both of you are calm, correct the misperception at a suitable moment so the correction does not reopen the conflict.

- **Problem:** Speaking before listening—talking first doesn't usually work. Deep listening needs to come first.
  **Solution:** Practice deep listening first; listen with the primary intention of helping the other person feel understood.

- **Problem:** Using double-talk or forked tongue—speaking differently to different people for personal gain causes division and suffering.
  **Solution:** Be consistent in your speech. No double-talk.

- **Problem:** Exaggerating—making small things seem much bigger than they are takes you away from truth and destroys trust.
  **Solution:** Stick to the truth; don't inflate small matters beyond their actual significance.

- **Problem:** Using violent or judgmental language—wrong speech (cruel, insulting, condemning) causes suffering (ill-being).
  **Solution:** Use peaceful language; refrain from words that insult, threaten, demean, or condemn others.

- **Problem:** Waiting for the other person to change—if you wait for the other person to change, you may spend all your time waiting.
  **Solution:** Begin with your own transformation. Become master of your own responses rather than trying to force others to change.

- **Problem:** Pride preventing reconciliation—pride can prevent you from asking for help, leading to tragedy (as in the Vietnamese couple story).
  **Solution:** Don't let pride stop you from asking for help or acknowledging difficulties.

- **Problem:** Not looking at your own suffering first—if you haven't understood your own suffering, you will find it very difficult to understand another person's.
  **Solution:** Ask yourself "Do I understand myself enough?" before trying to understand others.

- **Problem:** Losing compassion during listening—without practicing mindfulness of compassion, you can lose the ability to listen when faced with wrong perceptions or bitterness.
  **Solution:** Practice mindfulness of compassion; listen with the primary intention of helping the other person feel understood.

- **Problem:** Neglecting self-communication—without the first step of connecting with yourself, connecting deeply with others becomes very difficult.
  **Solution:** Reserve time alone each day for communicating with yourself before communicating with others.

### Lesson 4: The Six Mantras of Loving Speech

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Elizabeth and Her Sister

Elizabeth stopped lecturing and started using Mantra 2 ("I'm really happy that you're here") to express heartfelt appreciation, transforming her relationship with her sister.

##### Example 2: Elizabeth and Her Husband

When hurt, instead of punishing, Elizabeth practiced Mantra 4 carefully ("You said this thing to me that I really don't understand. What was that about?") and discovered his remarks were often about something else entirely, opening a door to his world.

##### Example 3: Elizabeth and the Gardener

After being scolded for picking rose petals, Elizabeth practiced Mantra 3 ("I understand the situation better now, and I won't touch the flowers in the courtyard if you prefer") and Mantra 1 ("I'm here for you") by offering to water the roses during the gardener's absence.

##### Example 4: Friend at Retreat

A retreat participant adapted Mantra 4 with roommates by sharing that he didn't need to talk, just needed space. This was an adapted expression of the spirit of Mantra 4—communicating his need honestly so roommates would understand and not take it personally.

#### Step 1: Prepare Before Any Mantra

Begin with mindful breathing to bring about your calm, focused presence. Breathe in and out three times before speaking any mantra. Look into the other person's eyes. Say the mantra in a calm, present state. Your calm will be communicated to the other person.

#### Step 2: Practice Mantra 1 — "I am here for you."

Practice mindful breathing and mindful walking to bring body and mind together. Offer your calm, focused presence—undivided, stable, at ease, and open—rather than material gifts. Use with yourself as well: "I am here for you" means you are there for yourself. You can use this mantra over the phone—hold the phone, breathe, and say it. Do not wait for the other person to reciprocate. Both parties benefit immediately.

#### Step 3: Practice Mantra 2 — "I know you are there, and I am very happy."

Only practice after you have established a calm, focused presence via Mantra 1. Acknowledge the other person's presence as precious to your happiness. Imagine the person has moved away or passed on to remind yourself of the gift of their presence. Practice several times a day—before work, at dinner, over the phone, or by email.

#### Step 4: Practice Mantra 3 — "I know you suffer, and that is why I am here for you."

Use when you notice the other person is suffering. You do not need to fix the problem—just be present. In this tradition, love is expressed through mindfulness. When your loved ones are suffering, just being present can help reduce their suffering.

#### Step 5: Practice Mantra 4 — "I suffer. Please help."

Use when you suffer and perceive that the other person has caused your suffering. Write the sentence on a piece of paper the size of a credit card and put it in your wallet. Do not sulk or pretend you do not suffer when asked. Do not punish the other person by saying "Leave me alone. I can manage without you."

Extended version: "I suffer. I want you to know it. I don't understand why you did or said what you did. So please explain. I need your help."

Three-sentence breakdown:
1. "I suffer and I want you to know" — share your suffering.
2. "I am doing my best" — practice mindfulness, look deeply into your suffering, and consider whether your perception is mistaken.
3. "Please help" — acknowledge you may not be able to figure it out alone.

Plum Village practice: Allow yourself 24 hours to process and express your suffering before moving toward resolution, recognizing that indefinite suffering harms both yourself and your relationships. If not calm enough within 24 hours, write the mantra on paper and leave it on the other person's desk.

#### Step 6: Practice Mantra 5 — "This is a happy moment."

Use when you are with someone you care about. Recognize conditions for happiness available in the present moment. You can breathe easily, have each other, have blue sky and the earth supporting you. Mindfulness helps you recognize these existing conditions.

#### Step 7: Practice Mantra 6 — "You are partly right."

Use when someone praises or criticizes you. When praised: "You are partly right. You know that I have other qualities as well" — retain humility. When criticized: "Darling, you are partly right, because I also have good qualities in me." This reflects reality—you don't lie and don't fall into false humility. Accept yourself with all weaknesses and strengths.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Begin every interaction with mindful breathing to cultivate a calm, focused presence
- ✅ Breathe in and out three times before speaking any mantra
- ✅ Look into the other person's eyes when delivering a mantra
- ✅ Practice mindful breathing and mindful walking to restore a calm, focused presence
- ✅ Do not wait for reciprocation of mantras
- ✅ Practice mantras consistently throughout the day
- ✅ Use mantras before work, at dinner, over the phone, or by email
- ✅ When suffering, practice looking deeply into the roots of your suffering
- ✅ Consider that your perception of being hurt may be mistaken
- ✅ Accept yourself with all weaknesses and strengths, and try to improve slowly at your own pace
- ✅ Accept others without judgment, recognizing they also have positive qualities
- ✅ Use loving speech instead of authority, especially with children
- ✅ Give loved ones space to listen to themselves
- ✅ Build a home base within yourself before trying to help others or society

#### Keep In Mind

- A calm, focused presence is the best gift you can give a loved one. More precious than material things. Requires mindfulness to restore body and mind to the here and now.
- Mindfulness is the foundation of love. You may find it very difficult to love properly and deeply without mindfulness.
- When you produce a mantra, both you and the other person benefit. The effect is twofold.
- Conditions for happiness are available now if you can recognize them through mindful awareness.
- Looking deeply: investigate the real cause of suffering rather than assuming the other person is at fault.
- A true inner home is an important base for relationships. From this base, you can help build a compassionate community.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- **24-Hour Suffering Rule (Plum Village practice):** The Plum Village practice allows 24 hours for processing suffering before practicing the fourth mantra. If you cannot calm down within 24 hours, write the mantra down and leave it on the other person's desk so it is sure to be seen.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Punishing the other person—when suffering, withdrawing or acting self-sufficiently to make the other person feel abandoned. Withholding presence or communication as a way to make the other person suffer is a form of indirect punishment that blocks honest dialogue.
  **Solution:** Use the fourth mantra "I suffer. Please help." Share your suffering rather than punishing.

- **Problem:** Pretending you don't suffer—responding "Suffer? Why should I be suffering?" when asked, which is untruthful and pushes people away.
  **Solution:** Acknowledge your suffering honestly. True communication requires authentic content.

- **Problem:** Saying "Leave me alone. I can manage without you"—this is a mistake; when we suffer, we need others.
  **Solution:** Use the fourth mantra extended version to express your need for help and understanding.

- **Problem:** Trying to fix instead of being present—when someone is suffering, the impulse is to fix it, but true love is simply being there.
  **Solution:** Use Mantra 3: "I know you suffer, and that is why I am here for you." Be present without trying to fix.

- **Problem:** Ignoring presence—not paying attention to the person you love, making them feel unloved.

#### Step 6: Transform Yesterday's Unskillful Thoughts with Today's Skillful Ones

Produce a thought in the opposite direction of yesterday's unskillful thought. If yesterday you produced a thought of anger and hate, today produce a thought of compassion and tolerance. Trust that the new thought can quickly catch up with yesterday's thought and neutralize it. Use right communication today to heal the past, enjoy the present, and prepare the ground for a good future.

#### Keep In Mind

- Karma means "action" in Buddhist teaching and refers not just to bodily action but to what we express with our bodies, words, thoughts, and intentions. Karma is the triple action of thoughts, speech, and bodily actions.
- We are our actions—what we do with our body, words, and mind. Our communications can continue to influence others beyond our physical existence; they do not disappear when our physical bodies disintegrate.
- The effects of our thinking, speech, and physical actions can ripple outward into the world.
- Everything we say and do bears our signature figuratively; we cannot disown it. We are responsible for our own communication.
- Imagine a nonlocal bank account in which we deposit every word, thought, and physical action. Nothing is lost; the account certainly exists as a metaphor, but its nature is nonlocal.
- We are like a cloud that produces rain. Through the rain, the cloud continues to affect crops, trees, and rivers even after the cloud is no longer floating in the sky. Likewise, everything we produce in terms of thought, speech, and action can continue to influence others even after our bodies disintegrate.
- The past is not exactly gone; it is still there disguised in the present moment. Suffering from the past can be touched and transformed in the present.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Thinking that communication is neutral—believing words and thoughts have no lasting effect
  **Solution:** Recognize that every thought, word, and action can influence others and continues beyond your physical existence.

- **Problem:** Disowning one's thoughts, words, or actions by saying "That's not my thought"
  **Solution:** Accept responsibility for all your communication. You cannot disown what you have produced.

- **Problem:** Speaking out of a desire to punish—using communication as a weapon rather than a tool for healing
  **Solution:** Before speaking or writing, check whether your words come from compassion or from a desire to punish.

- **Problem:** Producing thoughts of hatred, anger, or despair that negatively affect body and mind
  **Solution:** Cultivate thoughts of understanding, forgiveness, and compassion. Produce a thought in the opposite direction of yesterday's unskillful thought.

- **Problem:** Believing that past unskillfulness cannot be rectified because the person is no longer alive
  **Solution:** Use the practice of mindful breathing and recognition to heal past harm. The person you harmed figuratively is present in every cell of your body.

- **Problem:** Assuming the past is completely gone and cannot be transformed
  **Solution:** The past is not exactly gone; it is still there disguised in the present moment. Suffering from the past can be touched and transformed in the present.
