# Chapter 4: GO

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Make a Dent in the Universe
**Context:** To do great work, you need to feel that you're making a meaningful difference and putting a dent in the universe. This doesn't require curing cancer—your efforts need to feel valuable so that customers say "this makes my life better" and people would notice if you stopped.

**Examples:**
- Craigslist demolished the traditional classified-ad business with just a few dozen employees, generates tens of millions in revenue, and disrupted the entire newspaper industry
- The Drudge Report is run by one guy on a simple page, yet it has had a huge impact on the news industry and is the go-to place for new stories for TV producers, radio hosts, and reporters

**Steps:**
1. Identify what would make your work feel valuable and meaningful
2. Ensure customers would say your work makes their life better
3. Build something people would notice if it disappeared
4. Maintain urgency—you don't have forever; this is your life's work
5. Reject building "just another me-too product" and instead shake things up
6. Don't wait for someone else to make the change you want to see
7. Remember you don't need a huge team to make a difference

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Do something that matters rather than something generic
- ✅ Aim to destroy old models that have been around for decades in your industry
- ✅ Keep urgency front of mind—this is your legacy
- ✅ Believe that small teams can disrupt large, established systems

**Keep In Mind:**
- You don't have to find the cure for cancer to make a meaningful impact
- Your efforts need to feel valuable to you personally
- People will notice if you stop doing what you do
- This is your life's work—act accordingly
- Small groups can out-innovate and out-disrupt large incumbents

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Building just another me-too product without differentiation
  **Solution:** Focus on what will make your work genuinely matter and create real change in your industry
- **Problem:** Waiting for someone else to make the change you want to see
  **Solution:** Take ownership and build it yourself—don't wait for permission or a bigger team

**Key Terms:**
- Dent in the Universe: Making a meaningful, noticeable impact with your work
- Legacy: What you leave behind through your work and actions
- Disruption: Replacing established business models with new approaches

### Lesson 2: Scratch Your Own Itch
**Context:** The easiest and most straightforward way to create a great product or service is to make something you want to use yourself. This lets you design what you know and immediately assess whether what you're making is any good.

**Examples:**
- 37signals built Highrise (contact-management software) because they needed a way to track whom they talked to, what they said, and when to follow up—no focus groups or market studies needed
- James Dyson invented the bagless vacuum cleaner after experiencing firsthand that his bag vacuum kept losing suction power due to dust clogging the pores
- Vic Firth began making better drumsticks while playing timpani for the Boston Symphony Orchestra when commercial sticks didn't measure up; he dropped a bunch on the floor and matched them by moisture content, weight, density, and pitch
- Bill Bowerman, a track coach, poured rubber into a family waffle iron to create Nike's famous waffle sole because his team needed better, lighter running shoes
- Mary Kay Wagner knew her skin-care products were great because she used them herself on her own skin

**Steps:**
1. Identify a real problem you experience firsthand
2. Build a solution that solves that exact problem for yourself
3. Test and iterate based on your own usage—you'll know immediately if it's good
4. Avoid focus groups, market studies, or middlemen when possible
5. Look for other people with the same problem—you've likely exposed a huge market
6. Fall in love with the problem because you know it intimately
7. Use your own product/service regularly to assess quality directly rather than by proxy

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Solve a problem you personally have
- ✅ Make hundreds of daily decisions based on firsthand knowledge, not guesswork
- ✅ Use your own product to validate quality
- ✅ Leverage the intimate knowledge of the problem and solution
- ✅ Expect to work on this for years—make sure you care about it

**Keep In Mind:**
- When you solve someone else's problem, you're "stabbing in the dark" with hundreds of decisions daily
- When you solve your own problem, "the light comes on" and you know exactly what the right answer is
- There's no substitute for caring deeply about the problem you're solving
- You'll (hopefully) be working on this for years or the rest of your life—it better be something you care about
- Others will need exactly what you need—you're not alone in your problem

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Building for imaginary or hypothetical user needs without experiencing the pain yourself
  **Solution:** Only build products you would personally use and that solve a problem you've actually experienced
- **Problem:** Relying on focus groups and market studies instead of your own judgment
  **Solution:** Trust your firsthand experience—if the product is great for you, it's likely great for others with the same problem
- **Problem:** Building something you don't care about deeply
  **Solution:** Only commit to problems you're passionate about solving, since you'll be working on them for a long time

**Key Terms:**
- Scratch Your Own Itch: Solving a personal pain point by building a product or service for yourself first
- Focus Groups: Traditional market research method often unnecessary when you're solving your own problem
- Direct Assessment: Evaluating product quality firsthand through personal use rather than through proxy metrics

### Lesson 3: Start Making Something
**Context:** Ideas are cheap and plentiful—what matters is execution. Having an idea for something like eBay is worthless without actually building it. The original pitch idea is a negligible part of a business; the real question is how well you execute.

**Examples:**
- Stanley Kubrick advised aspiring filmmakers: "Get hold of a camera and some film and make a movie of any kind at all"—start creating, because the most important thing is to begin
- People who say "I had the idea for eBay; if only I had acted on it, I'd be a billionaire" are delusional—having the idea has nothing to do with actually creating eBay

**Steps:**
1. Stop talking about ideas and start building something
2. Get a camera, hit Record, and start shooting (or equivalent first action in your field)
3. Test whether your idea has real value by trying to sell it
4. If you can't sell it, it's not as valuable as you think
5. Begin now—don't wait for the perfect moment or conditions
6. Focus on execution quality over idea originality

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Recognize that ideas are cheap and everyone has one
- ✅ Prioritize execution over ideation
- ✅ Start creating immediately, even imperfectly
- ✅ Test your idea's value by attempting to sell it
- ✅ Don't let delusions about idea value prevent action

**Keep In Mind:**
- What you do is what matters, not what you think, say, or plan
- Until you actually start making something, your brilliant idea is just an idea
- The most important thing is to begin
- Execution is what separates success from fantasy
- The original pitch idea is almost negligible compared to execution quality

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Believing having the idea is equivalent to building the thing
  **Solution:** Realize that execution is 99% of the work—ideas are a tiny fraction of business success
- **Problem:** Waiting for perfect conditions or complete planning before starting
  **Solution:** Start making something right now, even if it's imperfect—action matters more than preparation

**Key Terms:**
- Execution: The actual building and implementation of an idea, which is far more important than the idea itself
- Idea Value: The perceived worth of a concept before it's been built or tested

### Lesson 4: No Time Is No Excuse
**Context:** The most common excuse people give is "There's not enough time." But there's always enough time if you spend it right. You don't have to quit your day job—start working on your project at night, squeeze out a few extra hours a week, and use that time to test whether your excitement is real or just a passing phase.

**Examples:**
- Instead of watching TV or playing World of Warcraft, work on your idea
- Instead of going to bed at ten, go to bed at eleven
- A few extra hours a week is enough to get something going

**Steps:**
1. Audit how you spend your evenings and free time
2. Identify low-value activities (TV, gaming, etc.) that can be replaced with project work
3. Make small schedule adjustments—stay up an hour later, wake up earlier, or use weekends
4. Start small: a few extra hours per week is sufficient to test your idea
5. Hang onto your day job while building on the side
6. Test whether your excitement is genuine or just a passing phase
7. If it doesn't pan out, you didn't risk or lose anything beyond a bit of time

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Squeeze out a few extra hours a week, not all-nighters or 16-hour days
- ✅ Keep your day job while testing your idea on the side
- ✅ Use low-value time slots (evenings, weekends) for project work
- ✅ Test your commitment before making big life changes
- ✅ Take responsibility for making your dreams come true

**Keep In Mind:**
- When you want something bad enough, you make the time regardless of other obligations
- Most people just don't want it bad enough—then they protect their ego with the excuse of time
- The perfect time never arrives—you're always too young, old, busy, or broke
- If you constantly fret about timing things perfectly, they'll never happen
- You didn't risk or lose anything other than a bit of time if it doesn't work out
- It's entirely your responsibility to make your dreams come true

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Using "no time" as an excuse to avoid starting
  **Solution:** Audit your time honestly—replace low-value activities (TV, gaming) with project work; a few hours a week is enough to start
- **Problem:** Waiting for the perfect time to start
  **Solution:** Accept that the perfect time never comes—start now with whatever time you have available
- **Problem:** Quitting your day job before validating your idea
  **Solution:** Keep your job and work on your project at night; you haven't risked anything if it doesn't pan out

**Key Terms:**
- Time Excuse: The common rationalization that there aren't enough hours to pursue a project
- Passing Phase: A temporary interest that fades—side projects help distinguish real commitment from fleeting enthusiasm

### Lesson 5: Draw a Line in the Sand
**Context:** Great businesses have a point of view, not just a product or service. You need to believe in something, have a backbone, know what you're willing to fight for, and show the world. A strong stand is how you attract superfans who point to you, defend you, and spread the word more passionately than any advertising could.

**Examples:**
- 37signals builds software that does less because they believe most software is too complex (too many features, buttons, confusion); they're proud of what their products DON'T do as much as what they do, and are willing to lose customers to make others love their products intensely
- Whole Foods stands for selling the highest quality natural and organic products available—they don't debate selling products with artificial flavors, which is why you can't buy Coke or Snickers there; they're called "Whole Paycheck" but are doing well
- Vinnie's Sub Shop in Chicago puts homemade basil oil on subs and closes when the bread runs out (usually around 2-3 p.m.) because they won't sell sandwiches made with afternoon bread that isn't as good as morning-fresh bread

**Steps:**
1. Identify what you truly believe in and what your business stands for
2. Define what you're willing to fight for and what you won't compromise on
3. Communicate your stand clearly to the world
4. Accept that strong opinions will turn some people off—they may call you arrogant or aloof
5. If no one's upset by what you're saying, you're probably not pushing hard enough (and probably boring)
6. Make decisions based on your beliefs rather than debating every choice
7. Be willing to lose some customers if it means others love your product intensely
8. Draw your line in the sand and stick to it

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Have a strong point of view and communicate it
- ✅ Be willing to lose customers who don't align with your stand
- ✅ Understand that strong opinions attract superfans who defend and promote you
- ✅ Make decisions obvious by grounding them in your beliefs
- ✅ Stand for something specific rather than trying to appeal to everyone

**Keep In Mind:**
- Strong opinions aren't free—you'll turn some people off
- For everyone who loves you, there will be others who hate you
- If no one's upset by what you're saying, you're probably not pushing hard enough
- When you stand for something, decisions are obvious; when you don't know what you believe, everything becomes an argument
- A real line in the sand is about living your beliefs, not just writing them down

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Having a fake mission statement that sounds good on paper but isn't actually practiced
  **Solution:** Stand for something you actually believe in and live it every day—don't just write it on a wall
- **Problem:** Trying to please everyone and watering down your stand
  **Solution:** Accept that you'll lose some customers—being intense to some is better than being lukewarm to everyone
- **Problem:** Being afraid of backlash or criticism
  **Solution:** Embrace the haters—if no one's upset, you're probably not saying anything meaningful

**Key Terms:**
- Line in the Sand: A clear, non-negotiable stand that defines what you believe in and will fight for
- Superfans: Customers who passionately defend and promote your brand because they believe in what you stand for
- Point of View: A business's clear perspective or philosophy, not just its product features

### Lesson 6: Mission Statement Impossible
**Context:** There's a world of difference between truly standing for something and having a mission statement that says you stand for something. Fake mission statements posted on walls sound phony and disconnected from reality. Standing for something isn't about writing it down—it's about believing it and living it.

**Examples:**
- Enterprise Rent-a-Car's mission statement is a long, generic paragraph about fulfilling automotive and commercial truck rental needs and exceeding customer expectations—it says nothing specific and sounds disconnected from reality
- Automated phone voices telling you "how much the company values you as a customer" while you wait 30 minutes for support are phony—actions speak louder than programmed platitudes

**Steps:**
1. Avoid writing generic mission statements just to have something on the wall
2. Focus on living your beliefs rather than articulating them in corporate-speak
3. Ensure your actions match your words—if the carpet is dirty and no one's at the counter, no mission statement will convince customers you care
4. Say nothing if you can't genuinely demonstrate your values
5. Let your actual behavior and decisions communicate what you stand for

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Live your beliefs instead of just writing them down
- ✅ Avoid corporate-speak and generic platitudes
- ✅ Let actions speak louder than words
- ✅ Be genuine rather than performing authenticity
- ✅ Don't post mission statements that don't reflect reality

**Keep In Mind:**
- There's a world of difference between truly standing for something and having a mission statement that says you stand for something
- Generic mission statements sound phony and disconnected from reality
- Standing for something isn't about writing it down—it's about believing it and living it
- If your actions contradict your words, no amount of mission-statement writing will fix the perception
- Customers can tell the difference between genuine affection and a robot programmed to say nice things

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Creating a generic, forgettable mission statement just to check a box
  **Solution:** Skip the mission statement entirely and let your actual behavior and product quality communicate what you stand for
- **Problem:** Saying one thing but doing another
  **Solution:** Align your actions with your values—don't promise exceptional service while providing mediocre support

**Key Terms:**
- Mission Statement: A formal written declaration of an organization's purpose, often generic and disconnected from reality
- Corporate-Speak: Language that sounds professional but is vague, generic, and meaningless
- Phony: Insincere or artificial communication that customers can see through

### Lesson 7: Outside Money is Plan Z
**Context:** Far too often, people think the answer to seed money is to raise money from outsiders. But for most businesses—especially service businesses—outside funding is not necessary and comes with significant downsides. Take on as little outside cash as you can; it should be Plan Z, not Plan A.

**Examples:**
- 37signals launched their first product on the cheap: shared office space, one server, promoted by sharing experiences online, founder answered customer emails himself
- Service businesses (consultants, software companies, wedding planners, graphic designers, etc.) don't require much to get going and should avoid outside funding

**Steps:**
1. Assess whether your business truly needs outside capital
2. If you're a service business, bootstrap with minimal resources
3. Share office space, work from home, or use a garage instead of leasing expensive space
4. Handle customer service yourself instead of hiring immediately
5. Promote through content and word-of-mouth rather than paid advertising
6. If you do consider outside funding, understand what you're giving up: control, focus on customers, time, equity, and strategic autonomy
7. Use outside money only as a last resort when all other options are exhausted

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Take on as little outside cash as possible
- ✅ Bootstrap service businesses without external funding
- ✅ Use frugal alternatives: shared space, one server, DIY customer service
- ✅ Promote through organic channels (blogging, sharing experiences) instead of paid advertising
- ✅ Treat outside money as Plan Z, not Plan A
- ✅ Avoid the addiction of spending other people's money

**Keep In Mind:**
- Spending other people's money may sound great, but there's a noose attached
- You give up control and have to answer to outsiders
- "Cashing out" begins to trump building a quality business when investors want returns in 3-5 years
- Spending other people's money is addictive—you run out, need to go back, and they take more of your company each time
- It's usually a bad deal when you're just beginning and have no leverage
- Customers move down the totem pole—you wind up building what investors want instead of what customers want
- Raising money is incredibly distracting with months of pitch meetings, legal maneuvering, and contracts
- Business owners consistently regret taking outside funding—they talk about the "investment hangover"
- Before you stick your head in that noose, look for another way

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Raising outside money without understanding what you're giving up
  **Solution:** Treat outside funding as a last resort; bootstrap as long as possible and understand that investors will want returns in 3-5 years, often at odds with long-term sustainability
- **Problem:** Getting addicted to spending other people's money
  **Solution:** Bootstrap frugally from the start—use shared space, DIY everything, and promote organically to avoid dependency on external capital
- **Problem:** Losing control of your business direction to investors
  **Solution:** Retain equity and decision-making power by self-funding; if you must raise money, understand that you'll be answering to others who may not share your vision

**Key Terms:**
- Outside Money / Seed Money: Capital raised from investors or external sources rather than self-funding
- Bootstrap: Building a business with minimal resources without external investment
- Investment Hangover: The regret business owners feel after taking outside funding and realizing the loss of control and autonomy
- Plan Z: A last-resort option; outside money should be considered only after all other options are exhausted

### Lesson 8: You Need Less Than You Think
**Context:** Most people overestimate what they need to start a business. Do you really need ten people or will two or three do? Do you really need $500,000 or is $50,000 (or $5,000) enough? Do you really need six months or can you make something in two? Challenge every assumption about resources and embrace frugality.

**Examples:**
- 37signals shared office space with another company, had only one server, didn't advertise, promoted by sharing experiences online, and the founder answered customer emails himself—and everything worked out just fine
- Great companies start in garages all the time—yours can too

**Steps:**
1. Question every resource assumption: Do I really need X people, dollars, months, or space?
2. Start with 2-3 people instead of 10
3. Bootstrap with $50,000 (or $5,000) instead of $500,000
4. Ship in 2 months instead of waiting 6
5. Share office space, work from home, or use your garage instead of leasing
6. Use a small storage space or outsource fulfillment instead of renting a warehouse
7. Promote through content and word-of-mouth instead of paid advertising
8. Use affordable tools (Quicken) instead of hiring expensive specialists
9. Handle customer service yourself instead of hiring full-time
10. Sell online instead of opening a retail store
11. Skip fancy business cards, letterhead, and brochures

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Question every resource requirement—you likely need less than you think
- ✅ Start small and grow only when necessary
- ✅ Share space, outsource, and use DIY solutions
- ✅ Bootstrap with minimal capital
- ✅ Launch quickly rather than waiting for perfection
- ✅ Handle customer-facing tasks yourself initially

**Keep In Mind:**
- There's nothing wrong with being frugal
- Maybe eventually you'll need to go bigger, but not right now
- Great companies start in garages all the time
- The more expensive it is to make a change, the less likely you are to make it
- Being lean gives you flexibility to pivot and adapt

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Over-planning and over-resourcing before launching
  **Solution:** Constantly ask "Do I really need this?" for every resource—people, money, time, space—and choose the minimum viable option
- **Problem:** Spending on infrastructure before validating demand
  **Solution:** Launch with what you have and only expand resources when you have proven demand and can't keep up

**Key Terms:**
- Frugality: The practice of being economical with resources, especially in the early stages of a business
- Bootstrap: Starting and growing a business with minimal external resources or funding
- Minimum Viable: The smallest sufficient version of a resource, team, or product that gets the job done

### Lesson 9: Start a Business, Not a Startup
**Context:** The "startup" is a special breed of company that gets a lot of attention, especially in the tech world. It's a magical place where expenses are someone else's problem, revenue is never an issue, and the laws of business physics don't apply. But this is a fairy tale. Every business is governed by the same market forces and economic rules. A business without a path to profit isn't a business—it's a hobby.

**Examples:**
- A startup is a place where you can spend other people's money until you figure out a way to make your own
- People with a "we'll figure out how to profit in the future" attitude are like building a rocket ship while pretending gravity doesn't exist

**Steps:**
1. Reject the startup mentality that allows you to ignore profitability
2. Start an actual business that deals with real things like bills and payroll
3. Worry about profit from day one
4. Don't mask deep problems by saying "It's OK, we're a startup"
5. Accept that revenue in, expenses out—turn a profit or wind up gone
6. Build a path to sustainability from the beginning

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Act like an actual business from day one
- ✅ Focus on revenue and profitability early
- ✅ Don't use the startup label as an excuse to ignore business fundamentals
- ✅ Deal with real constraints: bills, payroll, customers
- ✅ Treat sustainability as non-negotiable from the start

**Keep In Mind:**
- The startup is a fairy tale—every business is governed by the same market forces and economic rules
- Revenue in, expenses out—turn a profit or wind up gone
- Startups try to ignore reality and postpone the inevitable moment when they have to grow up and be a real, sustainable business
- A business without a path to profit isn't a business, it's a hobby
- Anyone who takes a "we'll figure out how to profit in the future" attitude is being ridiculous
- Acting like an actual business gives you a much better shot at succeeding

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Using the "startup" label to avoid dealing with business fundamentals like revenue and profit
  **Solution:** Act like an actual business from day one—worry about profit, handle bills and payroll, and don't hide behind the startup excuse
- **Problem:** Believing you can postpone profitability indefinitely
  **Solution:** Build a path to profit from the beginning—a business that can't sustain itself isn't a business, it's a hobby

**Key Terms:**
- Startup: A company culture or mentality that treats external funding, delayed profitability, and ignoring traditional business constraints as acceptable
- Business Physics: The fundamental economic rules that apply to all businesses: revenue must exceed expenses to survive
- Sustainability: The ability of a business to continue operating profitably over the long term

### Lesson 10: Building to Flip is Building to Flop
**Context:** Thinking about your exit strategy before you even start building is a sign your priorities are out of whack. You need a commitment strategy, not an exit strategy. If your whole strategy is based on leaving, chances are you won't get far in the first place. Building a company with the intention of being acquired means you emphasize the wrong things and optimize for the wrong outcomes.

**Examples:**
- Would you go into a relationship planning the breakup? Write a prenup on a first date? Meet with a divorce lawyer the morning of your wedding? That would be ridiculous
- Aspiring businesspeople pin their hopes on selling out, but the odds of getting acquired are tiny—maybe 1 in 1,000 or 1 in 10,000
- Business owners who sell out often retire for six months and then get back in the game because they miss the thing they gave away, usually coming back with a business that isn't nearly as good as their first

**Steps:**
1. Focus on how to make your project grow and succeed, not how you're going to jump ship
2. Build a commitment strategy centered on long-term growth and success
3. Optimize for customer love, not acquisition appeal
4. Accept that the odds of a lucrative exit are very low
5. If you do succeed, keep it going—good things don't come around that often
6. Don't let your business be the one that got away

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Have a commitment strategy, not an exit strategy
- ✅ Optimize for making customers love you, not making your company attractive to buyers
- ✅ Focus on long-term sustainability and growth
- ✅ Build something you want to keep running
- ✅ Accept that acquisition odds are extremely low

**Keep In Mind:**
- Your priorities are out of whack if you're thinking about getting out before you even dive in
- If your whole strategy is based on leaving, you probably won't get far
- When you build with the intention of being acquired, you emphasize the wrong things
- Instead of focusing on getting customers to love you, you worry about who's going to buy you—that's the wrong thing to obsess over
- Even if you pull off a flip and get a nice payday, money alone won't satisfy you—you'll miss the work
- Business owners who sell out often come back because they miss what they gave away, usually with a worse business
- Good things don't come around that often—don't let yours be the one that got away

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Obsessing over exit strategy before building anything of value
  **Solution:** Replace exit strategy thinking with a commitment strategy—focus entirely on making your business succeed and grow, not on how you'll leave it
- **Problem:** Optimizing for acquisition rather than customer value
  **Solution:** Focus obsessively on making customers love your product; acquisition appeal will follow if you build something genuinely great
- **Problem:** Believing an acquisition is likely or guaranteed
  **Solution:** Realize the odds are 1 in 1,000 or worse—build for the long term, not a quick flip

**Key Terms:**
- Exit Strategy: A plan for leaving or selling a business, often considered prematurely by founders
- Commitment Strategy: A focus on long-term growth, sustainability, and success rather than leaving
- Flip / Building to Flip: Building a company specifically to sell it quickly, which usually results in suboptimal decisions

### Lesson 11: Less Mass
**Context:** Embrace the idea of having less mass. Right now, you're the smallest, leanest, and fastest you'll ever be. From here on out, you'll start accumulating mass, and the more massive an object, the more energy required to change its direction. Avoid things that increase mass so you can change direction easily.

**Examples:**
- 37signals shares office space, has only one server, promotes online, and the founder handles customer emails—keeping mass low means they can quickly change anything
- Huge organizations can take years to pivot—they talk instead of act and meet instead of do

**Steps:**
1. Identify mass-builders in your business and actively avoid them
2. Avoid long-term contracts that lock you into specific arrangements
3. Avoid excess staff—hire only when absolutely necessary
4. Avoid permanent decisions that are hard to reverse
5. Minimize meetings—they talk instead of do
6. Keep process thin and lightweight
7. Minimize physical or mental inventory
8. Avoid hardware, software, and technology lock-ins
9. Avoid long-term road maps that constrain flexibility
10. Avoid office politics by keeping teams small and focused
11. Make mistakes and fix them quickly when mass is low
12. Change priorities, product mix, or focus as needed
13. Most importantly, keep the ability to change your mind

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Keep mass low to maintain agility
- ✅ Avoid long-term contracts and commitments
- ✅ Hire minimally and only when needed
- ✅ Make reversible decisions whenever possible
- ✅ Minimize meetings and process
- ✅ Avoid technology and vendor lock-ins
- ✅ Embrace the ability to change direction, product, or strategy quickly
- ✅ Accept that you can make mistakes and fix them fast when you're small

**Keep In Mind:**
- You're the smallest, leanest, and fastest you'll ever be right now—protect that
- The more massive an object, the more energy required to change its direction (true in physics and business)
- Mass is increased by: long-term contracts, excess staff, permanent decisions, meetings, thick process, inventory, technology lock-ins, long-term road maps, and office politics
- The more expensive it is to make a change, the less likely you are to make it
- Huge organizations can take years to pivot—they talk instead of act and meet instead of do
- With low mass, you can change your entire business model, product, feature set, and/or marketing message quickly
- You can make mistakes and fix them quickly
- You can change your priorities, product mix, or focus
- Most importantly, you can change your mind

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Accumulating mass through long-term contracts, excess hiring, and complex processes
  **Solution:** Actively avoid mass-builders—question every long-term commitment, hire only when necessary, and keep process minimal
- **Problem:** Committing to long-term road maps that lock in strategy
  **Solution:** Keep planning horizons short and stay flexible—reassess direction regularly and avoid permanent plans
- **Problem:** Locking into technology or vendor relationships that are hard to exit
  **Solution:** Choose open, flexible tools and avoid proprietary lock-ins that make pivoting expensive

**Key Terms:**
- Mass: Business weight accumulated through contracts, staff, inventory, process, and commitments that slows agility
- Pivot: A fundamental change in business direction, product, or strategy
- Agility: The ability to change direction quickly and adapt to new information or conditions
- Technology Lock-in: Dependence on specific hardware, software, or vendors that makes switching costly or difficult
