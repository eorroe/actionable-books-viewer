# Chapter 9: PROMOTION

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Welcome Obscurity
**Context:** Being unknown is a strategic advantage that protects your ego and gives you space to iterate without public scrutiny.

**Examples:**
- Dunkin' Donuts test-marketed pizza and hot dogs at just ten select locations before wider rollout
- Broadway shows routinely do trial runs in smaller cities before opening in New York
- "Would you want the whole world to watch you the first time you do anything?"

**Steps:**
1. Embrace the fact that no one knows you yet
2. Use this time to make mistakes without the whole world hearing about them
3. Keep tweaking and testing random ideas
4. Work out the kinks before scaling attention
5. Take risks now—once you get big, pressure to maintain predictability builds and you'll take fewer risks

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Treat obscurity as protection for your confidence
- ✅ Test ideas on small stages before going mainstream
- ✅ Take maximum risks while you're still unknown

**Keep In Mind:**
- Once millions of people use your product, every change has a much bigger impact
- You can reason with a hundred angry people, but you need riot gear for ten thousand
- The early days of obscurity are something you'll miss later when you're under the microscope

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Trying to tell everyone to look at you before you're ready to be looked at
  **Solution:** Build, test, and refine privately first; let the right audience come to you

**Key Terms:**
- Obscurity: The state of being unknown, framed as a strategic advantage for early-stage experimentation

### Lesson 2: Build an Audience
**Context:** Companies with audiences have a secret weapon—people voluntarily return to hear from them, making promotion cheaper and more effective than advertising.

**Examples:**
- 37signals built an audience of more than a hundred thousand daily readers for their Signal vs. Noise blog over ten years
- Traditional advertising is expensive and unreliable ("you waste half of your ad budget—you just don't know which half")
- An audience comes back on its own to see what you have to say

**Steps:**
1. Choose a medium: speak, write, blog, tweet, make videos
2. Share information that's valuable to your target audience
3. Be consistent and patient—audiences are built slowly but surely
4. When you need to get the word out, the right people will already be listening

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Focus on earning attention rather than buying it
- ✅ Share valuable information that builds loyalty over time
- ✅ Let people come to you instead of chasing them with ads

**Keep In Mind:**
- An audience is the most receptive group of customers and potential customers you'll ever have
- The cost of reaching a hundred thousand people daily via traditional methods would be hundreds of thousands or millions

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Spending big bucks on ads and sponsorships to reach cold audiences
  **Solution:** Invest in content and teaching that naturally attracts and retains an audience

**Key Terms:**
- Audience: A group of people who voluntarily return to hear from you, distinct from customers or fans

### Lesson 3: Out-teach Your Competition
**Context:** Instead of trying to outspend competitors on advertising, sales, or sponsorships, teach your audience something valuable—something most competitors aren't doing.

**Examples:**
- Hoefler Type Foundry teaches designers about typography at Typography.com
- Etsy holds entrepreneurial workshops explaining best practices to sellers
- Gary Vaynerchuk teaches people about wine online at Wine Library TV, drawing tens of thousands of daily viewers
- Big companies can afford Super Bowl ads, but they can't teach because they're obsessed with secrecy and legal red tape

**Steps:**
1. Identify what your audience wants to learn related to your industry
2. Create educational content (blogs, videos, workshops, etc.)
3. Share openly without fear of giving away secrets
4. Build trust and respect through teaching rather than traditional marketing

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Teaching forms a bond you don't get from traditional marketing tactics
- ✅ Even people who don't use your product can become fans if you teach them
- ✅ Use teaching as a way to outmaneuver bigger, bureaucratic competitors

**Keep In Mind:**
- Buying attention with ads is one thing; earning loyalty by teaching is a different, stronger connection
- Teaching is something small companies can do that big competitors can't

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Trying to compete on ad spend with larger competitors who have bigger budgets
  **Solution:** Pivot to education—competitors likely aren't thinking about teaching at all

**Key Terms:**
- Out-teach: A competitive strategy focused on educating your audience rather than outspending rivals on advertising

### Lesson 4: Emulate Chefs
**Context:** Famous chefs share everything they know through cookbooks and TV shows because recipes and techniques alone aren't enough to replicate their success—business owners should do the same.

**Examples:**
- Emeril Lagasse, Mario Batali, Julia Child, and others became famous by sharing recipes and techniques openly
- "A recipe is much easier to copy than a business. Shouldn't that scare Mario Batali?"
- Many businesspeople are paranoid and secretive, but most don't actually have proprietary advantages worth protecting

**Steps:**
1. Identify your "recipes"—the processes, philosophies, and knowledge that define how you operate
2. Create your "cookbook" (blog, book, videos, workshops, etc.)
3. Share what you know in an informative, educational, and promotional way
4. Don't be afraid of competitors learning your methods

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Share everything you know—don't hoard information out of fear
- ✅ Understand that execution, context, and brand matter more than any single recipe or technique
- ✅ Let your transparency build trust and authority

**Keep In Mind:**
- No one's going to buy your cookbook, open a business next door, and put you out of business
- The business world is full of "professionals" who seem stiff and boring because they're afraid to be real

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Being overly secretive and paranoid about competitive advantage when you don't actually have one
  **Solution:** Get over the fear—share openly and let your execution and personality differentiate you

**Key Terms:**
- Cookbook (metaphor): Your body of shared knowledge about how you operate, think, and create value

### Lesson 5: Go Behind the Scenes
**Context:** Giving people a backstage pass to how your business works humanizes your brand, builds connection, and satisfies natural curiosity.

**Examples:**
- Discovery Channel's Deadliest Catch and History Channel's Ice Road Truckers turned seemingly boring jobs into highly rated shows
- Food Network's Unwrapped explores secrets behind lunch-box treats, soda pop, and movie candy
- People love factory tours and behind-the-scenes footage on DVDs

**Steps:**
1. Imagine what a reality show about your business would share
2. Document and share those elements yourself—don't wait for someone else to do it
3. Show how decisions are made, how products are built, and what the daily reality looks like
4. Reveal the sweat and effort that goes into what you sell

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Give people a backstage pass and show them how your business works
- ✅ Let people see the human beings behind the faceless company
- ✅ Leverage natural curiosity about how things are made

**Keep In Mind:**
- Even seemingly boring jobs can be fascinating when presented right
- People want to see how sets are built, how animation is done, how directors cast films
- Letting people behind the curtain changes your relationship with them

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Assuming no one will care about the inner workings of your business
  **Solution:** People are curious about all kinds of businesses—share authentically and let them decide

**Key Terms:**
- Behind the scenes: Content that reveals the inner workings, decisions, and effort behind a business or product

### Lesson 6: Nobody Likes Plastic Flowers
**Context:** Avoid coming off as a stiff, perfect "professional." Show your real, flawed self—imperfections are relatable and build genuine connection.

**Examples:**
- "No one can relate to people like that" (referring to uniform, perfect professionals)
- "It's why we like real flowers that wilt, not perfect plastic ones that never change"
- The Japanese principle of wabi-sabi values character and uniqueness over a shiny facade

**Steps:**
1. Pare down to the essence of your message, but don't remove the poetry
2. Keep things clean and unencumbered, but don't sterilize
3. Talk like you really talk
4. Reveal things others are unwilling to discuss
5. Be upfront about your shortcomings
6. Show the latest version of what you're working on, even if it's not done yet

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Embrace wabi-sabi: value character and uniqueness over perfection
- ✅ Leave the poetry in what you make—don't over-polish and lose your soul
- ✅ Be genuine even if it means seeming less professional

**Keep In Mind:**
- When something becomes too polished, it loses its soul and seems robotic
- You might not seem as professional, but you will seem a lot more genuine

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Trying to sound and act "professional" in a way that comes off as fake and boring
  **Solution:** Show your warts and all—real beats perfect every time

**Key Terms:**
- Wabi-sabi: A Japanese principle that values character, uniqueness, and simplicity over perfection and polish

### Lesson 7: Press Releases Are Spam
**Context:** Generic press releases sent to hundreds of strangers are ineffective spam. Stand out with personal, passionate pitches to specific journalists.

**Examples:**
- "What do you call a generic pitch sent out to hundreds of strangers hoping that one will bite? Spam."
- Press releases are "tired and formulaic," buried under "an avalanche of hyperbolic headlines and fake quotes from CEOs"
- Journalists sift through dozens of press releases a day

**Steps:**
1. Don't write generic press releases
2. Call someone directly or write a personal note instead
3. If you read a story about a similar company or product, contact the journalist who wrote it
4. Pitch with passion, interest, and life
5. Do something meaningful, remarkable, and unforgettable

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Personalize your outreach to individual journalists
- ✅ Stand out by being different from everyone else sending the same generic pitch
- ✅ Make your first impression count with a specific, thoughtful message

**Keep In Mind:**
- If you want to get someone's attention, doing exactly the same thing as everyone else is silly
- Your first introduction shouldn't be a vague, generic note you also send to everyone else

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Sending generic press releases to hundreds of journalists you don't know
  **Solution:** Research specific journalists, read their work, and pitch them personally with a tailored, passionate message

**Key Terms:**
- Press release: A generic announcement sent to media outlets, criticized here as an ineffective spam-like tactic

### Lesson 8: Forget About Major Publications
**Context:** Pitching major mainstream publications like the Wall Street Journal or Time is nearly impossible for unknown companies. Focus instead on trade publications and niche bloggers.

**Examples:**
- "Pitching a reporter at one of these places is practically impossible. Good luck even getting ahold of that guy."
- 37signals gets more traffic from niche sites like Daring Fireball and Lifehacker than from Wired or Time
- Niche bloggers are "actually hungry for fresh meat" and thrive on being tastemakers
- Big-time reporters now use smaller sites to find new stories

**Steps:**
1. Forget about pitching WSJ, Time, Forbes, BusinessWeek, NYT, and similar major publications
2. Target trade publications and niche bloggers in your industry
3. Send an email—you can often get a response (and maybe even a post) the same day
4. Build relationships with these tastemakers who are looking for the next big thing

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Focus on outlets with lower barriers to entry and faster response times
- ✅ Leverage niche sites that are hungry for new stories and eager to be tastemakers
- ✅ Recognize that stories starting on the fringe can go mainstream quickly

**Keep In Mind:**
- Articles in big-time publications are nice, but they don't result in the same level of direct, instant activity
- Niche outlets often drive more meaningful traffic and sales spikes

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Wasting time and energy chasing coverage in major publications that won't write about you
  **Solution:** Focus on trade publications and niche bloggers who are actually hungry for fresh content and can deliver faster, more targeted results

**Key Terms:**
- Niche blogger: A small-scale content creator in a specific industry who is often eager to discover and promote new products or ideas

### Lesson 9: Drug Dealers Get It Right
**Context:** Give customers a small, free taste of your product to hook them and make them want more—but only if your product is strong enough to make them come back.

**Examples:**
- Drug dealers give away a little for free upfront because they know people will be back for more
- Bakeries, restaurants, and ice cream shops have used free samples successfully for years
- Car dealers let you test-drive before buying
- Software firms offer free trials or limited-use versions
- "Don't be afraid to give a little away for free—as long as you've got something else to sell"

**Steps:**
1. Identify how to make your product bite-size and easily digestible for a free trial
2. Give people a small, free taste with no money or time investment required
3. Make your product so good that the free sample compels them to return and pay
4. Be confident that people will come back—if you're not, your product isn't strong enough

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Offer a small, free taste that demonstrates real value
- ✅ Ensure the full product is worth paying for
- ✅ Use free samples as a low-risk way to acquire customers

**Keep In Mind:**
- This forces you to make something about your product bite-size
- You should know people will come back for more—if you're not confident, you haven't created a strong enough product

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Giving away too much for free without a clear path to conversion, or giving away a poor-quality sample
  **Solution:** Offer a compelling but limited free taste, and ensure your core product is strong enough to convert tasters into paying customers

**Key Terms:**
- Drug-dealer model: A promotional strategy where a small free sample creates desire for the full paid product

### Lesson 10: Marketing Is Not a Department
**Context:** Marketing isn't just the responsibility of a single team—it's everything your company does, every single day, in every interaction with customers.

**Examples:**
- "Accounting is a department. Marketing isn't."
- "Just as you cannot not communicate, you cannot not market"
- Every time you answer the phone, it's marketing
- Every time you send an e-mail, it's marketing
- Every time someone uses your product, it's marketing
- Every word on your website is marketing
- Every error message is marketing
- The after-dinner mint is marketing
- The checkout counter is marketing
- Your invoice is marketing

**Steps:**
1. Recognize that every employee is a marketer, not just a designated team
2. Audit every touchpoint: phone, email, product, website, invoices, packaging
3. Ensure every interaction reinforces your brand and values
4. Prioritize these small, cumulative experiences over flashy one-off marketing events

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Make marketing everyone's responsibility 24/7/365
- ✅ Focus on the sum total of all customer interactions rather than isolated campaigns
- ✅ Treat error messages, invoices, and checkout experiences as marketing opportunities

**Keep In Mind:**
- Marketing is the sum total of everything you do, not just a few individual events
- These little things are more important than choosing which piece of swag to throw in a conference goodie bag

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Treating marketing as a siloed department activity while neglecting everyday customer touchpoints
  **Solution:** Embed marketing awareness into every role and every customer interaction

**Key Terms:**
- Marketing (holistic definition): The cumulative effect of every interaction a company has with the world, not a discrete department or campaign

### Lesson 11: The Myth of the Overnight Sensation
**Context:** Most "overnight" successes took years of hard work. Instant success usually lacks foundation and doesn't last. Focus on slow, measured growth instead.

**Examples:**
- Starbucks, Apple, Nike, Amazon, Google, and Snapple all became great brands over time, not because of a big PR push upfront
- "Dig deeper and you'll usually find people who have busted their asses for years to get into a position where things could take off"
- "On the rare occasion that instant success does come along, it usually doesn't last—there's no foundation there to support it"

**Steps:**
1. Trade the dream of overnight success for slow, measured growth
2. Be patient and grind it out for a long time before the right people notice
3. Don't hire a PR firm too early—it's expensive ($10,000+/month) and you're still a no-name
4. Start building your audience today and keep at it consistently

**Best Practices:**
- ✅ Embrace patience and long-term consistency
- ✅ Build a foundation before seeking widespread attention
- ✅ Start audience-building now rather than waiting until you feel "ready"

**Keep In Mind:**
- You're not big enough to matter to major publications yet—and that's okay
- Once you have customers and a history, you'll have a real story to tell
- Great brands launch without PR campaigns all the time

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Problem:** Chasing overnight success through expensive PR firms before having a real product or audience
  **Solution:** Focus on building your audience and product first; when the foundation is strong, stories will come naturally

**Key Terms:**
- Overnight success: A myth where instant fame and riches are expected; reality involves years of unseen work and slow growth
