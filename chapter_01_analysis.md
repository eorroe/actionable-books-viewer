**Social Epidemics—Why Things Catch On** (Lecture 1)

## 2. Actionable Lessons with Step-by-Step Instructions

### Lesson 1: Reduce Barriers to Trial to Accelerate Adoption
**Context:** Products or ideas that are hard to try spread slowly, even when highly valued (e.g., satellite radio requiring long contracts and expensive installation).

**Steps:**
1. Identify the current barriers preventing people from experiencing your product or idea (contract length, cost, complexity, availability).
2. Lower or eliminate those barriers. For example, offer free trials, place the product in easily accessible locations (rental cars, pop-up shops), or simplify the onboarding process.
3. Monitor adoption rates after removing barriers to confirm increased spread.

### Lesson 2: Use Free Samples to Increase Purchase Likelihood
**Context:** Sampling a product reduces risk and activates the norm of reciprocity, making people feel compelled to reciprocate by purchasing.

**Steps:**
1. Distribute small, low-cost samples of your product to your target audience.
2. Ensure the sample experience is representative of the full product.
3. Pair the sample with a clear, easy path to purchase or engagement.

### Lesson 3: Design Ideas to Fit Human Memory and Motivation
**Context:** Ideas that align with how human memory and psychology work are more likely to be shared and retained. Stories with higher emotional content and appropriate length are more memorable.

**Steps:**
1. Analyze your message or product for emotional resonance—strong emotions increase sharing.
2. Optimize the length and structure of your idea for memory (e.g., concise, vivid stories over dense information).
3. Test how well the idea is recalled after exposure and refine based on retention.

### Lesson 4: Leverage Environmental Triggers to Shape Behavior
**Context:** Subtle cues in the environment (voting location, background music, visual reminders) influence what people think about and how they behave.

**Steps:**
1. Identify relevant environmental contexts where your audience makes decisions.
2. Introduce subtle, context-appropriate cues that align with your desired behavior or message (e.g., French music in a wine aisle to encourage French wine purchases).
3. Test changes in behavior when cues are present versus absent.

### Lesson 5: Prioritize Word of Mouth Over Paid Advertising
**Context:** Interpersonal communication spreads ideas more effectively than traditional advertising because of trust, relevance, and social motivation.

**Steps:**
1. Create products, messages, or experiences that people naturally want to talk about and share.
2. Focus on generating organic, interpersonal sharing rather than relying solely on paid ad spend.
3. Track word-of-mouth metrics (referrals, organic shares) to gauge social transmission.

### Lesson 6: Distinguish Social Media from Social Transmission
**Context:** Social media is merely a channel; social transmission is the sharing of information through social ties, whether online or offline.

**Steps:**
1. Do not confuse building a social media presence with building social transmission.
2. Design for offline and online sharing alike—the mechanism is social ties, not the platform.
3. Evaluate whether your strategy strengthens actual social ties and sharing, not just platform metrics.

### Lesson 7: Apply Natural Selection Principles to Idea Design
**Context:** Ideas, like biological traits, succeed or fail based on their fit with the environment—in this case, human nature, memory, and social context.

**Steps:**
1. Treat your idea or product as a variation in a population of competing ideas.
2. Identify the "environment"—your audience's psychology, social context, and existing behaviors.
3. Iterate and select versions of your idea that best fit that environment (e.g., more emotional, easier to share).
4. Retain and scale what works; discard what does not spread.

### Lesson 8: Understand the Three Pillars of Social Epidemics
**Context:** To predict why some ideas spread and others fail, you must study individual psychology, social influence, and social networks.

**Steps:**
1. Study individual psychology to understand how people process, remember, and prefer information.
2. Study social influence to understand when people imitate versus diverge, and how conformity shapes behavior.
3. Study social networks to understand how the pattern of connections affects spread and which individuals are most influential.
4. Apply insights from all three areas when designing or promoting an idea.

## 3. Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Social Epidemic:** A situation where something (a product, idea, behavior, rumor) diffuses through a population, starting from one person or a small group and spreading widely, often becoming popular.
- **Contagious:** Describes something that spreads widely and becomes popular through social influence. Almost anything can be contagious—products, political ideas, behaviors, fashion, names, diets, language.
- **Trialability:** How easy something is to try. Higher trialability increases the likelihood that an idea or product catches on.
- **Social Influence:** The impact one person's behavior has on another's. Spreads via interpersonal connection rather than physical transmission.
- **Social Transmission:** The sharing of information and influence through social ties, whether online or offline.
- **Social Media:** Online communication tools or channels (e.g., Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn). Distinct from the broader concept of social transmission.
- **Stickiness (Science of):** The quality of ideas that makes them memorable and enduring in human memory.
- **Triggers:** Subtle environmental cues that influence what people think about and how they behave.
- **Norm of Reciprocity:** A social norm where people feel compelled to return a favor or compensate for a gift or sample they have received.
- **Natural Selection (applied to ideas):** The process by which ideas succeed or fail based on their fit with the surrounding environment (human psychology and social context). Involves variation, selection, and retention.
- **Individual Psychology:** The study of how people process information, including exposure, perception, and memory.
- **Social Networks:** The pattern of social ties that impacts how information and influence spread, and which individuals within the network are more influential.

## 4. Examples from the Text

- **Livestrong Wristbands (2004):** Introduced as a $1 fundraising item at Nike stores. Celebrities began wearing them, and demand surged—wristbands were resold on eBay for 10–15 times the retail price. This is a classic social epidemic driven by social influence.
- **Urban Legend (10% Brain Myth):** The false idea that humans only use 10% of their brain has spread widely through advertisements and popular culture, demonstrating how misinformation can become contagious.
- **Recycling Behavior:** Over one-third of U.S. waste is recycled annually (over 90 million tons). Over three-fourths of newspapers are recycled, a 90% increase since 1990. This prosocial behavior became widespread and second nature.
- **Reusable Grocery Bags (Failure Example):** Most people own reusable bags but forget to bring them to the store. This shows that even good ideas can fail to fully catch on.
- **Satellite Radio:** Initially slow to adopt due to high barriers to trial (long contract, receiver installation, high upfront cost). Adoption increased when companies lowered trial barriers by installing receivers in rental cars and offering free six-month trials in new cars.
- **YouTube Videos:** Some amateur cell phone videos get millions of views despite having little advertising, no price, and no direct quality correlation with success—demonstrating that quality and advertising alone do not explain contagion.
- **Voting Context:** Voting at a church or school can influence how people vote, demonstrating environmental triggers shaping behavior.
- **French Music in Grocery Stores:** Playing French music increases the likelihood of purchasing French wine, demonstrating subtle environmental cues driving behavior.
- **Prescription Drug Effectiveness:** The same prescription drug may be perceived as less effective when purchased at a discount, illustrating how context and perception affect outcomes.

## 5. Best Practices

1. **Lower the bar to trial.** If your product or idea requires effort, money, or commitment to try, find ways to reduce those barriers before expecting widespread adoption.
2. **Distribute samples strategically.** Even small, free samples reduce perceived risk and trigger reciprocity norms that increase purchase intent.
3. **Design for emotion and memory.** Craft messages and ideas with strong emotional content and optimal length to improve recall and sharing.
4. **Use subtle environmental cues.** Place relevant triggers in decision-making contexts to nudge behavior without coercion.
5. **Focus on social transmission, not just social media.** Build strategies that encourage sharing through real social ties, both online and offline.
6. **Study all three pillars.** Analyze individual psychology, social influence, and social network structure together for a complete picture of why things spread.
7. **Iterate based on fit.** View ideas as subject to natural selection; continuously refine based on how well they fit human psychology and the social environment.
8. **Trust word of mouth.** Prioritize creating shareable experiences and messages over relying solely on paid advertising budgets.

---

## 6. Common Pitfalls

1. **Assuming quality alone drives success.** High quality does not guarantee popularity; psychological and sociological mechanisms matter more.
2. **Relying on advertising as the primary driver.** Big budgets do not ensure spread. Organic social transmission is often more impactful.
3. **Confusing social media with social influence.** Having a social media presence does not mean ideas are spreading through social ties; the mechanism and channel are different.
4. **Ignoring trial barriers.** Launching products or ideas that are difficult or costly to try will slow adoption regardless of value.
5. **Neglecting environmental context.** Failing to account for subtle cues and contexts that shape perception and behavior.
6. **Overlooking conformity and divergence.** Not understanding when people imitate others versus when they deliberately diverge can lead to failed positioning or messaging.
7. **Treating spread as random or lucky.** Social epidemics follow identifiable principles and patterns; viewing success as luck prevents systematic improvement.
8. **Focusing only on individual factors.** Ignoring social networks and social influence limits understanding of why some ideas spread while others with similar individual appeal do not.
