# One Small Step Can Change Your Life: The Kaizen Way

## Overview

This book teaches the kaizen philosophy of using small, continuous steps, such as reading one page per day or flossing one tooth, to achieve lasting change in personal habits and professional practices. It explains why radical change efforts often fail and how very small, comfortable steps can bypass the fight-or-flight response, create new neural connections through repetition, and lead to lasting improvement in health, relationships with partners, family, and colleagues, and careers. Kaizen is rooted in Japanese manufacturing principles developed at Toyota, according to historical accounts, and demonstrates that, according to kaizen practice, honoring the brain's preference for gradual change makes transformation not only possible but effortless and lasting.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When you want to change a habit, behavior, or circumstance but feel overwhelmed, afraid, or resistant to starting
- When you are trying to help others—employees, family members, or patients—overcome fear and make positive changes
- When previous attempts at radical change (crash diets, exercise programs that demand thirty minutes per session on at least five days per week, abrupt career shifts) have ended in relapse or failure
- When you want to foster creativity, reduce stress, improve relationships, or build new habits such as exercising regularly, eating well, or staying organized without relying on willpower
- When you need a strategy that works under time pressure or limited energy and motivation
- When you are facing a crisis and need to identify manageable small problems within a larger crisis or emergency

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Understand Why Kaizen Works and How It Differs From Innovation

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Julie's Exercise Breakthrough

Julie, a single mother with high blood pressure and overwhelming responsibilities, was told by her doctor to exercise thirty minutes on most days. Fearing failure and feeling misunderstood, she would have rejected this advice. Instead, her doctor suggested she march in place for just one minute each night while watching television. This tiny step bypassed her fear, built a new habit, and she was enthusiastically doing full aerobics workouts. The small step created a new mental pattern for change that eventually took over and propelled her toward her goal without additional conscious effort.

##### Example 2: Toyota's Kaizen Success and Crisis

From 1950 through 1989, Toyota exemplified quality manufacturing through kaizen—small, continual improvements suggested by every employee. Toyota paid Japanese employees $3.88 per suggestion, according to company records, and Toyota's Presidential Award was a fountain pen, yet workers provided more than one million suggestions per year, with Toyota implementing 95 percent of suggestions. However, when Toyota temporarily abandoned kaizen practices from 1990 to 1999 in favor of rapid growth, producing three million additional automobiles in six years, quality collapsed: nine million recalls resulted. Toyota later restored its reputation by returning to kaizen, slowing down production, and empowering local managers to correct defects while they were small and easily fixed.

#### Step 1: Recognize the Three-Brain Model and the Role of the Amygdala

Understand that the human brain has three parts according to the triune brain model (also called the Three-Brain Model): the reptilian brain stem (five hundred million years old), the mammalian midbrain (three hundred million years old, houses emotions and the fight-or-flight response), and the cortex (one hundred million years old, responsible for rational thought and creativity, according to the triune brain model). When you attempt a big change, the amygdala in the midbrain triggers fear, which slows or shuts down the cortex—the part of the brain you need for change. Small goals bypass the amygdala, keeping the amygdala inactive, while allowing the cortex to engage and create new neural connections through repetition.

#### Step 2: Distinguish Kaizen From Innovation

Know that innovation is a drastic, fast, large-scale change that often requires heroic willpower and triggers fear. Kaizen is its alternative: a gentle, continuous process of very small steps that requires little time, self-control, or discipline. Innovation can produce dramatic short-term results but often leads to relapse. Kaizen produces slower but lasting change by gradually building new habits and neural connections. Use innovation when it works, but do not rely on it exclusively.

#### Step 3: Choose Small Goals to Bypass Fear

When setting a goal, make it so small and trivial that you can guarantee you will take it every single day. For example, instead of "exercise thirty minutes on most days," try "march in place for one minute." Instead of "clean the house," try "pick up one paper clip." Small goals do not trigger the fight-or-flight response, according to kaizen practice, allowing your brain to stay open, creative, and receptive to change. As the small step becomes effortless and pleasurable, you can add another small step.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Accept that fear is a common response to change, not an indicator that the step size needs adjustment
- ✅ Frame fear as excitement when possible; successful people openly acknowledge fear as a sign they are pushing themselves
- ✅ Expect that the brain will resist change and plan small steps to circumvent that resistance
- ✅ Trust that small, repeated actions will create new neural connections through repetition and eventually produce large results
- ❌ Do not rely exclusively on innovation (radical, sudden change) as your only strategy for transformation
- ❌ Do not judge yourself harshly for feeling afraid or for needing to take tiny steps
- ❌ Do not skip the small steps because they seem too trivial or slow to produce results

#### Keep In Mind

- According to evolutionary psychology, the fight-or-flight response is an ancient survival mechanism that now activates whenever we try something new, not just when physical danger is present
- As Jack Welch, Chuck Jones, and Sally Ride observed, people who achieve success are not fearless. These individuals are more effective at acknowledging fear and taking action despite it
- In the author's view, kaizen requires willingness to try small steps without immediate proof of efficacy—not necessarily religious trust, but a belief in the brain and body's ability to change given time and small inputs
- Small steps build tolerance for change, and the mind often takes over and accelerates the pace on its own once resistance weakens

#### Security & Safety Notes

- If you are working with a client, employee, or loved one who is in crisis, ensure that small steps do not delay necessary professional intervention or medical care
- Be aware that small steps may be insufficient in emergency situations requiring immediate action

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Choosing a "small" step that still triggers resistance or avoidance
  **Solution:** Make the step so trivial that you are certain you can do it daily. If you find yourself making excuses, reduce the step size by half.
- **Problem:** Expecting fast results and becoming frustrated with the pace of kaizen
  **Solution:** Ask yourself: "Isn't slow change better than what I've experienced before—which is no change at all?" Trust the process and allow the brain to build competence through repetition when it is ready.
- **Problem:** Falling back into self-criticism when fear arises, using harsh questions like "Why am I so stupid?"
  **Solution:** Replace negative questions with positive ones. Ask: "What is one thing I like about myself today?" or "What is one small thing I did well?"

### Lesson 2: Ask Small Questions to Reduce Fear and Inspire Creativity

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Patrick's Manufacturing Team Turnaround

Patrick, a factory supervisor, asked his employees big, scary questions like "What is each of you going to do to make our company the best in the industry?" The employees froze, complained behind his back, and employees took 23 percent more sick days, according to company records. When Patrick softened his tone and asked "Can you think of a very small step you might take to improve our process or product?" faces lit up, contributions flowed, and practical ideas emerged: selling scrap metal, training new employees, and starting a public thank-you system. Morale and efficiency both improved.

##### Example 2: Grace's Journey to Finding Love

Grace, a successful businesswoman, could not find a lasting romantic relationship. She was asked to spend two minutes each day asking herself "What would my ideal mate be like?" At first she struggled, but the question gradually took root. She then called her therapist's voice mail daily to answer: "What small, caring act would you like to receive from an ideal partner right now?" Her brain began generating answers, and she started taking small steps: eating lunch in a food court to meet people, dressing with extra care, and considering where she might meet a compatible partner. Within six months of beginning the practice, she met her future husband at church.

#### Step 1: Ask Small, Nonthreatening Questions Repeatedly

Replace big, frightening questions ("How can I lose twenty pounds by summer?") with small, gentle ones ("What is one small step I could take toward my goal?" or "What is one thing I like about myself today?"). Repeat the question daily—over coffee, in the car, before bed. Write it on a Post-it note. The hippocampus (the brain's storage center) responds to repetition according to neuroscience research on memory formation and will eventually produce answers when the brain is ready, often when you are in the shower or driving.

#### Step 2: Use Questions Instead of Commands

According to research in cognitive psychology, questions engage the cerebral cortex through active problem-solving; commands trigger resistance. Ask yourself or others: "What is one small step I could take to improve my health?" rather than "You need to exercise more." According to research in cognitive psychology, questions engage the cerebral cortex through active problem-solving; commands shut it down.

#### Step 3: Proceed Gently Past Fear With Kaizen Questions

Big questions trigger the amygdala and suppress creativity. Small questions keep the amygdala inactive and the cortex engaged. Examples of effective kaizen questions include: "What's the smallest step I can take to be more efficient?" "What can I do in five minutes a day to reduce my credit-card debt?" "How could I find one source of information about adult education classes in my city?" Ask the question often enough, and your brain will store it, turn it over, and eventually generate responses.

#### Step 4: Ask Others Small Questions to Show Respect and Gather Insight

Ask colleagues, family members, or employees small questions to demonstrate respect and uncover practical ideas for improvement. Examples: "Is there one small improvement that the staff or customers would like me to make?" "What is one thing your spouse does that makes you happy?" "What do you like and not like about going to the doctor?" Listening to small answers from unexpected sources can yield powerful insights and strengthen relationships.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Choose a question and ask it repeatedly over days or weeks; do not demand immediate answers
- ✅ Use a kind tone with yourself, the same tone you would use for a beloved friend
- ✅ Ask questions that focus on solutions or self-appreciation to avoid the harmful pattern of self-criticism
- ✅ Be patient; answers often come when you least expect them
- ✅ Ask "What is one small thing that is special about me (or my organization)?" to program the brain to look for the good
- ❌ Do not ask questions so large that they trigger fear and shut down creative thinking
- ❌ Do not use a harsh, urgent, or self-critical tone when asking yourself questions
- ❌ Do not assume that only "important" people have valuable insights to share

#### Keep In Mind

- According to cognitive psychology research, the brain loves questions because they are playful and engaging; they do not demand immediate action
- Repetition is one of the hippocampus's criteria for storing information according to neuroscience research on memory consolidation
- Small questions are particularly effective for defusing complex fears related to dating, career, or health
- Calling a therapist's voice mail or writing answers in a journal can help build accountability without fear of judgment

#### Security & Safety Notes

- When asking small questions of others in a workplace setting, always reward honest feedback immediately and practice discretion to maintain trust
- Do not use small questions to manipulate or coerce others into unwanted actions

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Asking questions that are too big and frightening, triggering the amygdala and shutting down creativity
  **Solution:** Make your questions smaller. If "How can I get rich?" feels scary, try "What is one small step I could take to improve my finances?"
- **Problem:** Berating yourself with negative questions such as "Why am I such a loser?"
  **Solution:** Replace negative questions with positive ones. Ask daily: "What is one thing I like about myself today?"
- **Problem:** Expecting immediate answers and giving up when the brain does not respond right away
  **Solution:** Trust that the brain works when it is ready. Keep asking the question gently and repeatedly.

### Lesson 3: Think Small Thoughts Using Mind Sculpture

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Michael Phelps's Mental Rehearsal

Olympic swimmer Michael Phelps practiced mind sculpture daily even while lying in bed. His coach instructed him to imagine himself on the starting block, hearing the signal, pushing off, gliding through the water, and making perfect turns. Phelps imagined he was actually inside the aquatic center, not just watching a video of himself. He used all his senses: the feel of the swim cap, the roar of the crowd, the texture of the water. During one race, water in his goggles threatened to slow him down—but he had already envisioned this scenario during mental rehearsal and knew exactly how to handle it. With twenty-two medals, including eighteen gold, Phelps is the most decorated athlete in Olympic history.

##### Example 2: Michael the Manager Learns to Give Feedback

Michael, a corporate supervisor, avoided conducting annual employee reviews. He made a bargain with his therapist: thirty seconds a day of mind sculpture for three months. For the first month, he imagined giving a specific, detailed compliment to an employee in an enthusiastic tone. He imagined how he would stand, how his voice would sound, and what the room would feel like. The next month he added one critical comment alongside the compliment. He also practiced giving one real compliment at home each day. His family noticed the change. At the end of three months, Michael conducted the overdue reviews and began spontaneously giving feedback in the hallways.

#### Step 1: Isolate a Task That Makes You Uncomfortable

Choose a task you are afraid to perform or that makes you anxious. Give yourself at least one month before you must actually perform the activity. Examples include giving a presentation, confronting a difficult employee, or sticking to a diet.

#### Step 2: Devote a Short Time, Not Minutes, to Daily Practice

Decide how much time you are willing to devote to mind sculpture each day. The time commitment should be so low that you can easily fulfill it every single day. Repetition is essential: whatever you do repeatedly, even for just a short time at a time, the brain decides must be important and begins committing cells to the new behavior.

#### Step 3: Create a Full Sensory Experience

Sit or lie down in a quiet, comfortable spot and close your eyes. Imagine that you are in the difficult situation, looking through your own eyes. What do you see? What is the setting? Who is there? What are they wearing, and what are their facial expressions? Then expand to all senses: sounds, smells, flavors, and textures around you.

#### Step 4: Perform the Task in Your Imagination

Without moving an actual muscle, imagine that you are performing the task. What words do you use? How does your voice sound and resonate through your body? What are your physical gestures? Imagine a positive response from others: the audience leaning forward, taking notes, nodding in agreement.

#### Step 5: Progress Gradually and Only When Ready

When your allotted time becomes habitual and even fun, you may automatically begin performing the formerly difficult activity with enthusiasm. If you are not ready for the real thing, that is perfectly okay. Increase the time slowly—perhaps by just thirty seconds—only when the previous stage has become effortless. If you start making excuses or forgetting to practice, cut back on the amount of time.

#### Step 6: Practice Worst-Case Scenarios

Once the step no longer triggers resistance using mind sculpture for the task, imagine a worst-case scenario and how you would respond effectively. A public speaker might imagine feeling nervous sweat as the audience looks bored, then practice responding with poise and confidence.

#### Step 7: Take Small Real-World Steps When Ready

When you feel ready to attempt the actual task, start with small, low-risk versions. For public speaking, give your talk to an empty room or to one sympathetic person before presenting to a larger audience.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Start with compliments and positive scenarios before moving to criticism or difficult conversations
- ✅ Use mind sculpture for both learning new skills and overcoming phobias or anxieties
- ✅ Imagine all five senses to maximize brain engagement and neural rewiring
- ✅ Keep practice sessions short and increase duration only when they become effortless
- ✅ Use mind sculpture when illness or scheduling conflicts prevent physical practice
- ❌ Do not force the process; kaizen works only if change happens in a comfortable and easy manner
- ❌ Do not increase practice time too quickly; the brain needs repetition at a comfortable pace
- ❌ Do not skip the mental rehearsal even if you feel ready to act; the brain continues building connections during imagery

#### Keep In Mind

- According to research discussed by Ian Robertson in his book Mind Sculpture, the brain does not fully distinguish between real and imagined experience
- According to the research discussed by Ian Robertson, people who practice a five-finger piano exercise for two hours a day show comparable brain activity increases in the same brain regions as those who only imagine playing
- When you mentally practice a task using all senses, your brain's chemistry begins to change and rewire cells
- Michael Phelps's coach used mind sculpture because it allowed Phelps to practice without the physical fatigue of daily full swims
- Mind sculpture has been used successfully to treat flashbacks in earthquake victims and to eliminate recurring nightmares by envisioning a happy ending

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Mind sculpture is a supplement to, not a replacement for, professional medical or psychological treatment when dealing with trauma or severe anxiety
- Do not use mind sculpture as the sole intervention for life-threatening situations requiring immediate action

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Trying to practice mind sculpture for too long too soon, making it feel like a chore
  **Solution:** Start with just seconds per day. Increase by thirty seconds only when the current duration feels effortless.
- **Problem:** Using only visual imagination without engaging other senses
  **Solution:** Add sounds, smells, textures, and physical sensations. The more senses you engage, the more the brain treats the imagery as real.
- **Problem:** Jumping to the real-world task before the mental rehearsal has become habitual
  **Solution:** Only move to real-world action when the step no longer triggers resistance and can be performed without significant effort.

### Lesson 4: Take Small Actions

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Medical Clinic That Saved Itself

An outpatient clinic faced financial disaster due to high patient disenrollment caused by long wait times. Expensive software solutions and hiring additional staff were financially impossible. The clinic staff brainstormed small, free actions: apologizing for the wait, offering to see another physician or reschedule, and thanking patients for their patience. These tiny steps cost nothing and took only seconds. Patient satisfaction rates doubled within six months, and defections dropped by 60 percent.

##### Example 2: Rachel Quits Smoking Through Small Steps

Rachel, a forty-five-year-old heavy smoker, had tried and failed to quit one to two months at a time. She grew up in a family where self-reliance was prized and dependence on others was seen as weakness. Her first small step was to call her therapist's voice mail once a day, simply saying "Hi, it's Rachel." This tiny act of reaching out felt threatening but gradually became less frightening. She then added a call before each cigarette, saying "I'm having a cigarette now!" After two months, she began journaling for just two minutes a day. Her cigarette intake dropped by 30 percent without effort. Next, she practiced imagining a best friend by her side and asking herself what she would want that friend to do. Eventually she began calling real friends, building a support network. Within a month of using her usual smoking-cessation technique again, she quit and has not smoked for two years.

#### Step 1: Choose a Truly Trivial First Step

Use a partner to help you find a step so small that it requires minimal effort, so that you can guarantee you will take it every day. For health, instead of "exercise more," try "toss out the first bite of a snack." For a cleaner house, try "pick up one paper at the end of the day." For reconciling with a parent, try "spend one minute a day thinking about the parent's positive qualities." The step must be so easy that even on your most difficult day, you can still do it.

#### Step 2: Build the Habit Slowly

Once your first step becomes automatic, effortless, and even pleasurable, decide whether to add a second step. Use the same questioning process to determine your next step, again making sure you can guarantee the results. Do not expect measurable results within the first week; continue adding steps only when the current step feels easy. If you ever feel yourself dreading the activity or making excuses, cut back on the size of the step.

#### Step 3: Apply Kaizen to Specific Common Goals

Use the book's kaizen step guides for common New Year's resolutions:
- Eat more healthfully: toss out the first bite of one fattening snack for one month; then toss the first two bites; then three.
- Exercise: squeeze a tennis ball while watching television; walk around the block once; pass one additional house per day.
- Save money: save one dollar per day by downgrading one daily purchase or sharing an indulgence with a friend.
- Meet more people: write down one place you might go to meet people with similar interests; add one location each day.
- Ask for a raise: start a list of reasons you deserve more money; practice your request out loud for one minute per day.
- Use time more productively: list unproductive activities and alternatives that you find more engaging or useful; try new activities in a very limited way (write three sentences per day in a journal, or just sit in a yoga studio lobby).

#### Step 4: Overcome Resistance by Scaling Back

If you hit a wall of resistance, do not give up. Instead, make your steps even smaller. People who struggle with kaizen do so not because the steps are hard but because they are easy. Cultural messages often suggest that change must be instantaneous and require steel discipline. Remember that the goal is to bypass fear, not to prove your willpower. When the steps are sufficiently small, the mind will usually take over and accelerate the pace on its own.

#### Step 5: Use Kaizen to Break Habits and Addictions

Addictions such as smoking, overeating, or alcohol persist because the substance serves as a "friend" that provides comfort. Quitting cold turkey often fails because the person loses that support without replacing it with human connection. Use small steps to build a support network gradually: call a therapist's voice mail, write in a journal for two minutes, ask small questions about what a supportive friend would do. Eventually replace the addictive comfort with real human relationships.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Make your steps so small that they require minimal effort
- ✅ Trust that the brain will develop mastery when it is ready; do not force the pace
- ✅ Use kaizen steps for deeply resisted activities like flossing, exercise, or cleaning
- ✅ Remember that one small step can sometimes produce a dramatic improvement all by itself
- ✅ Floss one tooth per day, stand on the treadmill while reading the newspaper, or remove one grain of sugar from your spoon at a time
- ❌ Do not choose a step that is still large enough to trigger your inner critic
- ❌ Do not expect results within the first week or give up because change seems slow
- ❌ Do not compare your pace of change to others; your brain needs its own time to build new habits

#### Keep In Mind

- Small actions are the core principle of kaizen because no matter how much you prepare, eventually you must act
- Small actions present the change in a way that does not trigger resistance, so there is no need for alarm
- By managing the fear response, small actions allow the brain to build new, permanent habits at a faster pace than anticipated
- The study of persuasion demonstrates kaizen's power to reduce even strong resistance
- In one study by Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser, Southern California homeowners who agreed to display a small "Be a safe driver" sign in their window were four times more likely to agree to a larger request later—a billboard on their lawn—than homeowners who were not approached about the small sign first

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not use small steps as an excuse to delay necessary medical intervention in emergencies
- When quitting addictions that have severe withdrawal symptoms, consult a healthcare provider alongside kaizen techniques

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Choosing a step that feels too big and triggers resistance
  **Solution:** Reduce the step by half. If you cannot guarantee you will do it daily, it is too large.
- **Problem:** Becoming frustrated that change is happening slowly
  **Solution:** Ask: "Is slow change better than no change at all?" Remember that kaizen produces lasting results, while radical change often leads to relapse.
- **Problem:** Forgetting to practice the small step or making excuses
  **Solution:** Set a visual reminder (tape floss to the bathroom mirror, put a note on the remote control) or tie the step to an existing daily habit.

### Lesson 5: Address Small Problems Before They Escalate

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Toyota's Andon Cord and the Power of Small Problem-Solving

Taiichi Ohno placed a cord at each step of the Toyota assembly line that any worker could pull to stop production and address a defect. Other manufacturers thought this was counterproductive—how could a company assemble cars swiftly if the line stopped on a worker's whim? Ohno's method proved brilliantly successful because fixing a small problem on the scene prevented much bigger problems later. In contrast, BP ignored more than 350 small oil spills during the 2001–2007 period, leading to the catastrophic Deepwater Horizon explosion in 2010 that spilled 200 million gallons of crude oil into the Gulf of Mexico.

##### Example 2: William Bratton Cleans Up New York City Subways

When William Bratton was hired in 1990 to reduce New York City subway crime, he resisted pressure to use radical, showy solutions. Instead, he focused on small, petty crimes: urinating in public, vagrancy, and turnstile jumping. He had officers arrest turnstile jumpers day in and day out, handcuffing fifteen at a time in the station where passengers could see them. The rate of major crime in the subway system dropped significantly over approximately two years (about 24 months). One captain solved another small problem by asking: "What makes people look up on a subway car?" The answer was the loudspeaker, so announcements were used to alert passengers to police sweeps.

##### Example 3: Becky Learns to Ask for Help During Cancer Treatment

Becky, a fifty-five-year-old woman diagnosed with cancer, had cut off all communication with family and friends and felt overwhelmed by her treatment and daily chores. She agreed to see a therapist only for short sessions each day. Each morning she wrote a list of chores and put a star next to each one with which she would like assistance, describing the specific help she wanted. The list kept her from entering denial and kept her focused on asking for help safely. She slowly reconnected with parents and friends, and her wish list became more detailed and emotional. She grew calmer and more in control, and has remained in remission.

#### Step 1: Train Yourself to Spot Small Problems

Learn to notice minor annoyances and warning signs before they grow into major obstacles. Recall a major mistake you have made and ask: Were there small signs along the way indicating that things were not going according to plan? Identify one small mistake you made today without becoming angry with yourself. This single act, performed daily, raises your awareness of small mistakes. Then ask yourself whether the small mistake reflects a larger pattern that needs attention.

#### Step 2: Address Problems at the Beginning, Middle, and End of Change

Be especially vigilant at three times: at the beginning of your path to change (do not ignore small character flaws or relationship issues you notice early); near the finish line (do not let the excitement of almost completing a goal blind you to warning signs); and during an overwhelming crisis (small problems may be hidden beneath the surface of a massive disaster).

#### Step 3: Recognize Common Warning Signs That Should Not Be Ignored

Do not ignore: disturbing traits in a new dating partner (rudeness to waiters, excessive drinking); skills that do not meet your performance standards in a job applicant you are tempted to hire out of desperation; angry or critical self-talk; small, persistent signs of pain when exercising; and any resistance to the small step you have chosen.

#### Step 4: Solve Small Problems in Overwhelming Crises

When facing a lawsuit, illness, career collapse, or relationship breakdown, do not try to fix everything at once. Locate the smaller problems within the larger disaster and apply kaizen techniques to move slowly toward a solution. Write down one small, manageable action you can take today, even if it is just calling one person or completing one form.

#### Step 5: Create a Culture of Small Problem-Solving in Organizations

Encourage every employee to look for ways to improve processes and products daily. Reward small improvements and public acknowledgment of mistakes. Train staff to view every defect, complaint, or delay as an opportunity to learn and improve rather than a threat to morale.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Anticipate potential problems before they occur; look for subtle warning signs
- ✅ Address problems when they are small and inexpensive to fix
- ✅ Use a partner or team to help identify blind spots and generate creative solutions
- ✅ Reward honesty and early reporting of problems rather than punishing failure
- ✅ Train yourself to recall past mistakes and identify the small signs that preceded them
- ❌ Do not ignore small problems because they seem insignificant compared to larger issues
- ❌ Do not wait for a spectacular failure before addressing a chronic minor issue
- ❌ Do not let the pressure to finish a goal cause you to overlook warning signs near the finish line

#### Keep In Mind

- Small problems accumulate over time and eventually block your path to change if left unaddressed
- The Tao Te Ching teaches: "Confront the difficult while it is still easy; accomplish the great task by a series of small acts."
- High-reliability organizations (emergency rooms, aircraft carriers, nuclear plants) distinguish themselves by detecting very subtle warning signs and taking strong, decisive action
- Even when life is in chaos, small steps such as writing a wish list or asking for one specific form of help can restore a sense of control

#### Security & Safety Notes

- In safety-critical environments, small problem detection must be institutionalized and never left to individual discretion alone
- Do not use the "small problems" philosophy to minimize or excuse serious safety violations

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Missing small warning signs because they seem normal or unimportant
  **Solution:** Practice daily awareness exercises. Identify one small mistake you made today without self-criticism. Scan your environment for subtle signs of trouble.
- **Problem:** Waiting until a small problem becomes a crisis before addressing it
  **Solution:** Remember BP's experience: over 350 small oil spills led to the worst environmental disaster in U.S. history. Fix small leaks immediately.
- **Problem:** Feeling overwhelmed by an overwhelming problem and unable to see any small, actionable parts
  **Solution:** Write down the larger problem, then list every tiny component of it. Ask: "What is one small thing I can do about one small part of this today?"

### Lesson 6: Give Small Rewards to Sustain Motivation and Build Habits

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Toyota's Presidential Award

Toyota's best suggestion of the year receives the Presidential Award, bestowed at a formal ceremony. This coveted reward is not a fancy watch, a new car, or a shopping spree. It is a fountain pen. Toyota paid Japanese employees $3.88 per suggestion (compared to hundreds of dollars per year in the U.S.). Yet Toyota workers provide over one million suggestions per year, with Toyota implementing 95 percent of suggestions. Small rewards preserve intrinsic motivation, according to research in psychology—the internal desire to contribute and excel—while large cash prizes often undermine it by making the reward the goal itself rather than the joy of the work.

##### Example 2: Karen Pryor's Chocolate Squares

Karen Pryor, a whale and dolphin trainer, was working all day and attending graduate school at night. She found it hard to motivate herself to commute and attend classes. Breaking her journey into segments—walking to the subway, changing trains, taking the stairs—she allowed herself one square of chocolate after each completed segment. She trained herself to associate each segment of the journey with pleasure. She was able to get all the way to class without either the chocolate or the internal struggle.

##### Example 3: Jack Stupp's Recovery From Rheumatoid Arthritis

Jack Stupp was fifty-four years old when he was hospitalized with severe rheumatoid arthritis and confined to a wheelchair. Doctors advised against exercise. Each morning when he awoke in pain, he told himself he only had to get out of bed. When he stood upright, Jack gave himself a mental acknowledgment of progress: "Atta boy, Jack!" he'd say. Then he told himself that if he could walk to the gym, he would enjoy a chat with the staff. Once on the treadmill, Jack started by walking for just two minutes, rewarding himself with praise and encouragement. Minute by minute and small reward by small reward, he worked himself into physical fitness. By the time he was in his late seventies, he had won the Mr. World bodybuilding contest for his age division.

#### Step 1: Choose Rewards That Are Aligned With the Goal and the Person

A reward should support the goal rather than undermine it. Chocolate is perfect for Karen Pryor's commute but counterproductive for a man trying to cut back on unhealthy foods. A compliment works well for some people but feels condescending to others. For clients who are hesitant to call their therapist, phone calls as rewards work; for those who feel entitled to call anytime, the same reward is meaningless. Ask: "How do you know you are loved?" or "How do you know you are appreciated?" The answers often reveal small, specific preferences.

#### Step 2: Keep Rewards Small, Free, or Inexpensive

The reward should be proportionate to the step. Small rewards preserve intrinsic motivation by signaling appreciation for the person's internal desire to improve. Ideas include: a five-dollar food voucher (Southwest Airlines), written "Love Reports" from peers, hearing the boss say "thank you," receiving a compliment from a superior, or having someone bring you a cup of coffee when you're working late.

#### Step 3: Use Small Rewards When Incremental Steps Are Not Possible

Sometimes you cannot break a goal into tiny steps. A pregnant woman must quit smoking now, or a boss demands immediate results. In these cases, use small rewards to bridge the gap. Karen Pryor could not break her commute into tiny kaizen actions because by the time she grew comfortable with the schedule, the semester would be over. Instead, she used chocolate squares to reward herself after each segment of the journey.

#### Step 4: Reward Yourself or Others Immediately and Consistently

The power of small rewards lies in their immediacy and consistency. When a colleague helps you, say thank you right away. When an employee makes a small improvement, acknowledge it publicly or privately. When you complete a small step you have been avoiding, give yourself a small, earned reward. Do not wait for a major milestone to offer recognition.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Keep rewards small, inexpensive, and aligned with that person's values
- ✅ Ensure the reward does not contradict the goal (do not reward healthy eating with junk food)
- ✅ Use rewards as recognition, not as material gain or manipulation
- ✅ Ask people what makes them feel appreciated rather than assuming you know
- ✅ Reward honest feedback and small improvements immediately and always
- ❌ Do not use large cash prizes or expensive gifts that shift focus from the work to the reward
- ❌ Do not assume that what motivates you will motivate others. Recognize that individual motivators vary
- ❌ Do not withhold rewards until a large goal is reached; small, frequent recognition is more powerful

#### Keep In Mind

- The larger the external reward, the greater the risk of inhibiting or stunting the native drive for excellence, according to quality management expert Dr. W. Edwards Deming
- Big cash prizes can send the message that an employee is a cog in the machine who must be driven to excessive action by the possibility of personal gain
- Small rewards are a form of recognition rather than material gain, signaling that the corporation or relationship appreciates the person's internal desire to improve
- Once a large reward is in hand, motivation to continue the new behavior tends to fade or disappear
- No act of kindness, no matter how small, is ever wasted

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not use rewards to coerce or manipulate people into unsafe or unethical behavior
- Ensure reward systems do not create perverse incentives that lead to cutting corners or hiding problems

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Choosing a reward that undermines the goal (e.g., rewarding exercise with junk food)
  **Solution:** Ask whether the reward is aligned with the goal you are trying to achieve. If it creates conflict with what you are trying to achieve, choose something else.
- **Problem:** Using rewards that are perceived as controlling or irrelevant to the recipient
  **Solution:** Ask the person directly: "How do you know you are loved?" or "How do you know you are appreciated?" Give them time to think and provide four to five answers.
- **Problem:** Relying solely on large, infrequent rewards that fail to sustain long-term motivation
  **Solution:** Shift to small, frequent rewards that preserve the joy and intrinsic value of the activity itself.

### Lesson 7: Notice Small Moments That Lead to Creative Breakthroughs

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Edward Jenner and the Small Moment That Cured Smallpox

Edward Jenner noticed that milkmaids did not get smallpox. While others had known this coincidence for years, they had bigger things to worry about and dismissed it. Jenner's appreciation of this commonplace fact led him to a revolutionary discovery: the milkmaids had been exposed to cowpox, which gave them immunity to smallpox. This theory led him to perfect the technique of vaccination. Great moments of progress come not from instantaneous flashes of insight but from ordinary attention to little things.

##### Example 2: The Olive That Saved American Airlines Half a Million Dollars

An American Airlines flight attendant noticed that passengers did not eat the olives in their salads. She reported this observation through management channels. The airline discovered that it was charged by its food supplier for salads based on the number of items they contained. A salad with one to four items cost less than a salad with five to eight items. The uneaten olives were the fifth item. When the airline dropped the olives and switched to a four-item salad, it saved five hundred thousand dollars a year.

##### Example 3: Velcro, Traveler's Checks, and Instant Cameras

George de Mestral noticed burrs sticking to his dog's fur and his clothes while walking. Instead of being merely irritated, he allowed the tenacious little burrs to intrigue him. His attention to this everyday moment led him to invent Velcro. J. C. Fargo, president of American Express, was frustrated that letters of credit were useless off the beaten path. His attention to an inconvenience led him to create the traveler's check—the precursor to the credit card. Edwin Land took a picture of his three-year-old daughter, who expected to see it immediately. Instead of brushing off her impatience as childish, he saw a possibility. Five years later, the first instant camera was invented.

##### Example 4: John Gottman's Five-to-One Ratio for Relationships

According to research reported by Dr. John Gottman, he could predict—with 93 percent accuracy—whether a couple would report a marriage that both partners describe as meeting their stated needs, a marriage that one or both partners describe as unsatisfying, or divorce within four years. The key finding was that in successful relationships, positive attention outweighed negative attention by a factor of five to one. This positive attention took the form of small gestures: using a pleased tone of voice when answering the phone from a partner, inquiring about dentist appointments, putting down the remote when the partner walked in the door, and arriving home on time or calling if delayed. These small moments were more predictive of a loving relationship than romantic vacations and expensive presents.

#### Step 1: Cultivate Curiosity and Openness to Small Moments

Train yourself to look for the unexpected, the inconvenient, and the overlooked in daily life. When a child complains about not seeing a photo immediately, do not dismiss the complaint—ask what possibility it might reveal. When a flight attendant mentions an uneaten olive, do not ignore the observation—investigate further. Pay attention to what is missing, what is excessive, and what people take for granted.

#### Step 2: Practice the Small Moments Awareness Exercise

To sharpen your awareness of small moments, find a person with an opposite opinion on a contentious issue (abortion, gun control, or school vouchers). Engage this person in a conversation in which all you do is ask questions with only one agenda: to discover and understand the reasons for his or her point of view. Do not argue, persuade, or sound judgmental. You will know you are succeeding when the person becomes more relaxed and chatty or more willing to share their thoughts as he or she perceives your interest and respect.

#### Step 3: Focus on Small Moments in Relationships

Instead of waiting for grand romantic gestures, train yourself to notice and appreciate small gestures from your partner: a pleasing tone of voice, a kind touch, remembering small details about their day. Praise your partner's personality or appearance, not just their actions. Say "I love the way your hair looks in the morning" rather than only "You're such a good cook." Acknowledge one moment each day that reveals your partner's unique character. These small moments provide consistent tending and nurturing, which are the foundation of strong relationships.

#### Step 4: When Stuck, Look for Small Moments of Delight

If you are implementing a plan for change but feel disengaged, frustrated, and stagnant, look around for unexpected moments of pleasure. Ask yourself: "When did I enjoy this activity even a little bit today?" Write it down. People who are most successful at improving their health habits are those who can transform exercise or eating well into a source of excitement and pride. Do not assume happiness will arrive when you reach your goal; find meaning in the small moments of the journey itself.

#### Step 5: Regain the Childlike Ability to Live in the Moment

Children can be highly focused in the moment, able to take pleasure and be absorbed in their activities and friends. As we develop, we gain the ability to recall the past and anticipate the future, which means we often spend too much time dwelling on what was or worrying about what will be. Through kaizen, we can regain the ability to find meaning in the moment. Whenever you find yourself lost in worry or regret, ask: "Do I need to learn to change anything based on this worry or regret?" If the answer to "Does this give me a clear sense of purpose?" is no, scan the room for an object or person that gives you a clear sense of purpose and focus your thoughts on that item for thirty seconds.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Pay attention to small annoyances, coincidences, and surprises in daily life
- ✅ Keep a journal of small moments that intrigue or delight you
- ✅ Ask questions instead of making commands to engage creative thinking
- ✅ Look for what is missing in a product, service, or situation rather than only what is present
- ✅ Talk to people at all levels of an organization—from janitors to executives—to uncover unexpected insights
- ❌ Do not dismiss small moments as trivial or unimportant
- ❌ Do not assume that innovation only comes from dramatic flashes of insight
- ❌ Do not wait for a perfect, large solution when a small observation can lead to a breakthrough

#### Keep In Mind

- Progress often springs from attention to the little things, not instantaneous flashes of insight
- Paying attention to small moments requires curiosity and an open mind
- Even if you are not trying to start a large business, small moments can help you see through mental blocks
- The true creator may be recognized by an ability to always find significance in the commonest and humblest things

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not use the practice of identifying small moments as an excuse to ignore serious safety hazards or ethical violations
- When identifying small moments in a professional context, ensure you are not violating confidentiality or privacy

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Dismissing small observations because they seem too trivial to matter
  **Solution:** Remember that Velcro, the traveler's check, and the instant camera all sprang from small moments. Write down every curious observation and revisit it later.
- **Problem:** Waiting for a dramatic, large breakthrough instead of acting on small insights
  **Solution:** Ask yourself: "What is one tiny thing I noticed today that could lead to a small improvement?" Act on it immediately.
- **Problem:** Being too busy or stressed to notice small moments around you
  **Solution:** Practice the thirty-second pleasure scan. When worried, find one small thing to focus on that brings you joy. This trains the brain to live in the moment.

### Lesson 8: Make Kaizen a Lifelong Practice of Continuous Improvement

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Kaizen in Business and Life Beyond Goals

Kaizen is not just a tool for crossing a finish line such as losing weight or getting a promotion. It is a process that is never done. When kaizen was first adopted in the early 1930s, before World War II, the intent was to enhance manufacturing quality in an already-working system. Just as an elite marathon runner continues to search for ways to reduce the time by one additional second from the previous benchmark off his or her best time, you can seek strategies to constantly improve your life. Take an extra moment to show excitement about a young person's efforts, write a kind note to a loved one, or take one more set of stairs.

##### Example 2: Service and Gratitude as Spiritual Elements of Kaizen

Kaizen has two spiritual elements: service and gratitude. Service means looking for small ways to touch people's lives daily—letting a driver into your lane, thanking a sales clerk, smiling at someone in a hallway. Gratitude in kaizen is not about financial gains but about being grateful for health, for the next breath, for moments with a friend. When Warren Zevon was asked how he faced his terminal cancer diagnosis, he answered: "Enjoy every sandwich." This means to find meaning in each present moment, reflecting consistent application of kaizen principles.

#### Step 1: Commit to One Small Step of Continuous Improvement Daily

Even after you have achieved a goal, do not put kaizen in a drawer. Continue to seek small improvements in health, relationships, career, and personal standards. Ask yourself each day: "What is one small step I could take to improve my health, relationships, career, or other areas of life?" Keep the question open and playful.

#### Step 2: Practice Small Acts of Kindness and Curiosity

Focus on being generous in daily thoughts and actions so that you do not hoard your kindness for important people or events. Spend it freely when your children anger you or when an employee deserves a small compliment. Respect yourself by taking small steps toward improving your health and relationships; respect others by asking them small questions.

#### Step 3: Use Small Steps to Serve Others and the World

In business, every small change should be to the benefit of the customer. Successful corporations such as Amazon, Starbucks, and Southwest Airlines define themselves as primarily devoted to service. Each employee in a kaizen culture is asked to look each day for ways to improve the process or product: lowering cost, increasing quality, and always serving the customer.

#### Step 4: Adopt an Attitude of Gentle Patience and Optimism

Give your mind permission to make leaps on its own schedule, in its own time. Instead of aggressively forcing yourself into a military-style discipline approach about change, trust that your body and brain have the power to help you achieve your goals. This trust often takes the form of a gentle, patient attitude in the face of challenges—whether they are perceived as insurmountable or mundane.

#### Step 5: Reflect on the Profound Question of Kaizen

As you incorporate kaizen into your routines and discover its power, you will have begun to answer an important question: What more important task does this life hold than to draw out the possibility in each moment?

#### Best Practices

- ✅ See kaizen as a lifelong process, not a one-time project with a finish line
- ✅ Look for small ways to serve others daily, in business and in personal life
- ✅ Practice gratitude for small, everyday blessings rather than waiting for grand achievements
- ✅ Give the mind permission to change when it is ready
- ✅ Treat every small act of kindness as inherently important
- ❌ Do not abandon kaizen once you reach a goal; keep seeking incremental improvement
- ❌ Do not force change or rush the process; gentle patience produces lasting results
- ❌ Do not hoard your kindness and curiosity for special occasions; practice them daily

#### Keep In Mind

- In the author's view, kaizen requires willingness to try small steps without immediate proof of efficacy—not necessarily religious trust, but a belief in the power of your body and brain to help you achieve your goals.
- Large changes begin with small actions, but the journey of continuous improvement never ends
- Small acts of kindness and compassion can change ourselves and extend to influence others
- You do not have to try all six kaizen strategies; use the ones that speak most clearly to you

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not use kaizen as a reason to delay necessary professional medical or psychological intervention for serious mental health or medical conditions
- Ensure that the pursuit of continuous improvement does not lead to burnout or work-life imbalance

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Believing that kaizen is only for achieving specific goals, then abandoning it after success
  **Solution:** Treat kaizen as an enduring philosophy. After reaching a goal, ask: "What is one small step I could take to maintain or improve this area?"
- **Problem:** Using kaizen to push yourself too hard, contradicting the principle of gentle, patient change
  **Solution:** Remember that kaizen asks for small, comfortable steps. If you are exhausted or dreading the activity, make the step smaller.
- **Problem:** Forgetting the spiritual elements of service and gratitude in the rush to achieve goals
  **Solution:** Daily, identify one small way you served someone or one small thing you are grateful for. Write it down or share it with someone.
