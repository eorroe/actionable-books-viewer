# Getting to Yes with Yourself - Chapter 4: Stay in the Zone
## From Resistance to Acceptance

### Overview

Chapter 4 teaches that the key to getting to yes with others is first getting to yes with yourself in the present moment. The chapter explains that our best performance comes from being in a state of relaxed alertness—being "in the zone"—which requires letting go of internal resistance to the past, future, and present. By accepting life as it is rather than fighting it, we free ourselves to spot present opportunities, access our natural creativity, and reach mutually satisfying agreements. The chapter provides practical methods for accepting the past, trusting the future, and embracing the present, illustrated through real-world examples from high-stakes negotiations, mountain climbing, and personal relationships.

### When to Follow This Chapter's Teachings

- When preparing for a sensitive negotiation or difficult conversation
- When feeling stuck in past resentment, regret, or self-blame
- When anxiety about future outcomes is impairing your performance
- When you keep missing openings in conversations because you're distracted
- When struggling to let go of control in a relationship or situation
- When experiencing burnout or dissatisfaction with the present moment
- When you need to access your creativity and natural problem-solving ability

## Lessons From Chapter 4

### Lesson 1: The Inner Yes to Life — Accepting the Past, Trusting the Future, and Embracing the Present

#### Real Examples

##### Example 1: Venezuela Political Crisis Negotiation (p. 60-62)

William Ury was invited by the Carter Center to mediate the 2002 Venezuelan political crisis between President Hugo Chavez and the opposition. Millions of people were on the streets, civil war seemed imminent, and Ury had only minutes with Chavez. Instead of reciting prepared recommendations, he listened attentively, asked Chavez about his daughter, and let him tell his personal story about resigning from the military rather than order troops to kill civilians. When Chavez asked for Ury's thoughts, Ury offered one key recommendation: open dialogue with the opposition. Chavez reacted angrily. Rather than argue, Ury asked a powerful question: "What action if any could they possibly take tomorrow morning that would send you a credible signal that they were ready to change?" Within minutes, Chavez agreed to work with Ury to develop practical trust-building actions. The meeting lasted 2.5 hours instead of a few minutes because Ury stayed present and looked for openings rather than pushing his agenda.

**Use Case:** High-stakes political mediation; sensitive negotiations with hostile parties; any situation where you have limited time with a powerful person.

##### Example 2: Mountain Climbing and the Art of Letting Go (p. 64-65)

As a young mountain climber, Ury learned to rappel by gradually relaxing his grip on the rope—starting with a few seconds, feeling safe, then loosening more. If he held on too tightly, he would get stuck and unable to descend. In one storm, Ury and his friend Dusty wrapped their rope around a small pine tree as an anchor, began rappelling, and the tree uprooted. They stopped, found a more reliable anchor (a boulder), and rappelled safely. Ury draws the parallel: we need to be anchored to something solid (a sense that life is friendly, inner resources, values) before we can safely let go of our controlling grip on circumstances.

**Use Case:** Learning to release control; building psychological safety before taking risks; understanding that letting go requires preparation.

##### Example 3: Craig the Business Client (p. 66)

A client named Craig was enmeshed in a bitter business dispute. He admitted: "When I think of what Craig has done to me, I feel furious. So it gives me pleasure to attack him. If I settle our dispute, what will my life be like without my private war?" He was so focused on revenge and the past that he had lost sight of his true negotiation objectives. Once he let go and settled the dispute, he felt "much lighter," and his children noticed—commenting that "Daddy is not on the cell phone all the time."

**Use Case:** Business disputes consumed by revenge; letting go of past grievances in negotiations; recognizing when the fight has become its own reward.

##### Example 4: Nelson Mandela and Forgiveness (p. 66-67)

After 27 years in prison, Mandela chose to let go of hatred toward his former jailers rather than remain their prisoner. He said, "I felt hatred and fear but I said to myself, if you hate them when you get in that car, you will still be their prisoner. I wanted to be free and so I let it go." By forgiving, he freed himself and inspired thousands of South Africans to do the same, including fellow prisoner Vusumzi Mcongo, who had been severely tortured: "We cannot live with broken hearts...To stay with the past will only bring you into turmoil."

**Use Case:** Deep historical grievances; political reconciliation; post-conflict forgiveness; personal liberation from victimhood.

##### Example 5: Judith and Her Son Ben (p. 70-72)

Judith struggled with her son Ben's intense rebellion from age 9 to 13. She was on a "roller coaster of hurt and anger, helplessness and determination." She asked herself: "What is the worst thing that could happen here?" and realized: "Other than my child dying, the worst thing I could imagine was that I would end up having active relationships with only two of my three children." She realized she could live with that. She gradually let go of needing his acknowledgment, love, or approval. Paradoxically, this acceptance led Ben to eventually come closer to her, apologize, and express his love.

**Use Case:** Strained parent-child relationships; teenage rebellion; letting go of control in family dynamics; paradox of acceptance leading to change.

##### Example 6: Gabi's Medical Journey and Reframing (p. 67, 72)

Ury's daughter Gabi underwent 14 major surgeries. Rather than resent or regret the experience, Ury and his wife Lizanne reframed it as a "blessed shock" that challenged them to grow and draw on their inner resources. Gabi herself, despite her medical challenges, "doesn't lose any time looking back with resentment or regret or feeling sorry for herself. She shakes it off. She has a zest for life." Ury uses her laser-like focus on the present as a reminder to let go himself.

**Use Case:** Reframing trauma or hardship; finding meaning in suffering; parenting through illness; maintaining presence amid crisis.

##### Example 7: The Businessman and WATNA (p. 68-69)

A businessman told Ury: "I also like to think about my WATNA—my Worst Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. Because I worry a lot about what's going to happen if things go wrong...I say to myself: 'If they're not going to kill me, then I will probably survive.' And I laugh it off." This reality-testing question helps him let go of unnecessary fear.

**Use Case:** Pre-negotiation anxiety; catastrophic thinking; using worst-case analysis to gain perspective and reduce fear.

##### Example 8: Roger Fisher's Present-Opportunity Focus (p. 62)

Ury's mentor Roger Fisher, co-author of Getting to Yes, focused not on understanding the history of a conflict or predicting its future, but on the present opportunity: "Who can do what today to move this conflict toward resolution?" This focus on actionable present steps transformed how Ury approached mediation.

**Use Case:** Mediation methodology; conflict resolution framing; moving from analysis to action.

#### Step 1: In Sensitive Situations, Listen First and Look for Openings

When approaching a sensitive negotiation or interaction, resist the urge to immediately give advice or push your agenda. Instead, listen attentively, ask open-ended questions, stay focused on the present moment, and look for openings where the other person signals receptivity. As Ury discovered with Chavez, "if I had followed my first thought to begin the meeting by reciting my recommendations, the president would have cut the meeting short after a few minutes." The power to transform the conflict lies in the present moment—in spotting the opening when it appears.

#### Step 2: Build a Solid Anchor Before Letting Go

Before you can relax your grip on control, make sure you are anchored to something solid. This means developing a sense that the world is friendly, cultivating inner resources (skills, values, self-trust), and reframing your picture of life so that you feel secure enough to let go. Like the mountain climber who finds a reliable boulder before rappelling, you need a trustworthy anchor. "Our ability to relax and let life flow naturally depends on how solidly anchored we feel in a friendly world."

#### Step 3: Accept the Past by Reframing and Forgiving

To release the burden of the past, practice reframing your experiences—give positive meaning to even the most difficult events. We cannot change what happened, but we can change the meaning we assign to it. Practice forgiveness—not condoning what happened, but accepting it and freeing yourself from its weight. The first beneficiary of forgiveness is yourself. Forgiving yourself is equally important: "If we all hold on to the mistake, we can't see our own glory in the mirror because we have the mistake between our faces and the mirror."

#### Step 4: Trust the Future by Testing Your Fears

To release anxiety about the future, practice reality-testing your fears. Ask yourself: "What is the worst thing that can happen here?" Most of the time, you'll find you can live with the worst outcome. Observe your fear when it shows up, take a deep breath, and consciously release it. Remember your "inner BATNA"—your commitment to take care of your needs and your confidence that life is on your side. "The pessimist sees the difficulty in every opportunity. The optimist sees the opportunity in every difficulty."

#### Step 5: Embrace the Present by Releasing Expectations

To stay in the present, let go of "should" and "should not"—these words signal unaccepted expectations about how life ought to be. Accept reality as it is, regardless of how painful. This is not resignation—"constructive change starts from accepting reality regardless of how painful it might be—not from losing time and energy resisting it." Visit the past briefly to learn, visit the future to plan, but make your home in the present. Focus on what lasts (life itself, nature, the universe) while accepting what passes. "Let the passing pass, let the lasting last."

#### Step 6: Practice Gradual Letting Go

Letting go is a skill that develops with practice, like learning to rappel. Start small: relax your grip for a few seconds, feel that you are safe, then loosen a little more, followed by holding on again. Over time, it gets easier until you don't even have to think about it. "Once I did let go of the rope, there was nothing to do except to enjoy the view."

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Listen first; offer advice only when asked
- ✅ Stay focused on the present moment and actively look for openings in conversations
- ✅ Anchor yourself to something solid (inner resources, values, friendly worldview) before letting go of control
- ✅ Reframe difficult past experiences to extract meaning and growth
- ✅ Forgive others and yourself to free yourself from the weight of resentment
- ✅ Replace fear with conscious trust in your ability to handle whatever comes
- ✅ Make your psychological home in the present; visit past and future only briefly
- ✅ Notice "should" and "should not" language in your thinking as signals of unaccepted expectations
- ✅ Ask "What is the worst thing that can happen here?" to test anxious predictions
- ✅ Focus on what lasts (life, nature) while accepting what passes
- ✅ Practice gradual letting go—start small, build comfort, then release further

#### Keep In Mind

- The key to staying in the zone is to say **yes** to life—accept the past, trust the future, and embrace the present as they are
- Being in the zone (or "flow") is a state of relaxed alertness where your best performance and greatest satisfaction occur
- Pain may be inevitable, but suffering is optional—it is our resistance to pain that causes suffering
- "We are destined to lose many things. That is the nature of life. Never mind. Just don't lose the present. Nothing is worth it. There is nothing more important than 'this,' the fullness of life right now."
- Acceptance is the prerequisite for constructive change, not its enemy
- Forgiveness does not mean condoning or forgetting—it means accepting what happened and freeing yourself
- The "present" is literally a gift—accept it as it is, even if it's not the gift you wanted
- Trust is not a one-time shift but a conscious choice made many times a day

#### Security & Safety Notes

- In physically dangerous situations (e.g., rappelling), ensure you have a reliable anchor before letting go—never release control without confirming your safety
- In dangerous conflict zones, fear is a natural response, but catastrophic thinking ("What's the worst that can happen?") can help calibrate real vs. imagined threats
- The chapter references real-world high-risk contexts (civil war mediation, mountain climbing in storms) where staying present has literal life-or-death implications

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Rushing to give advice or push your agenda in a negotiation, causing the other party to disengage or end the interaction prematurely.
  **Solution:** Listen first, ask open-ended questions, and look for openings before offering recommendations.

- **Problem:** Being mentally distracted by past regrets or future worries, causing you to miss present opportunities for connection or agreement.
  **Solution:** Practice staying in the zone through relaxed alertness and present-moment focus.

- **Problem:** Trying to force control over outcomes or other people's behavior, which creates more suffering and often backfires.
  **Solution:** Gradually practice letting go of control, starting with small, low-stakes situations, building up to larger ones.

- **Problem:** Attempting to let go without being psychologically anchored first—feeling ungrounded and unsafe.
  **Solution:** Build inner resources, reframe life as friendly, and establish a solid sense of self before releasing control.

- **Problem:** Using "should" and "should not" to resist current reality, prolonging conflict and misery.
  **Solution:** Notice the language, accept reality as it is, and then decide what constructive action to take from that place of acceptance.

- **Problem:** Holding onto past resentments, which consumes you more than it harms the other person.
  **Solution:** Practice forgiveness—not for their sake, but for yours. Holding a grudge is like carrying your bags while traveling on a train.

- **Problem:** Excessive worry about the future (WATNA thinking) that impairs your ability to perform in the present.
  **Solution:** Ask "What is the worst thing that can happen?" to gain perspective, then consciously release the fear.

- **Problem:** Trying to change a situation or relationship before first accepting it as it is.
  **Solution:** Accept the present reality first; paradoxically, this acceptance often creates the conditions for healthy change to emerge naturally.

- **Problem:** Forgetting that pain is part of life but suffering is optional—resisting pain amplifies it.
  **Solution:** Allow pain to be present without adding the layer of resistance; say "yes" to the experience even when it's difficult.
