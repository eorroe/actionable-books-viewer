# Getting to Yes with Yourself
## Chapter 5: Respect Them Even If

### Overview

Chapter 5 teaches that the cheapest and highest-yielding concession in any difficult interaction is respect. It explains how giving others basic human dignity—even when they reject or attack us—breaks destructive cycles of mutual antagonism and opens the path to genuine agreement. The chapter argues that respect is indivisible: honoring others' humanity honors our own, and the ability to extend respect outward begins with the inner work of getting to yes with ourselves.

---

### When to Follow Chapter 5 Teachings

- When entering a negotiation or difficult conversation where tension, mistrust, or past conflict exists
- When feeling rejected, excluded, or attacked by another person or group
- When managing labor, community, or cross-cultural relationships that have broken down
- When you notice a cycle of retaliation and want to be the one to break it
- When seeking to understand someone whose views, actions, or identity seem oppositional to yours
- When building inclusion in a team, organization, family, or community

### Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Respect Is the Highest-Yielding Concession You Can Give

Respect means giving positive attention and treating the other person with the dignity you would like to receive. Its Latin roots mean "to look again"—to see another human being with new eyes as worthy of positive regard. Respect is a behavior that originates as an attitude, and it is indivisible: when you respect others, you honor the same humanity within yourself.

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Union-Management Negotiation (p. 76–77)

Management's chief negotiator opened a tense labor contract talk with hostility: "We don't trust you and we don't like what you are doing." Union leader Dennis Williams controlled his impulse to counterattack and instead responded calmly and respectfully, reframing the conversation around shared purpose. That single respectful response set the tone for the entire negotiation. It was one of only three times in over sixty years that a contract agreement was reached without a major fight. People from the other side later told him they appreciated the gesture of respect.

##### Example 2: Syrian Rebel Leaders Interviews (p. 78–79)

While researching an end to the Syrian civil war, the author interviewed rebel leaders on the Turkish-Syrian border. The leaders—once pediatricians, dentists, lawyers, students—shared stories of torture, loss, and trauma. The interviewers set aside three hours per person so leaders felt truly heard. One ultraconservative Salafi commander of three thousand fighters, who looked like a Western stereotype of a fundamentalist, had won a national poetry prize before being arrested and tortured for a subversive poem. When asked his greatest concern if his side won, he said religious extremism—and added: "Please put yourself here and imagine that your child or wife is one of the numbers. Every single number has a life and a soul." Listening with genuine respect led to better understanding of the conflict and laid groundwork for a Syrian-led solution.

##### Example 3: The Abraham Path in Bethlehem (p. 87–90)

Presenting the Abraham Path project to Palestinian leaders in Bethlehem, the author faced two hours of skeptical, suspicious, and hostile questions—including whether the project was linked to foreign intelligence agencies, with gunshots audible in the square outside. Instead of becoming defensive, the author acknowledged their distrust as legitimate, stepped to their side, and told them: "The true leaders can only be the peoples in the region… the leadership role belongs to you." The atmosphere shifted. Critics became optimistic. The project gained local ownership and was later named the world's best new walking trail by National Geographic Traveller.

##### Example 4: Hostage Negotiations (p. 84–85)

A generation ago, standard hostage response was a bullhorn ultimatum followed by a SWAT assault—often resulting in death or injury. Today, professional hostage negotiators use a wholly different approach: be polite, give the hostage taker a hearing, listen with close attention, acknowledge their point of view, stay cool and courteous. Treating hostage takers with basic human respect gives them a face-saving way out. In the overwhelming majority of cases, the hostage taker surrenders and hostages go free unharmed.

##### Example 5: Azim Khamisa's Forgiveness (p. 85–86)

After his twenty-year-old son Tariq was killed by a fourteen-year-old gang member during a pizza delivery, Azim Khamisa experienced an extraordinary transformation. Rather than pursuing revenge, he put himself in the shoes of his son's killer, forgave him (without condoning the crime), and reached out to the boy's grandfather. The young perpetrator, Tony, broke down in remorse, pleaded guilty, and later accepted a job offer at the foundation Azim and the grandfather founded to stop youth violence. Azim's choice turned a cycle of violence into one of healing and purpose.

##### Example 6: Larry and Jose (p. 82)

Larry married a Mexican American woman and became the only non-Hispanic in her family, provoking unspoken resentment from his brother-in-law Jose. Ten years later, Jose apologized: he had lobbied behind the scenes to break them up and had felt bad about it for years. Jose changed his attitude from rejection to respect, finally accepting Larry into the family and resolving years of felt but unspoken conflict.

##### Example 7: Barry-Wehmiller Factory (p. 83)

A factory under new ownership shifted from treating employees as paid automatons to genuinely listening to them and welcoming their ideas. During the 2008 financial crisis, when competitors were laying off workers, CEO Robert Chapman asked everyone—top to bottom—to take a six-week unpaid leave so no one would be laid off. Employees and management credited the turnaround to this attitude of respect and inclusion.

##### Example 8: Abraham Lincoln (p. 83)

When a Yankee patriot chastised Lincoln for speaking sympathetically of the defeated South during the Civil War, Lincoln responded: "Madam, do I not destroy my enemies when I turn them into my friends?" Lincoln expanded his circle of respect to encompass even those who had taken up arms against the Union.

##### Example 9: Meditation and Compassion Research (p. 80–81)

A study by Professor David DeSteno found that 50 percent of people who practiced meditation for eight weeks gave up their seat to a person in visible pain, compared to only 16 percent of non-meditators—a threefold difference. Meditation enhances attention to others and fosters the view that all beings are connected, dissolving artificial social distinctions and increasing the capacity for elementary respect.

#### Step 1: Put Yourself in Their Shoes

Imagine the world through the other person's eyes. Ask: What does this feel like to be that person? If I had lived his or her life, how would I act and react? Listen not just to their words but to the feelings and unspoken thoughts behind them. Listen from within their frame of reference, not just your own. You may not be wholly accurate, but shared humanity makes your understanding far more accurate than you might expect.

#### Step 2: Expand Your Circle of Respect

Consciously choose to include people whom at first you might not want or think to include. Recognize the wound of exclusion and respond with the remedy of inclusion. Treat each person as a human being with dignity, talent, and a story worth hearing. Make real efforts to welcome their ideas, listen to their concerns, and signal that they matter.

#### Step 3: Respect Even Those Who Reject You

When someone rejects, attacks, or dismisses you, your instinct is to pull back and reject them in return. Instead, do the opposite: respond with respect. This is a form of psychological jujitsu. Surprise them. Take the lead. Meet exclusion with inclusion. Meeting rejection with respect gives the other person a face-saving way out and breaks the cycle of mutual antagonism.

#### Step 4: Go to the Balcony Before Reacting

When you feel rejected or attacked, step back—go to the balcony—to observe your feelings and thoughts rather than being swept away by them. Remind yourself of your inner BATNA: your commitment to take care of your deepest needs. When you feel confident in your own ability to make yourself happy, you become less reactive to another person's offensive behavior. Having given yourself respect, you can more easily give others respect.

#### Step 5: Acknowledge Their Humanity Before Addressing Their Demands

Respect others' basic humanity without approving of their behavior or liking them as individuals. You can say no to their demands while saying yes to their dignity. Even when drawing clear limits to protect yourself or others, treat the other person as a human being just like yourself.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Give respect as the first move; it costs the least and yields the most
- ✅ Listen with full positive attention, from the other person's frame of reference
- ✅ Respond to rejection with respect rather than retaliation
- ✅ Include rather than exclude; expand your circle of respect intentionally
- ✅ Go to the balcony when under attack—observe your reactions before acting
- ✅ Put yourself in the other person's shoes to understand their underlying humanity
- ✅ Separate the person's dignity from their demands or actions
- ✅ Practice self-awareness (e.g., meditation) to strengthen your capacity for respect
- ✅ Take responsibility for your own happiness so you are less reactive to others
- ✅ Step to the other side and invite them to take leadership when appropriate

#### Keep In Mind

- An attitude of respect cannot be forced; it can only be nurtured. It emerges organically when you have already given yourself respect through inner work.
- When we feel rejected, we naturally reject back; when we feel excluded, we naturally exclude back. Breaking this pattern requires conscious choice.
- If we have chosen to take responsibility for our lives and actions, we are not likely to blame others.
- By paying attention inside yourself, you become better able to pay attention outside yourself.
- One person changing from antagonism to respect is often enough to change the tone and outcome of an entire interaction.
- It may not be easy, especially when under attack, but with practice and courage you can often turn mutual rejection into mutual respect.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Respecting someone's humanity does not mean ignoring injustice, evil, or wrongful actions. You can and should confront harmful behavior while still treating the person with dignity.
- Accepting those who reject you does not mean saying yes to their demands. Set clear limits for the sake of protecting yourself and others.
- Even in extreme situations like hostage taking or violent crime, the principle of respecting the person's humanity applies—while still taking whatever protective action is necessary.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Believing the other person must earn your respect before you give it. **Solution:** Respect is a choice, not a reward. You can give basic human respect without approving of specific behavior.
- **Problem:** Reacting impulsively when rejected or attacked, perpetuating a destructive cycle. **Solution:** Go to the balcony first. Observe your feelings before acting.
- **Problem:** Listening only from your own frame of reference, judging the other person's words by your standards. **Solution:** Practice listening from within the other person's frame of reference, hearing the feelings behind the words.
- **Problem:** Thinking respect is incompatible with setting limits or saying no. **Solution:** You can say no to demands while saying yes to dignity. The hostage negotiator says no to the hostage taker's demands while respecting the person.
- **Problem:** Trying to address every skeptical or hostile comment judiciously, appearing defensive and increasing suspicion. **Solution:** Acknowledge concerns, step to the other side, and invite them to take ownership or leadership.
