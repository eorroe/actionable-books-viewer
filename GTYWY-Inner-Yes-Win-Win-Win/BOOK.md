# Getting to Yes with Yourself (and Other Worthy Opponents)

## Overview

The book teaches readers how to master inner negotiation with themselves through six transformative steps, shifting from win-lose thinking to win-win-win outcomes that create inner satisfaction, stronger relationships, and positive impact on others.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When preparing for a difficult conversation or negotiation where you want to represent your interests clearly
- When you notice yourself reacting impulsively with anger, fear, or shutdown in a conflict before you have had a chance to think
- When you feel torn between conflicting priorities or uncertain about your true needs in a situation
- When you're stuck in the "3A trap" (attack, accommodate, avoid)
- When you catch yourself blaming others and want to uncover your own contribution to the dynamic
- When self-judgment is blocking your ability to act
- When you feel trapped in a conflict cycle of blame and counter-blame
- When negotiating with someone who appears more powerful than you
- When you find yourself making others responsible for your emotional needs
- When a relationship at home, work, or in the community is stuck in cycles of blame, resentment, or emotional withdrawal
- When you feel like a victim of circumstances beyond your control
- When you notice yourself slipping into win-lose thinking and need to shift to collaborative problem-solving
- When feeling trapped in a mindset of scarcity—believing there is not enough to go around
- When feeling dependent on others for your own happiness, satisfaction, or sense of self-worth
- When experiencing adversity, fear, or distrust that makes cooperation feel impossible
- When you find yourself seeing a counterpart as an enemy rather than a potential partner
- When a sensitive conversation with a colleague, family member, or counterpart requires careful preparation to avoid escalation
- When feeling stuck in past resentment, regret, or self-blame
- When anxiety about future outcomes is impairing your performance
- When entering a conversation where there is existing tension, mistrust, or a history of conflict
- When feeling rejected, excluded, or attacked by another person or group
- When you notice a cycle of retaliation and want to be the one to break it
- When mediating a conflict between others and need to help them move beyond adversarial win-lose dynamics
- When feeling stuck in a cycle of claiming value only for yourself
- When seeking to create agreements that benefit not just you and the other party, but the broader community or organization
- When feeling obligated to give but experiencing burnout or resentment
- When an ongoing conflict at home or work has left you feeling stuck, resentful, or unwilling to engage
- When external achievements have not brought the satisfaction you expected and you need to find lasting contentment from within
- When you want to shift from adversarial win-lose dynamics to cooperative win-win-win outcomes

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Put Yourself in Your Shoes — From Self-Judgment to Self-Understanding

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Abilio Diniz Boardroom Battle

**Use Case:** High-stakes business dispute

Brazilian businessman Abilio Diniz was trapped in a bitter two-and-a-half-year dispute with his French business partner over control of Brazil's leading supermarket retailer. Despite being chair and major shareholder, he felt angry, frustrated, and disrespected. He didn't know whether to fight or settle and often reacted out of anger contrary to his interests. He was his own worthiest opponent. By first uncovering his deepest need—freedom—he was able to resolve the dispute in just four days of negotiation.

##### Example 2: Political Mediation with Shouting President

**Use Case:** High-pressure diplomatic situation

During a tense political mediation, a country's president shouted angrily at Ury for almost thirty minutes. Ury used the balcony technique: silently noting his sensations, emotions, and thoughts ("Isn't it interesting? My jaw feels clenched. I notice some fear showing up. My cheeks feel flushed. Am I feeling embarrassed?"). This self-observation neutralized the emotional effect and allowed him to recover the conversation.

##### Example 3: Charlotte and Her Four-Year-Old Son

**Use Case:** Domestic/parenting conflict

A mother named Charlotte struggled with her son's refusal to go to bed, which triggered powerful angry reactions. By investigating the feeling of anger with curiosity, she learned to spot the "crossroads" between explosive anger and calm resolution. She realized that if she reacted with anger, her son would distance himself, but if she remained calm, she could advance her core interest in a close relationship.

##### Example 4: The Kitchen Table Exercise

**Use Case:** Daily self-awareness practice

Ury developed a morning visualization exercise where he imagines sitting at a kitchen table. As each thought or emotion shows up (anxiety, fear, shame, pride), he offers it an imaginary seat. He welcomes all customers, including the inner judge, treating them as old friends. This keeps him aware of his regular emotional patterns so they don't catch him by surprise.

##### Example 5: Jamil Mahuad's Peace Negotiation

**Use Case:** International conflict resolution

Former Ecuadorian president Jamil Mahuad learned to deal with painful feelings by putting them in the spotlight. Having been taught that sadness was not well-received by males in his family, he had difficulty connecting with grief. By recognizing and bringing his shadow emotions to light, he was able to control his anger and operate from a balcony perspective during a difficult peace negotiation with Peru, ending the longest-running war in the hemisphere.

##### Example 6: The Holy Grail Knight

**Use Case:** Metaphor for asking the right question

A young knight searching for the Holy Grail failed on his first attempt because he was tongue-tied. Decades later, the older, wiser knight asked the injured king a simple but powerful question: "What ails thee?" By listening to the king's woes and uncovering his deepest needs, a human connection grew, and the king gave him the Grail.

##### Example 7: Abilio's Continued Struggle

**Use Case:** Long-term self-mastery

Even after uncovering his deepest need (freedom), Abilio encountered internal difficulties. In a magazine interview about moving beyond the battle, he mentioned his adversary by full name thirty-eight times. In a board meeting, despite his intention to stay calm, he repeatedly called his opponents cowards. He persisted through long talks with his wife and family, weekly therapy, and dedication to spending more time on the balcony. By understanding and accepting himself, he became his own ally rather than his own worst opponent.

##### Example 8: The Inner Yes Opens External Negotiation

**Use Case:** Business dispute resolution

Before approaching his adversary at the negotiation table, Abilio took concrete actions to pursue his freedom: became chair of another major company's board, found a new office outside company headquarters, went on a prolonged holiday with his family, and began exploring a new business deal. By saying yes to his needs, he opened the possibility of a genuine negotiation where neither side would lose.

#### Step 1: Observe Yourself from the Balcony

The balcony is a metaphor for a mental and emotional place of perspective, calm, and self-control. Before, during, and after any problematic conversation or negotiation, step back mentally and observe the situation as if watching a play from the balcony. Notice your sensations, emotions, and thoughts without getting caught up in them. When triggered, you have a simple choice: to identify (observe) the thought or to get identified (caught up) with it.

#### Step 2: Name Your Emotions and Thoughts

Naming helps you identify so that you don't get identified. As you observe passing thoughts, emotions, and sensations, name them: "Oh, that is my old friend Fear; there goes the Inner Critic." This neutralizes their effect and helps you maintain balance and calm. You can even give them humorous names like "Freddy Fear," "Judge Judy," or "Anger Annie." As soon as you name the character in the play, you distance yourself from him or her.

#### Step 3: Avoid the 3A Trap

When we react, we typically fall into the "3A trap": we attack, we accommodate (give in), or we avoid altogether. These common reactions serve neither our true interests nor the other person's. Once the fight-or-flight reaction gets triggered, blood flows from our brain to our limbs, and our ability to think clearly diminishes. When we react, we give away our power. The alternative is to observe ourselves and choose a considered response.

#### Step 4: Practice Daily Self-Observation

To develop a habit of self-observation, cultivate your "inner scientist." You are the investigator, and the subject of your investigation is yourself—psychologists call this "me-search." Reserve a period of time once a day (as little as five or ten minutes) to sit quietly in a comfortable position, close your eyes, and simply watch your passing thoughts and feelings. If you get caught up in a thought, simply notice that you were caught up and go back to observing.

#### Step 5: Let the Mental Fizz Settle

Imagine a glass of water freshly filled from the faucet—full of fizz, unable to see through. If you wait and let it settle, the bubbles dissipate and the water turns crystal clear. This is what we're trying to do with our minds. Before a challenging phone call or meeting, take even a single minute of silence to yourself. One minute alone with eyes closed helps you observe your thoughts, feelings, and sensations and quiet your mind.

#### Step 6: Make the Balcony Your Home Base

The goal is not just to visit the balcony occasionally but to make it your home base. With practice, you can learn to be on the stage enacting the drama while simultaneously watching it from the balcony. The more you can live your life with clarity and calm, the more effectively you will be able to deal with others and pursue your interests with ease and success.

#### Step 7: Accept Yourself Just as You Are

Self-acceptance is a yes to self, perhaps the greatest gift you can give yourself. If self-judgment is a no to self, self-acceptance transforms you from your own opponent to your own ally. As Carl Rogers noted: "The curious paradox is that when I accept myself as I am, then I change."

#### Step 8: Make Concrete Actions to Pursue Your Needs

Saying yes to yourself involves taking concrete actions to meet your deepest needs, even before negotiating with others. For Abilio, this meant structural changes in his life that embodied his commitment to freedom. These actions build internal credibility and create space for external negotiation.

#### Step 9: Practice Continually

The journey from self-judgment to self-understanding takes hard and continual work. It is common to be torn and undecided in problematic situations. That is why the patient and courageous practice of putting yourself in your shoes is so vital.

#### Step 10: Use Support Systems

Abilio's process included long intimate talks with his wife and family, weekly therapy, and conversations with his advisor. You don't have to do this alone. Use your support systems to help you understand and accept yourself.

### Lesson 2: Develop Your Inner BATNA — Breaking Free from the Blame Game

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Soviet-American Nuclear Negotiations

**Use Case:** International negotiation facilitation

During Cold War conferences in the mid-1980s, Ury observed that opening sessions with mutual accusations poisoned the atmosphere and wasted valuable time. By labeling the first session "Mutual Accusations" and scheduling it before breakfast, the facilitators eliminated the blame game entirely, allowing substantive negotiation to begin immediately.

##### Example 2: Johnson & Johnson Tylenol Crisis (1982)

**Use Case:** Corporate crisis management

When seven people died from cyanide-laced Tylenol capsules in Chicago, CEO James Burke faced a critical choice. Experts advised against a nationwide recall since the incidents were localized and not J&J's fault. Burke chose to take full responsibility anyway, recalling all Tylenol products nationwide at an estimated $100 million cost. The result was a dramatic restoration of public trust and eventual recovery of market share, demonstrating that taking responsibility can transform crisis into opportunity.

##### Example 3: Sam's Car Accidents

**Use Case:** Personal behavioral change

A young man repeatedly destroyed vehicles through accidents while blaming external factors—other drivers, road conditions, poor lighting. After observing his own pattern and probing his suppressed feelings of insecurity and anger, he took full responsibility for his aggressive driving. Once he got to yes with himself, the accident pattern ceased entirely and family tensions resolved.

##### Example 4: Jerry White and the Land Mine

**Use Case:** Transforming victimhood into leadership

Jerry White, a college student studying abroad in Jerusalem, stepped on a land mine left from the Six-Day War and lost his leg. Lying in a hospital bed for months, a fellow patient told him: "Jerry, this will be the worst thing that ever happened to you or the best thing. You decide." White chose not to settle into victimhood, took responsibility for his life, co-founded Survivor Corps, and helped lead the International Campaign to Ban Landmines, which won the Nobel Peace Prize.

##### Example 5: Susan and Frank's Marriage

**Use Case:** Relationship transformation through inner responsibility

Susan blamed her husband Frank for their communication problems, believing he was at fault for never talking with her. Nagging only made him withdraw further. Through therapy, she realized her own nagging was a significant contribution to the negative dynamic, recognized Frank's trauma-based need for safety, and stopped trying to control him. She made an unconditional commitment to take care of her needs regardless of his behavior. The criticism stopped, Frank felt safe enough to open up, and their marriage was not only saved but transformed.

##### Example 6: Ury's Daughter Gabriela's Medical Journey

**Use Case:** Parenting through chronic illness

Ury and his wife faced their daughter Gabi's congenital VATER syndrome and fourteen major surgeries. They learned to stop blaming themselves, insensitive doctors, or life itself, and instead took responsibility for their own psychological needs. By managing their anxiety and treating doctors as partners rather than adversaries, they became more effective advocates for Gabi. One doctor initially criticized in front of them later became a close friend who provided hours of free consultation.

##### Example 7: Turkish-Kurdish Peace Dialogue

**Use Case:** Conflict resolution through genuine apology

At a confidential meeting between Turkish and Kurdish opinion leaders during Turkey's civil war, a retired Turkish general made a sincere apology: "As a former leader of the armed forces of Turkey, I want to acknowledge the suffering of countless Kurdish villagers... personally, I want to say that I am profoundly sorry." This single statement—rooted in the general's prior inner work of owning his actions—dramatically changed the atmosphere and opened the way for working together to end the war.

#### Step 1: Identify When You Are Blaming

Notice when you are caught in the blame game—blaming others, circumstances, or yourself for a conflict or problem. Recognize that blame feels temporarily righteous and innocent but actually undermines your power by making you a victim of external forces.

#### Step 2: Reframe the Core Question

Shift the guiding question from "Who is to blame?" to "What do we have to learn?" or "What can I do?" When faced with adversity, choose curiosity over blame. Ask yourself what lesson life is bringing rather than resisting your circumstances.

#### Step 3: Go to the Balcony and Then Listen to Yourself

When emotions run high, mentally step onto the balcony to gain perspective. Then listen inwardly to your underlying feelings—fear, anxiety, shame, anger—rather than numbing them or acting on them immediately. Accept these emotions with self-compassion. The way forward is through the pain, not around it.

#### Step 4: Take Responsibility for Your Life

Accept that you are responsible for your words, actions, and reactions. Stop deflecting responsibility onto external factors like your boss, family, or circumstances. Own your failures and faults as well as your successes. Understand that while you may not choose your circumstances, you can choose your response to them.

#### Step 5: Develop Your Inner BATNA

Make an unconditional commitment to yourself to take care of your deepest needs no matter what other people do or don't do. This commitment is your Inner BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement). It removes the responsibility for meeting your true needs from the other person's shoulders and places it back where it belongs: with you.

#### Step 6: Own Your Relationships

Recognize that every relationship has at least two parties contributing to its dynamics. Acknowledge your own contribution to problematic relationships, even if it seems small compared to the other person's. It takes two to create the mess—and only one to begin to transform it.

#### Step 7: Take Responsibility for Your Needs

Identify your core psychological needs for satisfaction, fulfillment, connection, and protection. Commit to meeting them yourself rather than making others responsible. In negotiations, this means you are less dependent on the other side to satisfy you, giving you confidence, freedom, and power.

#### Step 8: Transform Your Approach to Others

When you have gotten to yes with yourself, approach others from a position of inner strength rather than neediness. Communicate your boundaries clearly, respect the other person's autonomy, and focus on problem-solving rather than blame. Like Susan, you can say: "I'm no longer willing to accept X, but I won't pressure you to change. I'll interpret your actions as indicating your decision, and I'll make my decision accordingly."

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Take the "response-ability"—the ability to respond constructively to any situation
- ✅ Focus on what you can control: your own actions, reactions, and commitments
- ✅ Accept responsibility before offering apologies or seeking reconciliation
- ✅ View yourself as the director of your life's play, not just a victim of destiny
- ✅ Treat pain and fear by moving through them, not around them
- ✅ Reframe victim identity into leadership identity
- ✅ See relationships as systems where you have partial responsibility
- ✅ Cultivate self-understanding alongside self-responsibility to avoid self-pity or self-blame
- ✅ Make an unconditional commitment to your own well-being as your Inner BATNA
- ✅ Use your Inner BATNA as the foundation for developing your outer BATNA

#### Keep In Mind

- Self-understanding without self-responsibility risks dissolving into self-pity
- Self-responsibility without self-understanding can deteriorate into self-blame
- The blame game is tempting because it makes us feel innocent and righteous, but the costs are huge
- Taking responsibility may feel heavy but is actually liberating—it frees energy trapped in blaming others
- The moment you recognize you are in a prison of your own making, the walls begin to crumble
- You may not be the playwright of your life, but you can choose to be the director
- Even when you are only partly in control of circumstances, you have considerable control over your experiences
- Genuine power starts inside of you and is always available regardless of outer situations
- The best BATNA of all is the one that starts inside
- Responsibility equals power—the power to meet your deepest needs

#### Security & Safety Notes

- When dealing with medical professionals or other authority figures on whom you depend, maintain respect while also advocating assertively for your needs
- Create emotional safety for yourself before difficult interactions by managing your own anxiety levels
- Recognize when others' behavior may be rooted in trauma (like Frank's childhood trauma) without accepting harmful behavior
- Apologies from powerful figures should be evaluated based on the sincerity and prior inner work behind them

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Falling into the blame game when conflicts arise
  **Solution:** Immediately reframe from "Who is to blame?" to "What can I learn?" and "What can I do?"
- **Problem:** Making others responsible for meeting your core psychological needs
  **Solution:** Answer the question "Who is responsible for meeting my core psychological needs?" with "Myself" rather than "Someone else"
- **Problem:** Acting from neediness, which gives power to the other person
  **Solution:** Develop your Inner BATNA—make an unconditional commitment to take care of your needs no matter what
- **Problem:** Thinking taking responsibility means self-blame
  **Solution:** Understand that self-blame looks backward ("What a failure I have been"), while self-responsibility looks forward ("What can I do to make this successful?")
- **Problem:** Trying to control others' behavior
  **Solution:** Focus on controlling your own responses and commitments, not the other person's actions
- **Problem:** Going around pain instead of through it
  **Solution:** Deliberately face difficult emotions by entering the fear rather than steering around it
- **Problem:** Giving up during difficult negotiations because the other side seems more powerful
  **Solution:** Remember your Inner BATNA is always available and gives you confidence no matter the external power balance
- **Problem:** Assuming responsibility means accepting fault or guilt
  **Solution:** Reframe responsibility as "response-ability"—the ability to respond constructively, not to judge the past

### Lesson 3: Reframe Your Picture — From Unfriendly Universe to Friendly Life

#### Examples

##### Example 1: James and the Helicopter Mission

**Use Case:** High-stakes peace negotiation

A negotiation adviser named James was tasked with extracting a guerrilla commander from a jungle clearing to begin secret peace talks. When his helicopter landed, it was surrounded by hundreds of armed guerrillas shouting that it was a fatal trick. James had a choice: see the commander as a hostile enemy, or see him as a partner. He opened the helicopter door, walked up to the commander, and said, "Sir, I am now placing you under the personal protection of the president!" By treating the commander as a partner, the commander was able to treat him as a partner too. The commander boarded the helicopter, preliminary peace talks began, and six months later a full peace agreement was announced.

##### Example 2: Dr. Jill Bolte Taylor's Stroke

**Use Case:** Personal transformation through neuroscience

A Harvard neuroanatomist suffered a stroke at age 37 that flooded the left hemisphere of her brain. As her logical, boundary-making left brain went offline, she experienced euphoric happiness and a profound sense of connection to life—what Einstein called the "optical delusion" of separateness vanished. She later learned to voluntarily access this right-brain state through activities like waterskiing, guitar, and stained-glass art, and teaches that others can too.

##### Example 3: The Author's Paris Walk

**Use Case:** Setting constructive negotiation tone

Before a momentous, acrimonious business negotiation, the author took a walk in Paris and passed a sculpture exhibit featuring giant golden Buddhas with beaming smiles. Contemplating the statues inspired him to open the negotiation with the phrase: "Because life is too short! Life is too short for these mutually destructive conflicts..." That phrase set a constructive tone and the talks that followed were successful.

##### Example 4: Julio, the Economist

**Use Case:** Finding inner contentment

Julio, age 27, had achieved everything he had ever wanted—manager at a strategy firm, good relationship, MBA in New York—but woke up one day feeling sadness and emptiness. None of his achievements gave him peace. He slowed down, took up meditation, and spent more time in nature. He discovered the peace he wanted was already inside him. As his inner state shifted, his outer behavior shifted: he became less stressed, kinder, calmer—a better colleague, boss, and employee. By getting to yes with himself, he found it easier to get to yes with others.

##### Example 5: Gabi's Emergency Surgery

**Use Case:** Choosing gratitude during crisis

The author's daughter Gabi was hospitalized with sharp abdominal pains. After four days of worsening pain and an uncertain diagnosis, doctors performed a midnight emergency surgery for a total intestinal obstruction—the intestine was minutes away from bursting. Gabi recovered gradually. During that difficult time, Lizanne (the author's wife) chose gratitude over lamenting life's unfairness, learning a powerful lesson about acceptance and resilience that gave her renewed confidence in handling future adversities.

##### Example 6: Opa on His Deathbed

**Use Case:** Reframing a lifetime of negative assumptions

The author's father-in-law, Opa, who had witnessed the firebombing of Hamburg as a child and whose lifelong view was that "everything is against us," had a profound shift weeks before dying of cancer. He announced: "Here, we believe that everything is against us. Now I can see that everything is in our favor." Reframing his assumptions allowed him to relax, let go of fear, embrace death with gratitude, and die a fulfilled man surrounded by his loving family.

##### Example 7: Viktor Frankl's Patient in a Nazi Concentration Camp

**Use Case:** Finding meaning in extreme suffering

A young woman, knowing she would die within days in a concentration camp, told Dr. Viktor Frankl she was grateful fate had hit her so hard. Through her window she could see one branch of a chestnut tree with two blossoms, and she talked to it. The tree told her: "I am here—I am here—I am life, eternal life." In the midst of extreme suffering, she found a way to connect with life, make her own happiness, and relish her last hours with cheerfulness and gratitude.

##### Example 8: Einstein's Fundamental Question

**Use Case:** The power of reframing one's worldview

Albert Einstein, in the wake of World War II and the atomic bomb, posed what he believed was the most important question for each of us: "Is the universe a friendly place?" He reasoned that seeing the universe as hostile leads to treating others as enemies, arming ourselves, and eventual self-destruction. Seeing it as friendly leads to treating others as partners, cooperation, and survival. The answer is self-confirming: our response shapes our behavior and our interactions' outcomes.

#### Step 1: Remember Your Connection to Life

Human beings are not separate but inextricably woven into a larger web of living beings—biologically, economically, socially, and culturally. The sense of separateness Einstein called an "optical delusion of consciousness" is a left-brain construct. To dissolve this illusion:

- Engage in creative and physical activities that exercise the right brain: for example, waterskiing, playing an instrument, making art, climbing mountains, walking in nature, meditation, prayer, sitting in silence, listening to or playing beautiful music.
- Create a daily practice to develop your right-brain capacity. Spend time on your "inner mountaintop"—a walk in the park, a period of sitting in silence, meditation, prayer, art contemplation, or music.
- Engage in such activities daily to create and strengthen neural pathways back to the right side of the brain.
- When you face a difficult conversation or negotiation, access your right brain first—remember that you and the other person are part of the same whole. Use a simple phrase that calls to mind the bigger connected picture, such as "Life is too short for these mutually destructive conflicts."

#### Step 2: Remember Your Power to Make Your Own Happiness

Happiness is not something to be pursued outside yourself; it is something you make inside. Your capacity to manufacture happiness is your birthright.

- Recognize that major external events—winning millions or losing both legs—have surprisingly little long-term effect on happiness. After a year, people adapt.
- Understand that we change the way we see the world so that we can feel better. We are much more resilient than we imagine.
- Stop outsourcing your happiness to external conditions (money, success, status) or to other people (spouses, bosses, colleagues). Others cannot make you satisfied by relinquishing something you want.
- Reclaim your innate capacity to take care of your own deeper needs for contentment. Discover the simple things of life that make you happy.
- Use right-brain activities (time in nature, meditation, creative pursuits) to help reframe your view of life from scarcity to sufficiency or abundance.
- If you want to expand the pie in your negotiations, begin by finding ways to expand the pie inside yourself. Inner satisfaction creates outer satisfaction, which feeds back into more inner satisfaction—a virtuous circle that begins from within.

#### Step 3: Learn to Appreciate the Lessons That Life Brings You

Gratitude is one of the few attitudes that can measurably change people's lives. It is a gateway to what Wittgenstein called "absolute safety"—the state of mind in which one is inclined to say, "I am safe, nothing can injure me whatever happens."

- Cultivate gratitude actively and regularly. It produces measurable psychological, physical, and social benefits—for you and for those around you.
- Understand that gratitude does not mean denying what is painful; it means being able to see the bigger picture.
- When adversity strikes, choose gratitude over lamenting unfairness. Ask: What can I learn from this? How can this help me grow?
- In the midst of suffering or fear, find something—however small—to connect with life. It might be a tree, a flower, a piece of music, a memory. Use that connection to reframe your picture.
- Embrace life's challenges as being ultimately in your favor, as teachers that help you grow. You can choose whether or not to see challenges this way.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ View every difficult conversation as a choice between adversarial contest and collaborative problem-solving
- ✅ Before a high-stakes negotiation, engage your right brain—walk in nature, contemplate art, sit in silence—to dissolve the optical delusion of separation
- ✅ Begin difficult negotiations with a phrase that calls to mind the bigger connected picture, not the conflict
- ✅ Ask yourself Einstein's fundamental question consciously: Is the universe a friendly place?
- ✅ Cultivate a daily right-brain practice to make connection your default mode of living
- ✅ Look for ways to expand the inner pie first; outer expansion follows
- ✅ Choose gratitude actively in moments of adversity, fear, or suffering
- ✅ See the other side as a potential partner, even when they appear to be an enemy
- ✅ Trust in life as your ally, even when circumstances suggest otherwise
- ✅ Remember that happiness is manufactured inside, not pursued outside
- ✅ Take care of your own deeper needs for contentment rather than waiting for others to meet them

#### Keep In Mind

- Reframing is not always easy. In the heat of conflict, it is all too easy to fall into win-lose thinking and see the other side as an adversary. Fear of scarcity dominates us. The best way to change the game is to change the frame.
- It is not always easy to see life as being on your side, particularly in the midst of adversity. But it is possible.
- The less dependent you feel on others to satisfy your needs for happiness, the more mature and truly satisfying your relationships will be. The less needy you feel, the less conflict there will be.
- People who have rediscovered their capacity to create inner satisfaction are far less likely to get trapped in a mindset of scarcity and more likely to use their innate creativity to expand the pie.
- Babies naturally feel connected. This bigger picture is the view we are all born with. As adults, we may catch glimpses of it in moments of deep love, wonder, or beauty. We each have an innate ability to connect with life.
- Once Dr. Taylor found her way to the right brain after her stroke, she was able to find it again and again. The rest of us can learn to engage the right side of the brain more frequently and easily.
- The left brain is essential for navigating the world and protecting us against danger. But we also need the right brain to feel connection and contentment.
- Our longings and worries are both to some degree overblown because we have within us the capacity to manufacture the very commodity we are constantly chasing.
- True enduring satisfaction starts inside. From inner satisfaction comes outer satisfaction, which then feeds back into more inner satisfaction—a virtuous circle.
- Absolute safety comes from a sense of gratitude and wonder at the very existence of the world. Our bodies remain frail, but the feeling is one of absolute safety.
- Life can be extremely challenging at times, but we can choose whether or not to see the challenges as being ultimately in our favor. We can choose to learn from them, even the most difficult ones.
- Being happy comes from feeling grateful for life. The reverse is also true, perhaps even more so.
- We have the power to choose our basic attitude toward life, which directly influences our attitude toward others. Instead of saying no to life, we can choose to say yes to life.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Absolute safety (as described by Wittgenstein) is a state of mind—"I am safe, nothing can injure me whatever happens"—that comes from gratitude and wonder, not from external protection. It addresses one of our deepest needs: the need to feel safe. It is a psychological reframe, not a physical guarantee.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Seeing the universe as fundamentally hostile
  **Solution:** Consciously reframe by choosing to see life as friendly. Actively practice right-brain connection exercises.
- **Problem:** Falling into win-lose thinking in the heat of conflict
  **Solution:** Pause and reframe. Ask: Is this an adversarial contest or an opportunity for collaborative problem-solving? Access your right brain before responding.
- **Problem:** Believing happiness comes from external conditions or other people
  **Solution:** Reclaim your birthright capacity to manufacture your own inner happiness. Discover the simple things that make you happy. Take care of your own deeper needs.
- **Problem:** Assuming there is a fixed pie that cannot be expanded
  **Solution:** Look for ways to expand the pie creatively. First expand your inner pie (your contentment), and the outer pie becomes easier to expand.
- **Problem:** Letting the left-brain "chatter" dominate in difficult situations
  **Solution:** Have a daily practice to engage the right brain. When facing a difficult situation, deliberately access your bigger-picture, connection-oriented perspective.
- **Problem:** Lamenting the unfairness of life instead of practicing gratitude during adversity
  **Solution:** Choose gratitude. Ask what you can learn from the challenge. Find something small to connect with life. Gratitude is a measurable gateway to happiness.
- **Problem:** Waiting for outer satisfaction before seeking inner satisfaction
  **Solution:** Start from within. Build inner satisfaction first, and outer satisfaction that follows will be more enduring and will reinforce inner satisfaction in a virtuous circle.

### Lesson 4: Stay in the Zone — From Resistance to Acceptance

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Venezuela Political Crisis Negotiation

**Use Case:** High-stakes political mediation; sensitive negotiations with hostile parties; any situation where you have limited time with a powerful person.

William Ury was invited by the Carter Center to mediate the 2002 Venezuelan political crisis between President Hugo Chavez and the opposition. Millions of people were on the streets, civil war seemed imminent, and Ury had only minutes with Chavez. Instead of reciting prepared recommendations, he listened attentively, asked Chavez about his daughter, and let him tell his personal story about resigning from the military rather than order troops to kill civilians. When Chavez asked for Ury's thoughts, Ury offered one key recommendation: open dialogue with the opposition. Chavez reacted angrily. Rather than argue, Ury asked a powerful question: "What action if any could they possibly take tomorrow morning that would send you a credible signal that they were ready to change?" Within minutes, Chavez agreed to work with Ury to develop practical trust-building actions. The meeting lasted 2.5 hours instead of a few minutes because Ury stayed present and looked for openings rather than pushing his agenda.

##### Example 2: Mountain Climbing and the Art of Letting Go

**Use Case:** Learning to release control; building psychological safety before taking risks; understanding that letting go requires preparation.

As a young mountain climber, Ury learned to rappel by gradually relaxing his grip on the rope—starting with a few seconds, feeling safe, then loosening more. If he held on too tightly, he would get stuck and unable to descend. In one storm, Ury and his friend Dusty wrapped their rope around a small pine tree as an anchor, began rappelling, and the tree uprooted. They stopped, found a more reliable anchor (a boulder), and rappelled safely. Ury draws the parallel: we need to be anchored to something solid (a sense that life is friendly, inner resources, values) before we can safely let go of our controlling grip on circumstances.

##### Example 3: Craig the Business Client

**Use Case:** Business disputes consumed by revenge; letting go of past grievances in negotiations; recognizing when the fight has become its own reward.

A client named Craig was enmeshed in a bitter business dispute. He admitted: "When I think of what Craig has done to me, I feel furious. So it gives me pleasure to attack him. If I settle our dispute, what will my life be like without my private war?" He was so focused on revenge and the past that he had lost sight of his true negotiation objectives. Once he let go and settled the dispute, he felt "much lighter," and his children noticed—commenting that "Daddy is not on the cell phone all the time."

##### Example 4: Nelson Mandela and Forgiveness

**Use Case:** Deep historical grievances; political reconciliation; post-conflict forgiveness; personal liberation from victimhood.

After 27 years in prison, Mandela chose to let go of hatred toward his former jailers rather than remain their prisoner. He said, "I felt hatred and fear but I said to myself, if you hate them when you get in that car, you will still be their prisoner. I wanted to be free and so I let it go." By forgiving, he freed himself and inspired thousands of South Africans to do the same, including fellow prisoner Vusumzi Mcongo, who had been severely tortured: "We cannot live with broken hearts...To stay with the past will only bring you into turmoil."

##### Example 5: Judith and Her Son Ben

**Use Case:** Strained parent-child relationships; teenage rebellion; letting go of control in family dynamics; paradox of acceptance leading to change.

Judith struggled with her son Ben's intense rebellion from age 9 to 13. She was on a "roller coaster of hurt and anger, helplessness and determination." She asked herself: "What is the worst thing that could happen here?" and realized: "Other than my child dying, the worst thing I could imagine was that I would end up having active relationships with only two of my three children." She realized she could live with that. She gradually let go of needing his acknowledgment, love, or approval. Paradoxically, this acceptance led Ben to eventually come closer to her, apologize, and express his love.

##### Example 6: Gabi's Medical Journey and Reframing

**Use Case:** Reframing trauma or hardship; finding meaning in suffering; parenting through illness; maintaining presence amid crisis.

Ury's daughter Gabi underwent 14 major surgeries. Rather than resent or regret the experience, Ury and his wife Lizanne reframed it as a "blessed shock" that challenged them to grow and draw on their inner resources. Gabi herself, despite her medical challenges, "doesn't lose any time looking back with resentment or regret or feeling sorry for herself. She shakes it off. She has a zest for life." Ury uses her laser-like focus on the present as a reminder to let go himself.

##### Example 7: The Businessman and WATNA

**Use Case:** Pre-negotiation anxiety; catastrophic thinking; using worst-case analysis to gain perspective and reduce fear.

A businessman told Ury: "I also like to think about my WATNA—my Worst Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. Because I worry a lot about what's going to happen if things go wrong...I say to myself: 'If they're not going to kill me, then I will probably survive.' And I laugh it off." This reality-testing question helps him let go of unnecessary fear.

##### Example 8: Roger Fisher's Present-Opportunity Focus

**Use Case:** Mediation methodology; conflict resolution framing; moving from analysis to action.

Ury's mentor Roger Fisher, co-author of Getting to Yes, focused not on understanding the history of a conflict or predicting its future, but on the present opportunity: "Who can do what today to move this conflict toward resolution?" This focus on actionable present steps transformed how Ury approached mediation.

#### Step 1: In Sensitive Situations, Listen First and Look for Openings

When approaching a sensitive negotiation or interaction, resist the urge to immediately give advice or push your agenda. Instead, listen attentively, ask open-ended questions, stay focused on the present moment, and look for openings where the other person signals receptivity. As Ury discovered with Chavez, "if I had followed my first thought to begin the meeting by reciting my recommendations, the president would have cut the meeting short after a few minutes." The power to transform the conflict lies in the present moment—in spotting the opening when it appears.

#### Step 2: Build a Solid Anchor Before Letting Go

Before you can relax your grip on control, make sure you are anchored to something solid. This means developing a sense that the world is friendly, cultivating inner resources (skills, values, self-trust), and reframing your picture of life so that you feel secure enough to let go. Like the mountain climber who finds a reliable boulder before rappelling, you need a trustworthy anchor. "Our ability to relax and let life flow naturally depends on how solidly anchored we feel in a friendly world."

#### Step 3: Accept the Past by Reframing and Forgiving

To release the burden of the past, practice reframing your experiences—give positive meaning to even the most difficult events. We cannot change what happened, but we can change the meaning we assign to it. Practice forgiveness—not condoning what happened, but accepting it and freeing yourself from its weight. The first beneficiary of forgiveness is yourself. Forgiving yourself is equally important: "If we all hold on to the mistake, we can't see our own glory in the mirror because we have the mistake between our faces and the mirror."

#### Step 4: Trust the Future by Testing Your Fears

To release anxiety about the future, practice reality-testing your fears. Ask yourself: "What is the worst thing that can happen here?" Most of the time, you'll find you can live with the worst outcome. Observe your fear when it shows up, take a deep breath, and consciously release it. Remember your "inner BATNA"—your commitment to take care of your needs and your confidence that life is on your side. "The pessimist sees the difficulty in every opportunity. The optimist sees the opportunity in every difficulty."

#### Step 5: Embrace the Present by Releasing Expectations

To stay in the present, let go of "should" and "should not"—these words signal unaccepted expectations about how life ought to be. Accept reality as it is, regardless of how painful. This is not resignation—"constructive change starts from accepting reality regardless of how painful it might be—not from losing time and energy resisting it." Visit the past briefly to learn, visit the future to plan, but make your home in the present. Focus on what lasts (life itself, nature, the universe) while accepting what passes. "Let the passing pass, let the lasting last."

#### Step 6: Practice Gradual Letting Go

Letting go is a skill that develops with practice, like learning to rappel. Start small: relax your grip for a few seconds, feel that you are safe, then loosen a little more, followed by holding on again. Over time, it gets easier until you don't even have to think about it. "Once I did let go of the rope, there was nothing to do except to enjoy the view."

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Listen first; offer advice only when asked
- ✅ Stay focused on the present moment and actively look for openings in conversations
- ✅ Anchor yourself to something solid (inner resources, values, friendly worldview) before letting go of control
- ✅ Reframe difficult past experiences to extract meaning and growth
- ✅ Forgive others and yourself to free yourself from the weight of resentment
- ✅ Replace fear with conscious trust in your ability to handle whatever comes
- ✅ Make your psychological home in the present; visit past and future only briefly
- ✅ Notice "should" and "should not" language in your thinking as signals of unaccepted expectations
- ✅ Ask "What is the worst thing that can happen here?" to test anxious predictions
- ✅ Focus on what lasts (life, nature) while accepting what passes
- ✅ Practice gradual letting go—start small, build comfort, then release further

#### Keep In Mind

- The key to staying in the zone is to say **yes** to life—accept the past, trust the future, and embrace the present as they are
- Being in the zone (or "flow") is a state of relaxed alertness where your best performance and greatest satisfaction occur
- Pain may be inevitable, but suffering is optional—it is our resistance to pain that causes suffering
- "We are destined to lose many things. That is the nature of life. Never mind. Just don't lose the present. Nothing is worth it. There is nothing more important than 'this,' the fullness of life right now."
- Acceptance is the prerequisite for constructive change, not its enemy
- Forgiveness does not mean condoning or forgetting—it means accepting what happened and freeing yourself
- The "present" is literally a gift—accept it as it is, even if it's not the gift you wanted
- Trust is not a one-time shift but a conscious choice made many times a day

#### Security & Safety Notes

- In physically dangerous situations (e.g., rappelling), ensure you have a reliable anchor before letting go—never release control without confirming your safety
- In dangerous conflict zones, fear is a natural response, but catastrophic thinking ("What's the worst that can happen?") can help calibrate real vs. imagined threats
- The chapter references real-world high-risk contexts (civil war mediation, mountain climbing in storms) where staying present has literal life-or-death implications

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Rushing to give advice or push your agenda in a negotiation, causing the other party to disengage or end the interaction prematurely
  **Solution:** Listen first, ask open-ended questions, and look for openings before offering recommendations.
- **Problem:** Being mentally distracted by past regrets or future worries, causing you to miss present opportunities for connection or agreement
  **Solution:** Practice staying in the zone through relaxed alertness and present-moment focus.
- **Problem:** Trying to force control over outcomes or other people's behavior, which creates more suffering and often backfires
  **Solution:** Gradually practice letting go of control, starting with small, low-stakes situations, building up to larger ones.
- **Problem:** Attempting to let go without being psychologically anchored first—feeling ungrounded and unsafe
  **Solution:** Build inner resources, reframe life as friendly, and establish a solid sense of self before releasing control.
- **Problem:** Using "should" and "should not" to resist current reality, prolonging conflict and misery
  **Solution:** Notice the language, accept reality as it is, and then decide what constructive action to take from that place of acceptance.
- **Problem:** Holding onto past resentments, which consumes you more than it harms the other person
  **Solution:** Practice forgiveness—not for their sake, but for yours. Holding a grudge is like carrying your bags while traveling on a train.
- **Problem:** Excessive worry about the future (WATNA thinking) that impairs your ability to perform in the present
  **Solution:** Ask "What is the worst thing that can happen?" to gain perspective, then consciously release the fear.
- **Problem:** Trying to change a situation or relationship before first accepting it as it is
  **Solution:** Accept the present reality first; paradoxically, this acceptance often creates the conditions for healthy change to emerge naturally.
- **Problem:** Forgetting that pain is part of life but suffering is optional—resisting pain amplifies it
  **Solution:** Allow pain to be present without adding the layer of resistance; say "yes" to the experience even when it's difficult.

### Lesson 5: Respect Them Even If — Respect as the Highest-Yielding Concession

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Union-Management Negotiation

**Use Case:** Labor negotiation; tense workplace or organizational talks.

Management's chief negotiator opened a tense labor contract talk with hostility: "We don't trust you and we don't like what you are doing." Union leader Dennis Williams controlled his impulse to counterattack and instead responded calmly and respectfully, reframing the conversation around shared purpose. That single respectful response set the tone for the entire negotiation. It was one of only three times in over sixty years that a contract agreement was reached without a major fight. People from the other side later told him they appreciated the gesture of respect.

##### Example 2: Syrian Rebel Leaders Interviews

**Use Case:** Cross-cultural conflict research; understanding opposing perspectives.

While researching an end to the Syrian civil war, the author interviewed rebel leaders on the Turkish-Syrian border. The leaders—once pediatricians, dentists, lawyers, students—shared stories of torture, loss, and trauma. The interviewers set aside three hours per person so leaders felt truly heard. One ultraconservative Salafi commander of three thousand fighters, who looked like a Western stereotype of a fundamentalist, had won a national poetry prize before being arrested and tortured for a subversive poem. When asked his greatest concern if his side won, he said religious extremism—and added: "Please put yourself here and imagine that your child or wife is one of the numbers. Every single number has a life and a soul." Listening with genuine respect led to better understanding of the conflict and laid groundwork for a Syrian-led solution.

##### Example 3: The Abraham Path in Bethlehem

**Use Case:** Cross-cultural peacebuilding; community trust-building in conflict zones.

Presenting the Abraham Path project to Palestinian leaders in Bethlehem, the author faced two hours of skeptical, suspicious, and hostile questions—including whether the project was linked to foreign intelligence agencies, with gunshots audible in the square outside. Instead of becoming defensive, the author acknowledged their distrust as legitimate, stepped to their side, and told them: "The true leaders can only be the peoples in the region… the leadership role belongs to you." The atmosphere shifted. Critics became optimistic. The project gained local ownership and was later named the world's best new walking trail by National Geographic Traveller.

##### Example 4: Hostage Negotiations

**Use Case:** Crisis negotiation; de-escalation in life-threatening situations.

A generation ago, standard hostage response was a bullhorn ultimatum followed by a SWAT assault—often resulting in death or injury. Today, professional hostage negotiators use a wholly different approach: be polite, give the hostage taker a hearing, listen with close attention, acknowledge their point of view, stay cool and courteous. Treating hostage takers with basic human respect gives them a face-saving way out. In the overwhelming majority of cases, the hostage taker surrenders and hostages go free unharmed.

##### Example 5: Azim Khamisa's Forgiveness

**Use Case:** Transforming grief into healing; breaking cycles of violence.

After his twenty-year-old son Tariq was killed by a fourteen-year-old gang member during a pizza delivery, Azim Khamisa experienced an extraordinary transformation. Rather than pursuing revenge, he put himself in the shoes of his son's killer, forgave him (without condoning the crime), and reached out to the boy's grandfather. The young perpetrator, Tony, broke down in remorse, pleaded guilty, and later accepted a job offer at the foundation Azim and the grandfather founded to stop youth violence. Azim's choice turned a cycle of violence into one of healing and purpose.

##### Example 6: Larry and Jose

**Use Case:** Family reconciliation; resolving unspoken family conflict.

Larry married a Mexican American woman and became the only non-Hispanic in her family, provoking unspoken resentment from his brother-in-law Jose. Ten years later, Jose apologized: he had lobbied behind the scenes to break them up and had felt bad about it for years. Jose changed his attitude from rejection to respect, finally accepting Larry into the family and resolving years of felt but unspoken conflict.

##### Example 7: Barry-Wehmiller Factory

**Use Case:** Organizational culture transformation; leadership and employee relations.

A factory under new ownership shifted from treating employees as paid automatons to genuinely listening to them and welcoming their ideas. During the 2008 financial crisis, when competitors were laying off workers, CEO Robert Chapman asked everyone—top to bottom—to take a six-week unpaid leave so no one would be laid off. Employees and management credited the turnaround to this attitude of respect and inclusion.

##### Example 8: Abraham Lincoln

**Use Case:** Political reconciliation; expanding one's circle of respect even to adversaries.

When a Yankee patriot chastised Lincoln for speaking sympathetically of the defeated South during the Civil War, Lincoln responded: "Madam, do I not destroy my enemies when I turn them into my friends?" Lincoln expanded his circle of respect to encompass even those who had taken up arms against the Union.

##### Example 9: Meditation and Compassion Research

**Use Case:** Scientific evidence for respect as a cultivable trait.

A study by Professor David DeSteno found that 50 percent of people who practiced meditation for eight weeks gave up their seat to a person in visible pain, compared to only 16 percent of non-meditators—a threefold difference. Meditation enhances attention to others and fosters the view that all beings are connected, dissolving artificial social distinctions and increasing the capacity for elementary respect.

#### Step 1: Put Yourself in Their Shoes

Imagine the world through the other person's eyes. Ask: What does this feel like to be that person? If I had lived his or her life, how would I act and react? Listen not just to their words but to the feelings and unspoken thoughts behind them. Listen from within their frame of reference, not just your own. You may not be wholly accurate, but shared humanity makes your understanding far more accurate than you might expect.

#### Step 2: Expand Your Circle of Respect

Consciously choose to include people whom at first you might not want or think to include. Recognize the wound of exclusion and respond with the remedy of inclusion. Treat each person as a human being with dignity, talent, and a story worth hearing. Make real efforts to welcome their ideas, listen to their concerns, and signal that they matter.

#### Step 3: Respect Even Those Who Reject You

When someone rejects, attacks, or dismisses you, your instinct is to pull back and reject them in return. Instead, do the opposite: respond with respect. This is a form of psychological jujitsu. Surprise them. Take the lead. Meet exclusion with inclusion. Meeting rejection with respect gives the other person a face-saving way out and breaks the cycle of mutual antagonism.

#### Step 4: Go to the Balcony Before Reacting

When you feel rejected or attacked, step back—go to the balcony—to observe your feelings and thoughts rather than being swept away by them. Remind yourself of your inner BATNA: your commitment to take care of your deepest needs. When you feel confident in your own ability to make yourself happy, you become less reactive to another person's offensive behavior. Having given yourself respect, you can more easily give others respect.

#### Step 5: Acknowledge Their Humanity Before Addressing Their Demands

Respect others' basic humanity without approving of their behavior or liking them as individuals. You can say no to their demands while saying yes to their dignity. Even when drawing clear limits to protect yourself or others, treat the other person as a human being just like yourself.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Give respect as the first move; it costs the least and yields the most
- ✅ Listen with full positive attention, from the other person's frame of reference
- ✅ Respond to rejection with respect rather than retaliation
- ✅ Include rather than exclude; expand your circle of respect intentionally
- ✅ Go to the balcony when under attack—observe your reactions before acting
- ✅ Put yourself in the other person's shoes to understand their underlying humanity
- ✅ Separate the person's dignity from their demands or actions
- ✅ Practice self-awareness (e.g., meditation) to strengthen your capacity for respect
- ✅ Take responsibility for your own happiness so you are less reactive to others
- ✅ Step to the other side and invite them to take leadership when appropriate

#### Keep In Mind

- An attitude of respect cannot be forced; it can only be nurtured. It emerges organically when you have already given yourself respect through inner work.
- When we feel rejected, we naturally reject back; when we feel excluded, we naturally exclude back. Breaking this pattern requires conscious choice.
- If we have chosen to take responsibility for our lives and actions, we are not likely to blame others.
- By paying attention inside yourself, you become better able to pay attention outside yourself.
- One person changing from antagonism to respect is often enough to change the tone and outcome of an entire interaction.
- It may not be easy, especially when under attack, but with practice and courage you can often turn mutual rejection into mutual respect.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Respecting someone's humanity does not mean ignoring injustice, evil, or wrongful actions. You can and should confront harmful behavior while still treating the person with dignity.
- Accepting those who reject you does not mean saying yes to their demands. Set clear limits for the sake of protecting yourself and others.
- Even in extreme situations like hostage taking or violent crime, the principle of respecting the person's humanity applies—while still taking whatever protective action is necessary.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Believing the other person must earn your respect before you give it
  **Solution:** Respect is a choice, not a reward. You can give basic human respect without approving of specific behavior.
- **Problem:** Reacting impulsively when rejected or attacked, perpetuating a destructive cycle
  **Solution:** Go to the balcony first. Observe your feelings before acting.
- **Problem:** Listening only from your own frame of reference, judging the other person's words by your standards
  **Solution:** Practice listening from within the other person's frame of reference, hearing the feelings behind the words.
- **Problem:** Thinking respect is incompatible with setting limits or saying no
  **Solution:** You can say no to demands while saying yes to dignity. The hostage negotiator says no to the hostage taker's demands while respecting the person.
- **Problem:** Trying to address every skeptical or hostile comment judiciously, appearing defensive and increasing suspicion
  **Solution:** Acknowledge concerns, step to the other side, and invite them to take ownership or leadership.

### Lesson 6: Give and Receive — From Win-Lose to Win-Win-Win

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Derek Sorenson Sports Negotiation

**Use Case:** Negotiation in professional sports; building long-term relationships through fair dealing.

Derek Sorenson, a professional negotiator for a sports team, initially took a win-lose approach with a player's agent, making a low offer and refusing to budge. The agent gave in, but Sorenson felt uneasy about the agent's frustration and the damage to the relationship. He returned to the agent and met the original request, giving thousands of additional dollars. The agent was extremely appreciative and later gave Sorenson a call when a player came up for free agency. Sorenson's willingness to give for mutual gain built long-term goodwill that benefited his organization.

##### Example 2: The North Wind and the Sun (Aesop's Fable)

**Use Case:** The power of giving versus taking in persuasion.

The North Wind tried to rip a shepherd boy's cloak off by force, but the harder he blew, the more tightly the boy held on. The Sun simply shone warmly, and the boy voluntarily took off his cloak. The Sun's giving approach proved more powerful and satisfying than the North Wind's taking approach. The fable illustrates that natural giving (like the Sun shining) wins cooperation, whereas forceful taking breeds resistance.

##### Example 3: Haley, the Five-Year-Old with Leukemia

**Use Case:** Finding meaning and joy through giving even in extreme hardship.

Haley, gravely ill with leukemia at Children's Hospital, gave a large stuffed letter "G" to Gabi (the author's daughter). Despite her extreme discomfort and proximity to death, Haley experienced the joy of making another child smile. Her story demonstrates that even in terrible circumstances, giving for giving's sake can bring profound satisfaction.

##### Example 4: Scott Harrison and charity: water

**Use Case:** Purpose-driven giving; authentic service to others.

Scott Harrison grew up with forced altruism, wore a mask of altruism for approval, then rebelled into a selfish life promoting nightclubs. After a crisis in Punta del Este where he realized he was "emotionally, spiritually, and morally bankrupt," he spent time alone questioning his life, asked "What would the opposite of my life look like? What if I actually served others?" He volunteered on a medical ship in West Africa, then founded charity: water, which has built wells and provided clean water to hundreds of thousands. His journey shows that authentic giving—chosen freely, not out of obligation—can bring enduring inner satisfaction.

##### Example 5: Gabi Ury's Guinness World Record

**Use Case:** Purpose extending from personal goal to service to others.

Gabi Ury, born with a medical condition requiring fourteen major surgeries, decided to break the Guinness World Record for longest-held abdominal plank for her sixteenth birthday. What started as a personal goal extended to a purpose: raising money and awareness for Children's Hospital Colorado. She doubled the existing world record (1 hour, 20 minutes) and raised over $58,000 for the hospital, more than eleven times her target. Her story illustrates how a personal purpose can naturally extend from giving to yourself to giving to others.

##### Example 6: Abilio Diniz Business Dispute

**Use Case:** Transforming win-lose business conflict into win-win-win resolution.

Abilio Diniz and his former business partner spent two and a half years in a bitter win-lose fight—suing each other, attacking each other in the press, blocking each other's initiatives. Neither succeeded in getting what he wanted. By shifting the negotiation dynamic to focus on what each side could give the other (rather than what they could take), the author identified two common interests: freedom and dignity. Abilio's partner could release him from a noncompete clause; Abilio could leave the board and exchange voting shares for nonvoting shares. In four intense days, they reached a genuine win-win-win solution that satisfied both parties, benefited their families and 150,000 employees, and restored Abilio's sense of having "got my life back."

#### Step 1: Examine and Shift Your Own Attitude

The foundational step is to examine your own attitude toward others and consciously shift it from taking (claiming value only for yourself) to giving (creating value for others as well). This inner work is essential because giving is a behavior that originates inside as a basic attitude. In the heat of conflict, fear of scarcity often wins out, but the opportunity to change the game to win-win-win lies in your hands. You must be willing to lead the way by examining and shifting your own attitude rather than waiting for the other side to change first.

#### Step 2: Root Giving in Self-Interest (Give for Mutual Gain)

To cultivate an attitude of giving, root it in your self-interest by understanding how creating value for others helps you tangibly meet your needs. Giving does not mean sacrificing your interests or giving in to the other person's demands. It simply means looking for mutual gain—helping others at the same time as you help yourself. The most successful negotiators focus on addressing the interests and needs of their counterparts while looking after their own needs, finding ways to expand the pie for both sides. Research by Carsten De Dreu analyzing twenty-eight negotiation simulation studies confirms that cooperative approaches focusing on meeting both parties' needs produce the best outcomes. Li Ka-Shing's business success came from treating partners fairly and giving them a little more than he took, which made everyone want to partner with him.

#### Step 3: Focus Negotiations on What You Can Give, Not Just Take

In any conflict or negotiation, consciously choose to focus discussions on what each side has the power to give the other, rather than presenting threats or demands. When Abilio Diniz and his partner shifted from threats to discussing what they could give each other—freedom and dignity—the dynamic changed completely. Identify the underlying interests behind positions and propose agreements founded on common interests. For example, instead of fighting over a position, ask: "What can I offer that the other side values, and what do they have that I need?"

#### Step 4: Discover the Joy of Giving Without Expecting Return

Move from giving in order to receive (transactional) to giving without expecting a direct tangible return. Give simply for the pleasure of giving, like the Sun shining naturally. Discover the sheer joy that can come from giving naturally without thinking about receiving a return. Paradoxically, giving simply for the pleasure of giving can bring the most satisfaction in the end. Sherryann, a manager who mentors junior colleagues and spearheads initiatives without quid pro quo, exemplifies this stage: "I'm not looking for quid pro quo; I'm looking to make a difference and have an impact."

#### Step 5: Find Your Purpose and Give What You Are Here to Give

The most enduring way to strengthen your attitude of giving is to discover a purpose or activity that makes you a natural giver. A purpose answers: Why do we get up in the morning? What makes us excited? What inspires us? Your purpose might be raising a family, creating art, serving customers, mentoring colleagues, or helping those who are suffering. Like a muscle, the attitude of giving benefits from exercise through a purpose. When giving becomes engrained in the fabric of your life through purpose, it becomes a sustained source of satisfaction. Gabi Ury's plank project illustrates this: it started as a personal goal but became a purpose-driven mission to help Children's Hospital. Paola left her law career to make natural soaps, discovering her childhood love of mixing potions. The apparent magnitude of the gift does not matter; what counts is giving in an openhearted way.

#### Step 6: Overcome the Jonah Complex (Fear of Your Own Greatness)

Perhaps the greatest obstacle to giving your gifts is the fear not of your limitations but of your talents. Abraham Maslow called this the "Jonah complex"—the fear that prevents us from exercising our talents and fulfilling our destiny. Like Jonah, who ran away from his call to warn Nineveh, we often hide our light under a bushel when faced with the opportunity to give our gifts. Only when we face a significant challenge or setback do we wake up and realize we can only fulfill our purpose if we give what we are here to give. Like Abraham, who immediately heeded his call to hospitality, we must listen to our own call, clean the window that looks out, and let our light shine for others.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Focus negotiations on what each side can give the other, not just what they can take
- ✅ Look for common interests (freedom, dignity, respect) behind conflicting positions
- ✅ Give for mutual gain: help others at the same time as you help yourself
- ✅ Give without expecting a direct tangible return—give simply for the pleasure of giving
- ✅ Root your giving in a purpose that makes you come alive
- ✅ Go to the balcony: step back from the heat of argument to see the bigger picture
- ✅ Put yourself in your own shoes to uncover your true needs
- ✅ Show respect to adversaries as part of the giving attitude
- ✅ Take care of your own needs first so you can give without burning out
- ✅ Treat giving as a muscle that benefits from regular exercise through purpose

#### Keep In Mind

- All prior steps of getting to yes with yourself (addressing deepest needs, satisfying from within, showing respect) lead up to this point of giving
- Giving works in part because it increases the probability that someone else will do something good for you
- The benefits of giving go well beyond material self-interest
- There are limits we need to respect, even to giving for pleasure and meaning—taking care of your needs prevents feeling used and burned out
- Giving simply for the pleasure of giving can bring the most satisfaction
- Paradoxically, it is by giving that we often receive what we most want
- Each of the six steps in the book helps transform the win-lose mindset into a win-win-win mindset
- When we change our default mode to giving, we get to yes with ourselves (inner satisfaction) and find it easier to get to yes with others (outer success), beginning a circle of giving and receiving with no end

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Take care of your own needs first so you can give without burning out or feeling used. Sustainable giving requires that you are not depleting yourself.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Fear of scarcity—afraid that cooperating means there will not be enough to meet your needs or the other side will take advantage of you
  **Solution:** Root giving in self-interest by appreciating how creating value for others tangibly helps you meet your needs. See giving as mutual gain, not sacrifice.
- **Problem:** Giving out of obligation—wearing a mask of altruism to gain approval, leading to unhappiness and eventual rebellion against giving
  **Solution:** Give from genuine choice, not obligation. Discover your authentic purpose and give because it is who you are and what you like to do, not to please others.
- **Problem:** Win-lose mindset—focusing only on claiming value for yourself, leading to adversarial conflicts where everyone loses
  **Solution:** Examine and shift your attitude from taking to giving. Focus on what you can give the other side and look for mutual gain.
- **Problem:** Burnout from over-giving—giving without taking care of your own needs, leading to feeling used and exhausted
  **Solution:** Respect limits. Make sure you take care of your needs too. Giving for pleasure and meaning requires sustainability.
- **Problem:** Focusing on positions instead of interests—fighting over demands rather than underlying needs
  **Solution:** Identify common interests behind conflicting positions, such as freedom and dignity. Focus negotiations on these shared needs.
- **Problem:** Avoidance—not talking about conflicts, which makes them worse over time
  **Solution:** Engage with the conflict and look for win-win solutions through giving, rather than avoiding the issue.
- **Problem:** Soft accommodating approach—giving everything away to please others, which may leave you unable to serve anyone in the long run
  **Solution:** Adopt a win-win approach that shows concern for both your interests and the other side's interests, not just one or the other.

### Lesson 7: Conclusion — The Three Wins and the Six Inner Yes Steps

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Morning Mirror Preparation

**Use Case:** Daily personal ritual for self-preparation.

Before facing daily challenges, the author looks in the mirror and reminds himself that the biggest opponent he will face that day is himself. He reviews the six inner yes steps mentally, asking himself guiding questions for each. This prepares him to get out of his own way.

##### Example 2: Nelson Mandela's Transformation

**Use Case:** Overcoming extreme adversity and injustice through inner work.

While imprisoned for 27 years, Mandela applied the inner yes principles: he went to the "balcony" to observe himself, learned to know himself deeply, refused to play the blame game, took full responsibility, reframed his perspective to see life on his side, let go of resentments, and forgave his enemies. Upon release, he welcomed all races and led South Africa to a win-win-win democratic agreement ending apartheid.

##### Example 3: Preventing Conflicts Before They Start

**Use Case:** Relationship and workplace harmony.

By staying calm and grounded through inner yes practice, you avoid being provoked, avoid taking attacks personally, and prevent saying or doing things you will later regret. With sincere respect and genuine willingness to address others' needs, you resolve matters before they escalate into serious disputes.

##### Example 4: Author's Professional Journey

**Use Case:** Large-scale peacemaking.

Ury spent 35 years working to prevent wars, initially believing practical negotiation strategies were the key. He realized that sustained world peace requires inner peace first—the difficulty in global cooperation stems from our inability to get to yes with ourselves.

#### Step 1: Put Yourself in Your Shoes

**Action:** Notice the inner critic and observe your thoughts and feelings without judgment.

Each morning, ask yourself: Can you notice the inner critic at work and simply observe your thoughts and feelings without judging? What underlying needs do your feelings point to? What do you really need? This step helps you separate your observing self from your reactive emotions.

#### Step 2: Develop Your Inner BATNA

**Action:** Stop blaming others and commit to taking care of your deepest needs no matter what.

Ask: Are you blaming anyone or anything for your needs not being met? What benefit does this blame provide you—and what are the costs? Can you commit to take care of your deepest needs no matter what? Your inner BATNA is knowing you can meet your own core needs independently.

#### Step 3: Reframe Your Picture

**Action:** See life as on your side rather than against you.

Ask: Do you feel like life is in some way against you? How can you make your own happiness today? If life is challenging, can you nonetheless choose to say yes to it, just the way it is? Reframe challenges as opportunities and choose to embrace life rather than resist it.

#### Step 4: Stay in the Zone

**Action:** Release past resentments and future anxieties to remain present and effective.

Ask: Are you carrying any resentments about the past or anxieties about the future? What will it take to let go and accept life as it is today? What is one small step you can take to stay in the zone, where you are at your best? The zone is the state of flow and optimal performance.

#### Step 5: Respect Them Even If

**Action:** Extend genuine respect to others regardless of their behavior toward you.

Ask: Are you feeling any antagonism toward anyone? What is it like to be in their shoes? Even if they are not showing you respect, can you still respect them? This breaks the cycle of mutual disrespect and opens the door to cooperation.

#### Step 6: Give and Receive

**Action:** Shift the game from taking to giving and from win-lose to win-win-win.

Ask: Do you feel a fear of scarcity in any situation you are currently facing? What will it take for you to change the game from taking to giving? This step transforms the fundamental dynamic from competition to collaboration.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Practice the six steps daily, especially in the morning, to build mental muscles
- ✅ Adapt the method to your own needs and preferences (solitary walk, coffee with a friend, etc.)
- ✅ Prepare before difficult conversations by running through the six steps in advance
- ✅ Exercise all six steps together rather than relying on just one
- ✅ View the journey as lifelong, not a single trip
- ✅ When on the fly in conflict, rely on steady practice to stay on the balcony
- ✅ Treat each step like a muscle—the more you exercise it, the stronger it gets
- ✅ Make the method your own so it works best for you

#### Keep In Mind

- Understanding the six steps helps a lot, but in the end, no one can do the work but you
- As with any sport, you may never be perfect, but you will gradually get better with practice
- The work of arriving at an inner yes is some of the hardest work anyone can do, all the more so because it is invisible
- There is no bigger yes than the yes inside, no bigger win than the win within
- An inner yes brings a growing sense of calm, contentment, satisfaction, and sufficiency
- The key work in negotiation starts from within
- Getting to yes with yourself not only helps resolve conflicts but actually prevents them from arising in the first place

#### Security & Safety Notes

- The book does not explicitly mention security or safety considerations in the conclusion chapter.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Expecting perfection in the practice of inner yes
  **Solution:** Accept that the journey is lifelong and gradual improvement is the goal, not perfection.
- **Problem:** Only practicing one or two steps of the six-step method
  **Solution:** Exercise all six steps together, as each step makes the next easier and they collectively enable full transformation.
- **Problem:** Reacting impulsively in the middle of a difficult conversation without preparation
  **Solution:** Run through the six steps in advance before any problematic conversation to bring your best ally instead of your worst opponent.
- **Problem:** Blaming others for unmet needs, which creates dependency and resentment
  **Solution:** Recognize the costs of blame and commit to taking care of your deepest needs yourself by developing your inner BATNA.
- **Problem:** Treating life as an adversary and resisting challenges
  **Solution:** Reframe your picture to see life as on your side, and choose to say yes to life even when it is challenging.
- **Problem:** Carrying past resentments and future anxieties that keep you from the zone
  **Solution:** Practice letting go and accepting life as it is today, taking one small step at a time to stay grounded.
