# Getting to Yes with Yourself - Chapter 6 Analysis
## Give and Receive: From Win-Lose to Win-Win-Win

## Overview

Chapter 6 teaches readers how to transform their basic attitude from "taking" (claiming value only for oneself) to "giving" (creating value for others as well as oneself), enabling win-win-win outcomes that benefit the individual, the other party, and the larger whole. This shift is presented as the crowning move of the entire book's methodology, building on all prior steps of getting to yes with yourself. The chapter combines research evidence, classical stories, and real-world case examples to demonstrate that giving—when rooted in self-interest, pleasure, and purpose—leads to both inner satisfaction and outer success.

## When to Follow Chapter Teachings

- When negotiating or mediating a conflict and need to move beyond adversarial win-lose dynamics
- When facing a relationship where both parties seem to be losing
- When feeling stuck in a cycle of claiming value only for yourself
- When seeking to create agreements that benefit not just you and the other party, but the broader community or organization
- When experiencing fear of scarcity that prevents cooperation
- When looking for deeper meaning and satisfaction in work or personal relationships
- When feeling obligated to give but experiencing burnout or resentment
- When fearing your own talents or greatness and avoiding your true purpose

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: The Power of Giving - From Win-Lose to Win-Win-Win

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Derek Sorenson Sports Negotiation

Derek Sorenson, a professional negotiator for a sports team, initially took a win-lose approach with a player's agent, making a low offer and refusing to budge. The agent gave in, but Sorenson felt uneasy about the agent's frustration and the damage to the relationship. He returned to the agent and met the original request, giving thousands of additional dollars. The agent was extremely appreciative and later gave Sorenson a call when a player came up for free agency. Sorenson's willingness to give for mutual gain built long-term goodwill that benefited his organization (p. 141-165).

##### Example 2: The North Wind and the Sun (Aesop's Fable)

The North Wind tried to rip a shepherd boy's cloak off by force, but the harder he blew, the more tightly the boy held on. The Sun simply shone warmly, and the boy voluntarily took off his cloak. The Sun's giving approach proved more powerful and satisfying than the North Wind's taking approach. The fable illustrates that natural giving (like the Sun shining) wins cooperation, whereas forceful taking breeds resistance (p. 169-198).

##### Example 3: Haley, the Five-Year-Old with Leukemia

Haley, gravely ill with leukemia at Children's Hospital, gave a large stuffed letter "G" to Gabi (the author's daughter). Despite her extreme discomfort and proximity to death, Haley experienced the joy of making another child smile. Her story demonstrates that even in terrible circumstances, giving for giving's sake can bring profound satisfaction (p. 206-219).

##### Example 4: Scott Harrison and charity: water

Scott Harrison grew up with forced altruism, wore a mask of altruism for approval, then rebelled into a selfish life promoting nightclubs. After a crisis in Punta del Este where he realized he was "emotionally, spiritually, and morally bankrupt," he spent time alone questioning his life, asked "What would the opposite of my life look like? What if I actually served others?" He volunteered on a medical ship in West Africa, then founded charity: water, which has built wells and provided clean water to hundreds of thousands. His journey shows that authentic giving—chosen freely, not out of obligation—can bring enduring inner satisfaction (p. 239-274).

##### Example 5: Gabi Ury's Guinness World Record

Gabi Ury, born with a medical condition requiring fourteen major surgeries, decided to break the Guinness World Record for longest-held abdominal plank for her sixteenth birthday. What started as a personal goal extended to a purpose: raising money and awareness for Children's Hospital Colorado. She doubled the existing world record (1 hour, 20 minutes) and raised over $58,000 for the hospital, more than eleven times her target. Her story illustrates how a personal purpose can naturally extend from giving to yourself to giving to others (p. 309-395).

##### Example 6: Abilio Diniz Business Dispute

Abilio Diniz and his former business partner spent two and a half years in a bitter win-lose fight—suing each other, attacking each other in the press, blocking each other's initiatives. Neither succeeded in getting what he wanted. By shifting the negotiation dynamic to focus on what each side could give the other (rather than what they could take), the author identified two common interests: freedom and dignity. Abilio's partner could release him from a noncompete clause; Abilio could leave the board and exchange voting shares for nonvoting shares. In four intense days, they reached a genuine win-win-win solution that satisfied both parties, benefited their families and 150,000 employees, and restored Abilio's sense of having "got my life back" (p. 462-534).

#### Step 1: Examine and Shift Your Own Attitude

The foundational step is to examine your own attitude toward others and consciously shift it from taking (claiming value only for yourself) to giving (creating value for others as well). This inner work is essential because giving is a behavior that originates inside as a basic attitude. In the heat of conflict, fear of scarcity often wins out, but the opportunity to change the game to win-win-win lies in your hands. You must be willing to lead the way by examining and shifting your own attitude rather than waiting for the other side to change first (p. 50-51).

#### Step 2: Root Giving in Self-Interest (Give for Mutual Gain)

To cultivate an attitude of giving, root it in your self-interest by understanding how creating value for others helps you tangibly meet your needs. Giving does not mean sacrificing your interests or giving in to the other person's demands. It simply means looking for mutual gain—helping others at the same time as you help yourself. The most successful negotiators focus on addressing the interests and needs of their counterparts while looking after their own needs, finding ways to expand the pie for both sides. Research by Carsten De Dreu analyzing twenty-eight negotiation simulation studies confirms that cooperative approaches focusing on meeting both parties' needs produce the best outcomes. Li Ka-Shing's business success came from treating partners fairly and giving them a little more than he took, which made everyone want to partner with him (p. 83-101, 109-116).

#### Step 3: Focus Negotiations on What You Can Give, Not Just Take

In any conflict or negotiation, consciously choose to focus discussions on what each side has the power to give the other, rather than presenting threats or demands. When Abilio Diniz and his partner shifted from threats to discussing what they could give each other—freedom and dignity—the dynamic changed completely. Identify the underlying interests behind positions and propose agreements founded on common interests. For example, instead of fighting over a position, ask: "What can I offer that the other side values, and what do they have that I need?" (p. 473-480).

#### Step 4: Discover the Joy of Giving Without Expecting Return

Move from giving in order to receive (transactional) to giving without expecting a direct tangible return. Give simply for the pleasure of giving, like the Sun shining naturally. Discover the sheer joy that can come from giving naturally without thinking about receiving a return. Paradoxically, giving simply for the pleasure of giving can bring the most satisfaction in the end. Sherryann, a manager who mentors junior colleagues and spearheads initiatives without quid pro quo, exemplifies this stage: "I'm not looking for quid pro quo; I'm looking to make a difference and have an impact" (p. 222-231).

#### Step 5: Find Your Purpose and Give What You Are Here to Give

The most enduring way to strengthen your attitude of giving is to discover a purpose or activity that makes you a natural giver. A purpose answers: Why do we get up in the morning? What makes us excited? What inspires us? Your purpose might be raising a family, creating art, serving customers, mentoring colleagues, or helping those who are suffering. Like a muscle, the attitude of giving benefits from exercise through a purpose. When giving becomes engrained in the fabric of your life through purpose, it becomes a sustained source of satisfaction. Gabi Ury's plank project illustrates this: it started as a personal goal but became a purpose-driven mission to help Children's Hospital. Paola left her law career to make natural soaps, discovering her childhood love of mixing potions. The apparent magnitude of the gift does not matter; what counts is giving in an openhearted way (p. 293-310, 400-416).

#### Step 6: Overcome the Jonah Complex (Fear of Your Own Greatness)

Perhaps the greatest obstacle to giving your gifts is the fear not of your limitations but of your talents. Abraham Maslow called this the "Jonah complex"—the fear that prevents us from exercising our talents and fulfilling our destiny. Like Jonah, who ran away from his call to warn Nineveh, we often hide our light under a bushel when faced with the opportunity to give our gifts. Only when we face adversity do we wake up and realize we can only fulfill our purpose if we give what we are here to give. Like Abraham, who immediately heeded his call to hospitality, we must listen to our own call, clean the window that looks out, and let our light shine for others (p. 418-460).

#### Best Practices

- Focus negotiations on what each side can give the other, not just what they can take (p. 473-476)
- Look for common interests (freedom, dignity, respect) behind conflicting positions (p. 477-480)
- Give for mutual gain: help others at the same time as you help yourself (p. 100-101)
- Give without expecting a direct tangible return—give simply for the pleasure of giving (p. 222-224)
- Root your giving in a purpose that makes you come alive (p. 293-307)
- Go to the balcony: step back from the heat of argument to see the bigger picture (p. 516-519)
- Put yourself in your own shoes to uncover your true needs (p. 519-520)
- Show respect to adversaries as part of the giving attitude (p. 526-527)
- Take care of your own needs first so you can give without burning out (p. 234-236)
- Treat giving as a muscle that benefits from regular exercise through purpose (p. 294-297)

#### Keep In Mind

- All prior steps of getting to yes with yourself (addressing deepest needs, satisfying from within, showing respect) lead up to this point of giving (p. 74-81)
- Giving works in part because it increases the probability that someone else will do something good for you (p. 69-71)
- The benefits of giving go well beyond material self-interest (p. 166-168)
- There are limits we need to respect, even to giving for pleasure and meaning—taking care of your needs prevents feeling used and burned out (p. 234-236)
- Giving simply for the pleasure of giving can bring the most satisfaction (p. 200-205)
- Paradoxically, it is by giving that we often receive what we most want (p. 285-289)
- Each of the six steps in the book helps transform the win-lose mindset into a win-win-win mindset (p. 535-542)

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Fear of scarcity—afraid that cooperating means there will not be enough to meet your needs or the other side will take advantage of you (p. 43-46)
  **Solution:** Root giving in self-interest by appreciating how creating value for others tangibly helps you meet your needs. See giving as mutual gain, not sacrifice (p. 83-85).

- **Problem:** Giving out of obligation—wearing a mask of altruism to gain approval, leading to unhappiness and eventual rebellion against giving (p. 237-243)
  **Solution:** Give from genuine choice, not obligation. Discover your authentic purpose and give because it is who you are and what you like to do, not to please others (p. 258-260).

- **Problem:** Win-lose mindset—focusing only on claiming value for yourself, leading to adversarial conflicts where everyone loses (p. 22-27, 118-120)
  **Solution:** Examine and shift your attitude from taking to giving. Focus on what you can give the other side and look for mutual gain (p. 50-51, 100-101).

- **Problem:** Burnout from over-giving—giving without taking care of your own needs, leading to feeling used and exhausted (p. 234-236)
  **Solution:** Respect limits. Make sure you take care of your needs too. Giving for pleasure and meaning requires sustainability (p. 234-236).

- **Problem:** Focusing on positions instead of interests—fighting over demands rather than underlying needs (p. 477)
  **Solution:** Identify common interests behind conflicting positions, such as freedom and dignity. Focus negotiations on these shared needs (p. 477-480).

- **Problem:** Avoidance—not talking about conflicts, which makes them worse over time (p. 137-138)
  **Solution:** Engage with the conflict and look for win-win solutions through giving, rather than avoiding the issue (p. 137-140).

- **Problem:** Soft accommodating approach—giving everything away to please others, which may leave you unable to serve anyone in the long run (p. 132-133)
  **Solution:** Adopt a win-win approach that shows concern for both your interests and the other side's interests, not just one or the other (p. 123-127).
