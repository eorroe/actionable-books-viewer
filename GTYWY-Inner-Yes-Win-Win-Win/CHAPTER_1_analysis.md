# Getting to Yes with Yourself - Chapter 1 Analysis

## Chapter: Put Yourself in Your Shoes
**Subtitle:** From Self-Judgment to Self-Understanding

## Overview

Chapter 1 introduces the foundational concept of the "inner yes" - the idea that before we can effectively negotiate with others, we must first get to "yes" with ourselves. The chapter teaches that our greatest opponent in any conflict is often ourselves, and that the path to external resolution begins with internal clarity. Through self-observation, self-empathy, and uncovering our deepest needs, we transform from our own worst opponents into our own allies.

## When to Apply These Teachings

- When preparing for a difficult conversation or negotiation
- When feeling triggered or reactive in conflict situations
- When you don't know what you really want
- When you're stuck in the "3A trap" (attack, accommodate, avoid)
- When you need to understand your own motivations before understanding others
- When self-judgment is blocking your ability to act

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Go to the Balcony - Self-Observation

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Abilio Diniz Boardroom Battle
**Use Case:** High-stakes business dispute
Brazilian businessman Abilio Diniz was trapped in a bitter two-and-a-half-year dispute with his French business partner over control of Brazil's leading supermarket retailer. Despite being chair and major shareholder, he felt angry, frustrated, and disrespected. He didn't know whether to fight or settle and often reacted out of anger contrary to his interests. He was his own worthiest opponent. By first uncovering his deepest need—freedom—he was able to resolve the dispute in just four days of negotiation.

##### Example 2: Political Mediation with Shouting President
**Use Case:** High-pressure diplomatic situation
During a tense political mediation, a country's president shouted angrily at Ury for almost thirty minutes. Ury used the balcony technique: silently noting his sensations, emotions, and thoughts ("Isn't it interesting? My jaw feels clenched. I notice some fear showing up. My cheeks feel flushed. Am I feeling embarrassed?"). This self-observation neutralized the emotional effect and allowed him to recover the conversation.

##### Example 3: Charlotte and Her Four-Year-Old Son
**Use Case:** Domestic/parenting conflict
A mother named Charlotte struggled with her son's refusal to go to bed, which triggered powerful angry reactions. By investigating the feeling of anger with curiosity, she learned to spot the "crossroads" between explosive anger and calm resolution. She realized that if she reacted with anger, her son would distance himself, but if she remained calm, she could advance her core interest in a close relationship.

##### Example 4: The Kitchen Table Exercise
**Use Case:** Daily self-awareness practice
Ury developed a morning visualization exercise where he imagines sitting at a kitchen table. As each thought or emotion shows up (anxiety, fear, shame, pride), he offers it an imaginary seat. He welcomes all customers, including the inner judge, treating them as old friends. This keeps him aware of his regular emotional patterns so they don't catch him by surprise.

##### Example 5: Jamil Mahuad's Peace Negotiation
**Use Case:** International conflict resolution
Former Ecuadorian president Jamil Mahuad learned to deal with painful feelings by putting them in the spotlight. Having been taught that sadness was not well-received by males in his family, he had difficulty connecting with grief. By recognizing and bringing his shadow emotions to light, he was able to control his anger and operate from a balcony perspective during a difficult peace negotiation with Peru, ending the longest-running war in the hemisphere.

##### Example 6: The Holy Grail Knight
**Use Case:** Metaphor for asking the right question
A young knight searching for the Holy Grail failed on his first attempt because he was tongue-tied. Decades later, the older, wiser knight asked the injured king a simple but powerful question: "What ails thee?" By listening to the king's woes and uncovering his deepest needs, a human connection grew, and the king gave him the Grail.

#### Step 1: Observe Yourself from the Balcony

The balcony is a metaphor for a mental and emotional place of perspective, calm, and self-control. Before, during, and after any problematic conversation or negotiation, step back mentally and observe the situation as if watching a play from the balcony. Notice your sensations, emotions, and thoughts without getting caught up in them. When triggered, you have a simple choice: to identify (observe) the thought or to get identified (caught up) with it.

#### Step 2: Name Your Emotions and Thoughts

Naming helps you identify so that you don't get identified. As you observe passing thoughts, emotions, and sensations, name them: "Oh, that is my old friend Fear; there goes the Inner Critic." This neutralizes their effect and helps you maintain balance and calm. You can even give them humorous names like "Freddy Fear," "Judge Judy," or "Anger Annie." As soon as you name the character in the play, you distance yourself from him or her.

#### Step 3: Avoid the 3A Trap

When we react, we typically fall into the "3A trap": we attack, we accommodate (give in), or we avoid altogether. These common reactions serve neither our true interests nor the other person's. Once the fight-or-flight reaction gets triggered, blood flows from our brain to our limbs, and our ability to think clearly diminishes. When we react, we give away our power. The alternative is to observe ourselves and choose a considered response.

#### Step 4: Practice Daily Self-Observation

To develop a habit of self-observation, cultivate your "inner scientist." You are the investigator, and the subject of your investigation is yourself—psychologists call this "me-search." Reserve a period of time once a day (as little as five or ten minutes) to sit quietly in a comfortable position, close your eyes, and simply watch your passing thoughts and feelings. If you get caught up in a thought, simply notice that you were caught up and go back to observing.

#### Step 5: Let the Mental Fizz Settle

Imagine a glass of water freshly filled from the faucet—full of fizz, unable to see through. If you wait and let it settle, the bubbles dissipate and the water turns crystal clear. This is what we're trying to do with our minds. Before a challenging phone call or meeting, take even a single minute of silence to yourself. One minute alone with eyes closed helps you observe your thoughts, feelings, and sensations and quiet your mind.

#### Step 6: Make the Balcony Your Home Base

The goal is not just to visit the balcony occasionally but to make it your home base. With practice, you can learn to be on the stage enacting the drama while simultaneously watching it from the balcony. The more you can live your life with clarity and calm, the more effectively you will be able to deal with others and pursue your interests with ease and success.

#### Best Practices

- **Do** go to the balcony before, during, and after any problematic conversation or negotiation
- **Do** name your emotions and thoughts to neutralize their effect
- **Do** approach your inner life with curiosity and detachment
- **Do** suspend self-judgment to the extent possible
- **Do** ask yourself "Isn't that curious?" instead of judging thoughts or feelings
- **Do** practice self-observation daily as a muscle-building exercise
- **Do** welcome all emotions without exclusion at your mental kitchen table
- **Do** recognize that actions can be wrong, but not thoughts or feelings
- **Don't** judge yourself critically or reject parts of yourself
- **Don't** try to suppress or exclude the inner judge—accept him instead
- **Don't** get identified with passing thoughts, emotions, or sensations
- **Don't** fall into the 3A trap (attack, accommodate, avoid) when triggered

#### Keep In Mind

- "If you talked to your friends the way you talk to yourself, you wouldn't have any."
- "To observe without evaluating is the highest form of intelligence." — Jiddu Krishnamurti
- "The curious paradox is that when I accept myself as I am, then I change." — Carl Rogers
- Our natural tendency is to judge ourselves critically and ignore or reject parts of ourselves
- Learning to observe yourself is simple, but not easy, particularly in conflicts
- With practice, you get better and better at staying on the balcony
- The balcony is not just a place to visit but ideally a home base

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Falling off the balcony when emotions get triggered (e.g., snapping at family after staying calm at work)
  **Solution:** Exercise self-observation like a muscle daily so it becomes a habit that transfers across contexts
- **Problem:** Self-judgment blocking self-understanding
  **Solution:** Treat even darker thoughts and emotions as interesting research material; ask "Isn't that curious?"
- **Problem:** Getting caught up in thoughts and emotions rather than observing them
  **Solution:** Name the emotion or thought to create distance; if you get caught up, simply notice it and return to observing
- **Problem:** Trying to suppress the inner judge, who then goes underground
  **Solution:** Accept the inner judge as one of the regular characters, even appreciate him as an old uncle trying to protect you
- **Problem:** Letting anger leak out destructively when not recognized
  **Solution:** Listen for dark feelings or thoughts you may normally disown; if you don't recognize anger, it can leak out unexpectedly

### Lesson 2: Listen to Yourself with Empathy

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Salary Raise Negotiation
**Use Case:** Workplace negotiation
When asking your boss for a raise, you may be so preoccupied with solving your own problem that you don't focus on the boss's problem (tight budget). Yet unless you can help the boss solve that problem, the boss is unlikely to offer a raise. Listening to yourself first can clear your mind so you can then listen to your boss and understand the budget constraint, enabling a more creative solution.

##### Example 2: Daily Kitchen Table Practice
**Use Case:** Emotional self-awareness
Each morning, Ury imagines sitting at a kitchen table where familiar thoughts and emotions arrive. He offers each one a seat and treats them as old friends or acquaintances. He even makes a place for the inner judge. This exercise helps him remain aware of his regular emotional patterns so they are less likely to catch him by surprise.

##### Example 3: Jamil Mahuad's Shadow Work
**Use Case:** Personal emotional integration
Mahuad, taught that sadness was unwelcome for males in his family, had difficulty connecting with grief. By recognizing and bringing painful emotions "to light," he incorporated that new part into himself, gained control over his anger, and operated from a balcony perspective during a historic peace negotiation.

#### Step 1: Distinguish Empathy from Sympathy

Sympathy means "to feel with"—feeling sorry for someone's predicament without necessarily understanding it. Empathy means "to feel into"—understanding what it is like to be in that situation. When listening to yourself, aim for empathy, not sympathy. You wouldn't judge a friend for having feelings; extend the same non-judgmental understanding to yourself.

#### Step 2: Listen with Positive Attention

Instead of talking negatively to yourself, try to listen to yourself with respect and positive attention. Instead of judging yourself, accept yourself just as you are. This is not easy because our natural tendency is self-criticism. The majority of our thoughts—up to 80 percent—are thought to be negative, obsessing about mistakes, battling guilt, or thinking about inadequacies.

#### Step 3: Listen to Your Body

Listening is not just an intellectual exercise but an emotional and physical one. When you feel afraid, try to feel the fear in your body. What does it feel like? Icy? A pit in your stomach? Parched throat? Recognize its familiar feeling and stay with it without pushing it away. Try to relax and feel your way into the fear. Breathe into it. This helps you slowly release it.

#### Step 4: Use Participant Observation

Combine active participation with outside observation. Anthropologists find that the best way to understand a foreign culture is to participate in it actively while maintaining an outside observer's perspective. Use this same method to understand yourself: feel your emotions from the inside while observing them from the balcony.

#### Step 5: Seek Support If Needed

If deeper listening to yourself seems awkward or too challenging, ask a friend—or even a professional counselor or therapist—to listen to you until you can make a habit of listening to yourself. Or consider keeping a daily journal. Writing down your feelings and thoughts, even for a few minutes, keeps you on the balcony and helps uncover patterns you wouldn't see in the rush of life.

#### Step 6: Clear Your Mind to Listen to Others

One great benefit of listening to yourself before entering a problematic conversation is that it clears your mind so you can then listen far more easily to others. The main obstacle to listening to others may be all the unheard emotions and thoughts clamoring for attention and cluttering up your mind. The secret to listening to others may be to listen to yourself first.

#### Best Practices

- **Do** listen to yourself with the same empathy you would offer a close friend
- **Do** accept yourself just as you are, without judgment
- **Do** listen to your body as well as your mind
- **Do** combine observation (outside view) with listening (inside experience)
- **Do** welcome all emotions, including anger and fear, without stigma
- **Do** keep a daily journal to uncover patterns
- **Do** recognize that self-judgment is the greatest barrier to self-understanding
- **Do** seek support from friends or professionals if needed
- **Don't** talk to yourself the way you wouldn't talk to friends
- **Don't** suppress or disown dark feelings—they will leak out destructively
- **Don't** confuse empathy with sympathy
- **Don't** neglect the physical dimension of emotions

#### Keep In Mind

- "You talked to your friends the way you talk to yourself, you wouldn't have any."
- Self-judgment is the greatest barrier to self-understanding
- If you don't recognize when you're feeling angry and listen for what's behind it, it can leak out destructively
- The kitchen table exercise helps you remain aware of regular emotional patterns
- Accepting the inner judge as a regular character is the best way to tame him
- Listening to yourself before negotiations clears your mind for listening to others

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Self-judgment prevents honest self-reflection
  **Solution:** Ask "Isn't that curious?" to create distance and open inquiry rather than judgment
- **Problem:** Unheard emotions clutter the mind and block listening to others
  **Solution:** Listen to yourself first to clear mental space
- **Problem:** Dark feelings leak out destructively when not recognized
  **Solution:** Listen for emotions you normally disown or stigmatize, especially anger
- **Problem:** Inner judge goes underground if suppressed
  **Solution:** Accept him as one of the regular characters at your mental kitchen table
- **Problem:** Difficulty connecting with painful emotions (sadness, grief)
  **Solution:** Bring shadow emotions to light by putting them in the spotlight; this starts incorporating that "new" part into who you are

### Lesson 3: Uncover Your Underlying Needs

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Abilio Diniz - From Stock Price to Freedom
**Use Case:** Business negotiation
Abilio initially gave Ury a list of concrete demands: sell stock at a certain price, eliminate a three-year noncompete clause, real estate, and other items. When pressed for what these would give him, he revealed his deepest need: freedom. This freedom meant time with his family (most important) and freedom to pursue business dreams. His kidnapping experience had given special resonance to this need. Freedom became the "north star" that oriented all actions, leading to resolution in four days.

##### Example 2: The Holy Grail Knight
**Use Case:** The power of asking "What ails thee?"
A young knight failed to obtain the Holy Grail because he was tongue-tied. Decades later, the older knight simply asked the injured king, "What ails thee?" By listening to the king's woes and uncovering his deepest needs, a human connection of friendship grew, and the king gave him the Grail.

##### Example 3: Sumatra Civil War - From Independence to Interests
**Use Case:** International conflict resolution
Rebel leaders in a 25-year civil war in Sumatra were crystal clear about their position (independence) but not clear about their deeper motivations. Ury asked "Why do you want independence?" After an uncomfortable silence, they identified interests: self-rule, control over economic resources, preservation of culture and language. When asked what strategy would best serve these interests, they acknowledged war could not be won even in ten years. They formed a political party, ran for office, and eventually became governor and vice-governor, achieving their strategic interests without formal independence.

##### Example 4: The Salary Raise - From Position to Creative Options
**Use Case:** Workplace negotiation
A person's position might be "I want a 15 percent raise." But their underlying interests could be recognition, fairness, career development, material need, or a combination. If the interest is recognition and the boss cannot give a high raise due to budget constraints, creative options like a new title or prestigious assignment might still satisfy the interest. Uncovering interests opens up new possibilities.

##### Example 5: The Universal "Why" Chain
**Use Case:** Personal self-discovery
Asking "Why?" repeatedly leads to universal bedrock desires:
- "Why do you want the raise?" → "To have more money."
- "Why do you want more money?" → "So I can get married."
- "Why do you want to get married?" → "Because it will bring me love."
- "Why do you want to be loved?" → "To be happy."
The bedrock desire is universal: to be loved and happy. This opens up new internal inquiry: Does your happiness depend on external outcomes, or can it come from inside?

#### Step 1: Ask "What Do I Really Want?"

When you have a list of concrete demands or desires, press yourself deeper. What will these things give you? What do you most want right now in your life? Go beyond surface-level wants to the deeper need beneath them. In Abilio's case, the concrete items gave him freedom.

#### Step 2: Listen to Your Feelings

Feelings of dissatisfaction are the language that your needs use to communicate with you. When your needs are frustrated or unfulfilled, you naturally feel anxiety, fear, anger, or sadness. Instead of pushing these feelings away, listen to them. They are pointing you toward unmet concerns and interests.

#### Step 3: Ask "Why?" Repeatedly

The magic question to uncover your true interests and needs is "Why?" Keep asking yourself why—as many times as necessary—until you get down to your bedrock need. The deeper you go, the more likely you are to invent creative options that can satisfy your interests. In negotiation, positions are what you say you want; interests are why you want it.

#### Step 4: Identify Your Universal Needs

Among our basic psychological needs, two universal ones stand out: protection (or safety), which promises the absence of pain, and connection (or love), which promises the presence of pleasure. Recognizing these universal needs can help you understand what drives your desires and reactions.

#### Step 5: Distinguish Position from Interest

Your position is your stated demand ("I want a 15 percent raise"). Your interests are your underlying needs, desires, concerns, fears, and aspirations (recognition, fairness, career development, material need). In negotiation and in life, focusing on interests rather than positions opens up creative solutions.

#### Step 6: Find Your North Star

Once you've uncovered your deepest need, let it become your "north star"—the guiding principle that orients all your actions. For Abilio, freedom became that north star. For the knight, it was the wounded king's well-being. Having clarity on your deepest need transforms your ability to make decisions and negotiate.

#### Best Practices

- **Do** ask "What do I really want?" and press beyond surface answers
- **Do** treat feelings of dissatisfaction as useful information about unmet needs
- **Do** ask "Why?" repeatedly to reach bedrock needs
- **Do** distinguish between positions (what you want) and interests (why you want it)
- **Do** look for creative options that satisfy underlying interests
- **Do** identify your universal needs (protection/safety and connection/love)
- **Do** let your deepest need become your north star
- **Do** take concrete actions to pursue your deepest needs even before entering negotiation
- **Don't** stop at surface-level wants without probing deeper
- **Don't** confuse positions with interests
- **Don't** ignore feelings of dissatisfaction—they are signals
- **Don't** assume your happiness depends solely on external outcomes

#### Keep In Mind

- Feelings of dissatisfaction are the language your needs use to communicate with you
- The deeper you go in uncovering underlying needs, the more universal those needs become
- Uncovering interests opens up new possibilities you might not have thought of before
- If you can find love and happiness from the inside, you are more likely to find them whether you get the external outcome or not
- Self-acceptance is a yes to self, perhaps the greatest gift we can give ourselves
- Acceptance creates the sense of safety within which we can more easily face a problem

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Being clear about position but unclear about underlying interests (like the Sumatra rebels)
  **Solution:** Systematically ask "Why?" behind your stated position until you reach bedrock needs
- **Problem:** Thinking that accepting yourself diminishes motivation for change
  **Solution:** Remember that acceptance usually has the opposite effect—it creates safety for change
- **Problem:** Reacting impulsively instead of acting from clarified needs
  **Solution:** Let your deepest need become your north star; orient all actions around it
- **Problem:** Continuing to fight because you don't know what you really want
  **Solution:** Take time for self-inquiry before external negotiation; say yes to yourself first
- **Problem:** Assuming happiness depends on external outcomes
  **Solution:** Explore whether happiness can come from inside, independent of outcomes

### Lesson 4: From Self-Judgment to Self-Understanding

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Abilio's Continued Struggle
**Use Case:** Long-term self-mastery
Even after uncovering his deepest need (freedom), Abilio encountered internal difficulties. In a magazine interview about moving beyond the battle, he mentioned his adversary by full name thirty-eight times. In a board meeting, despite his intention to stay calm, he repeatedly called his opponents cowards. He persisted through long talks with his wife and family, weekly therapy, and dedication to spending more time on the balcony. By understanding and accepting himself, he became his own ally rather than his own worst opponent.

##### Example 2: The Inner Yes Opens External Negotiation
**Use Case:** Business dispute resolution
Before approaching his adversary at the negotiation table, Abilio took concrete actions to pursue his freedom: became chair of another major company's board, found a new office outside company headquarters, went on a prolonged holiday with his family, and began exploring a new business deal. By saying yes to his needs, he opened the possibility of a genuine negotiation where neither side would lose.

#### Step 1: Accept Yourself Just as You Are

Self-acceptance is a yes to self, perhaps the greatest gift we can give yourself. If self-judgment is a no to self, self-acceptance transforms you from your own opponent to your own ally. As Carl Rogers noted: "The curious paradox is that when I accept myself as I am, then I change."

#### Step 2: Make Concrete Actions to Pursue Your Needs

Saying yes to yourself involves taking concrete actions to meet your deepest needs, even before negotiating with others. For Abilio, this meant structural changes in his life that embodied his commitment to freedom. These actions build internal credibility and create space for external negotiation.

#### Step 3: Practice Continually

The journey from self-judgment to self-understanding takes hard and continual work. It is common to be torn and undecided in problematic situations. That is why the patient and courageous practice of putting yourself in your shoes is so vital.

#### Step 4: Use Support Systems

Abilio's process included long intimate talks with his wife and family, weekly therapy, and conversations with his advisor. You don't have to do this alone. Use your support systems to help you understand and accept yourself.

#### Best Practices

- **Do** practice self-acceptance even when it feels difficult
- **Do** take concrete actions to pursue your deepest needs
- **Do** use support systems (family, friends, therapists)
- **Do** persist through setbacks and internal contradictions
- **Do** understand that this is continual work, not a one-time fix
- **Don't** expect immediate or perfect self-mastery
- **Don't** try to do the psychological work alone if you have support available
- **Don't** give up when you find yourself falling off the balcony

#### Keep In Mind

- Even once you uncover your deepest need, internal difficulties may remain
- Self-judgment may persist even after intellectual understanding
- The process is patient and courageous work
- Becoming your own ally rather than your own worst opponent transforms your external relationships
- Saying yes to yourself opens the possibility of genuine negotiation with others

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Intellectual understanding but emotional resistance (Abilio mentioned adversary 38 times in one interview)
  **Solution:** Combine intellectual work with emotional work (therapy, journaling, support systems)
- **Problem:** Snap reactions despite expressed intention to stay calm
  **Solution:** Build the balcony muscle through daily practice so it transfers to high-stakes moments
- **Problem:** Feeling furious and undecided even after clarity on deepest needs
  **Solution:** Accept that this is normal; persist with support systems and daily practice
- **Problem:** Believing acceptance will diminish motivation
  **Solution:** Remember acceptance creates safety for facing problems and working on them
