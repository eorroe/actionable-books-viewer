# Getting to Yes with Yourself - Chapter 2 Analysis

## Overview

Chapter 2 "Develop Your Inner BATNA" teaches readers how to break free from the blame game and reclaim personal power through self-responsibility. The chapter argues that genuine negotiation power doesn't start externally but comes from an internal commitment to take care of one's own needs regardless of what others do. By shifting from blame to self-responsibility, readers learn to get to "yes" with themselves first, which then enables them to get to "yes" with others in both personal and professional conflicts.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When you feel trapped in a conflict cycle of blame and counter-blame
- When negotiating with someone who appears more powerful than you
- When you find yourself making others responsible for your emotional needs
- When dealing with difficult relationships at home, work, or in the community
- When you need to maintain composure and effectiveness under high-stakes pressure
- When you feel like a victim of circumstances beyond your control

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: The Blame Game Undermines Your Power

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Soviet-American Nuclear Negotiations
During Cold War conferences in the mid-1980s, Ury observed that opening sessions with mutual accusations poisoned the atmosphere and wasted valuable time. By labeling the first session "Mutual Accusations" and scheduling it before breakfast, the facilitators eliminated the blame game entirely, allowing substantive negotiation to begin immediately.

##### Example 2: Johnson & Johnson Tylenol Crisis (1982)
When seven people died from cyanide-laced Tylenol capsules in Chicago, CEO James Burke faced a critical choice. Experts advised against a nationwide recall since the incidents were localized and not J&J's fault. Burke chose to take full responsibility anyway, recalling all Tylenol products nationwide at an estimated $100 million cost. The result was a dramatic restoration of public trust and eventual recovery of market share, demonstrating that taking responsibility can transform crisis into opportunity.

##### Example 3: Sam's Car Accidents
A young man repeatedly destroyed vehicles through accidents while blaming external factors—other drivers, road conditions, poor lighting. After observing his own pattern and probing his suppressed feelings of insecurity and anger, he took full responsibility for his aggressive driving. Once he got to yes with himself, the accident pattern ceased entirely and family tensions resolved.

##### Example 4: Jerry White and the Land Mine
Jerry White, a college student studying abroad in Jerusalem, stepped on a land mine left from the Six-Day War and lost his leg. Lying in a hospital bed for months, a fellow patient told him: "Jerry, this will be the worst thing that ever happened to you or the best thing. You decide." White chose not to settle into victimhood, took responsibility for his life, co-founded Survivor Corps, and helped lead the International Campaign to Ban Landmines, which won the Nobel Peace Prize.

##### Example 5: Susan and Frank's Marriage
Susan blamed her husband Frank for their communication problems, believing he was at fault for never talking with her. Nagging only made him withdraw further. Through therapy, she realized her own nagging was a significant contribution to the negative dynamic, recognized Frank's trauma-based need for safety, and stopped trying to control him. She made an unconditional commitment to take care of her needs regardless of his behavior. The criticism stopped, Frank felt safe enough to open up, and their marriage was not only saved but transformed.

##### Example 6: Ury's Daughter Gabriela's Medical Journey
Ury and his wife faced their daughter Gabi's congenital VATER syndrome and fourteen major surgeries. They learned to stop blaming themselves, insensitive doctors, or life itself, and instead took responsibility for their own psychological needs. By managing their anxiety and treating doctors as partners rather than adversaries, they became more effective advocates for Gabi. One doctor initially criticized in front of them later became a close friend who provided hours of free consultation.

##### Example 7: Turkish-Kurdish Peace Dialogue
At a confidential meeting between Turkish and Kurdish opinion leaders during Turkey's civil war, a retired Turkish general made a sincere apology: "As a former leader of the armed forces of Turkey, I want to acknowledge the suffering of countless Kurdish villagers... personally, I want to say that I am profoundly sorry." This single statement—rooted in the general's prior inner work of owning his actions—dramatically changed the atmosphere and opened the way for working together to end the war.

#### Step 1: Identify When You Are Blaming

Notice when you are caught in the blame game—blaming others, circumstances, or yourself for a conflict or problem. Recognize that blame feels temporarily righteous and innocent but actually undermines your power by making you a victim of external forces.

#### Step 2: Reframe the Core Question

Shift the guiding question from "Who is to blame?" to "What do we have to learn?" or "What can I do?" When faced with adversity, choose curiosity over blame. Ask yourself what lesson life is bringing rather than resisting your circumstances.

#### Step 3: Go to the Balcony and Then Listen to Yourself

When emotions run high, mentally step onto the balcony to gain perspective. Then listen inwardly to your underlying feelings—fear, anxiety, shame, anger—rather than numbing them or acting on them immediately. Accept these emotions with self-compassion. The way forward is through the pain, not around it.

#### Step 4: Take Responsibility for Your Life

Accept that you are responsible for your words, actions, and reactions. Stop deflecting responsibility onto external factors like your boss, family, or circumstances. Own your failures and faults as well as your successes. Understand that while you may not choose your circumstances, you can choose your response to them.

#### Step 5: Develop Your Inner BATNA

Make an unconditional commitment to yourself to take care of your deepest needs no matter what other people do or don't do. This commitment is your Inner BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement). It removes the responsibility for meeting your true needs from the other person's shoulders and places it back where it belongs: with you.

#### Step 6: Own Your Relationships

Recognize that every relationship has at least two parties contributing to its dynamics. Acknowledge your own contribution to problematic relationships, even if it seems small compared to the other person's. It takes two to create the mess—and only one to begin to transform it.

#### Step 7: Take Responsibility for Your Needs

Identify your core psychological needs for satisfaction, fulfillment, connection, and protection. Commit to meeting them yourself rather than making others responsible. In negotiations, this means you are less dependent on the other side to satisfy you, giving you confidence, freedom, and power.

#### Step 8: Transform Your Approach to Others

When you have gotten to yes with yourself, approach others from a position of inner strength rather than neediness. Communicate your boundaries clearly, respect the other person's autonomy, and focus on problem-solving rather than blame. Like Susan, you can say: "I'm no longer willing to accept X, but I won't pressure you to change. I'll interpret your actions as indicating your decision, and I'll make my decision accordingly."

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Take the "response-ability"—the ability to respond constructively to any situation
- ✅ Focus on what you can control: your own actions, reactions, and commitments
- ✅ Accept responsibility before offering apologies or seeking reconciliation
- ✅ View yourself as the director of your life's play, not just a victim of destiny
- ✅ Treat pain and fear by moving through them, not around them
- ✅ Reframe victim identity into leadership identity
- ✅ See relationships as systems where you have partial responsibility
- ✅ Cultivate self-understanding alongside self-responsibility to avoid self-pity or self-blame
- ✅ Make an unconditional commitment to your own well-being as your Inner BATNA
- ✅ Use your Inner BATNA as the foundation for developing your outer BATNA

#### Keep In Mind

- Self-understanding without self-responsibility risks dissolving into self-pity
- Self-responsibility without self-understanding can deteriorate into self-blame
- The blame game is tempting because it makes us feel innocent and righteous, but the costs are huge
- Taking responsibility may feel heavy but is actually liberating—it frees energy trapped in blaming others
- The moment you recognize you are in a prison of your own making, the walls begin to crumble
- You may not be the playwright of your life, but you can choose to be the director
- Even when you are only partly in control of circumstances, you have considerable control over your experiences
- Genuine power starts inside of you and is always available regardless of outer situations
- The best BATNA of all is the one that starts inside
- Responsibility equals power—the power to meet your deepest needs

#### Security & Safety Notes

- When dealing with medical professionals or other authority figures on whom you depend, maintain respect while also advocating assertively for your needs
- Create emotional safety for yourself before difficult interactions by managing your own anxiety levels
- Recognize when others' behavior may be rooted in trauma (like Frank's childhood trauma) without accepting harmful behavior
- Apologies from powerful figures should be evaluated based on the sincerity and prior inner work behind them

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Falling into the blame game when conflicts arise
  **Solution:** Immediately reframe from "Who is to blame?" to "What can I learn?" and "What can I do?"
- **Problem:** Making others responsible for meeting your core psychological needs
  **Solution:** Answer the question "Who is responsible for meeting my core psychological needs?" with "Myself" rather than "Someone else"
- **Problem:** Acting from neediness, which gives power to the other person
  **Solution:** Develop your Inner BATNA—make an unconditional commitment to take care of your needs no matter what
- **Problem:** Thinking taking responsibility means self-blame
  **Solution:** Understand that self-blame looks backward ("What a failure I have been"), while self-responsibility looks forward ("What can I do to make this successful?")
- **Problem:** Trying to control others' behavior
  **Solution:** Focus on controlling your own responses and commitments, not the other person's actions
- **Problem:** Going around pain instead of through it
  **Solution:** Deliberately face difficult emotions by entering the fear rather than steering around it
- **Problem:** Giving up during difficult negotiations because the other side seems more powerful
  **Solution:** Remember your Inner BATNA is always available and gives you confidence no matter the external power balance
- **Problem:** Assuming responsibility means accepting fault or guilt
  **Solution:** Reframe responsibility as "response-ability"—the ability to respond constructively, not to judge the past
