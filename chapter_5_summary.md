# Chapter 5: The Hedgehog Concept – Simplicity within the Three Circles

## Overview

This chapter introduces the **Hedgehog Concept**, the distinguishing framework that separates good-to-great companies from comparison companies. Drawing from Isaiah Berlin's famous essay, Collins categorizes organizations as either **hedgehogs** (those who know one big thing and simplify a complex world into a single organizing idea) or **foxes** (those who pursue many ends simultaneously and remain scattered and diffused). 

The Hedgehog Concept is not a goal, strategy, or intention to be the best—it is an *understanding* of what an organization can potentially be the best at, derived from the intersection of three circles:
1. What you can be the best in the world at
2. What drives your economic engine
3. What you are deeply passionate about

Greatness requires all three circles. Having only one or two produces good results, not great ones.

---

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Adopt the Hedgehog Mindset – Simplify to One Big Idea

**What to DO:**
Reduce all challenges and strategies to a single, crystalline concept that unifies and guides every decision. Anything that does not relate to this core idea holds no relevance.

**Examples:**
- **Walgreens**: "The best, most convenient drugstores, with high profit per customer visit." This simple concept beat Intel, GE, Coca-Cola, and Merck over 25 years.
- **Freud** (unconscious), **Darwin** (natural selection), **Einstein** (relativity), **Adam Smith** (division of labor) – all hedgehogs who took complex worlds and simplified them.
- **Wells Fargo**: Running a bank like a business, focused on the western United States – not trying to be a global bank like Citicorp.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Write down every major strategic initiative, product line, and market your organization currently pursues.
2. For each item, ask: Does this directly relate to one unifying concept, or is it a distraction?
3. Identify the one big idea that explains why your organization exists and how it wins.
4. Test whether this idea can guide every major decision—hiring, resource allocation, acquisitions, and exits.
5. Ruthlessly eliminate anything that does not fit within this single concept.

**Best Practices:**
- The Hedgehog Concept should be simple enough to state in one sentence.
- It should be obvious and straightforward—"so simple it sounds ridiculous to talk about it."
- Hedgehogs are not simpletons; they have piercing insight that sees through complexity to underlying patterns.
- Stick with what you understand and let your abilities, not your egos, determine what you attempt.

**Keep in Mind:**
- "Growth!" is NOT a Hedgehog Concept. If you have the right Hedgehog Concept, your main problem will not be how to grow, but how *not* to grow too fast.
- Being a hedgehog means ignoring the noise of management fads, visionary pronouncements, and fear-based reacting.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Being a fox**: Scattered, diffused, moving on many levels, never integrating thinking into one overall vision. Comparison companies (like Eckerd) looked smarter and more cunning but failed because they lacked a unifying concept.
- **Thinking simplicity equals stupidity**: Hedgehogs understand that profound insight requires simplicity (e.g., E=mc²). Simplicity is a sign of deep understanding, not lack of intelligence.
- **Having a goal to be the best instead of understanding what you can be the best at**: A Hedgehog Concept is not a goal, strategy, or intention—it is an *understanding*.

### Lesson 2: Master the Three Circles Framework

**What to DO:**
Ensure your Hedgehog Concept sits at the intersection of all three circles. Missing any one circle produces success but not greatness.

**The Three Circles:**
1. **What you can be the best in the world at** (and equally important, what you cannot be the best at)
2. **What drives your economic engine**
3. **What you are deeply passionate about**

**Examples:**
- **Walgreens**: Convenient drugstores (best-at) + profit per customer visit (economic engine) + passion for serving customers conveniently (passion).
- **Fannie Mae**: Best capital markets player in mortgages (best-at) + profit per mortgage risk level (economic engine) + passion for democratizing home ownership (passion).
- **Abbott Laboratories**: Best at creating products that contribute to cost-effective health care (best-at) + profit per health care solution (economic engine) + passion for improving health care (passion).

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Circle 1 Assessment**: Honestly evaluate what your organization can potentially do better than any other organization in the world. Also identify what you absolutely cannot be the best at—even if it is your current core business.
2. **Circle 2 Assessment**: Identify the single denominator (profit per x, or cash flow per x in the social sector) that has the greatest and most sustainable impact on your economic engine.
3. **Circle 3 Assessment**: Discover what genuinely ignites passion in you and your team. Do not manufacture passion—uncover it.
4. **Intersection Test**: Verify that your Hedgehog Concept lies at the true intersection of all three circles.
5. **Sanity Check**: Confirm that each circle is independently strong; a weak circle will pull the entire concept down.

**Best Practices:**
- If you make a lot of money doing things at which you could never be the best, you build a successful company, not a great one.
- If you become the best at something but lack intrinsic passion, you will never remain on top.
- If you are passionate but cannot be the best at it or it doesn't make economic sense, you may have fun but won't produce great results.
- All three circles are required for a fully developed Hedgehog Concept.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Confusing core business with Hedgehog Concept**: Just because something is your core business—just because you've been doing it for years—does not mean you can be the best in the world at it.
- **Confusing core competence with best-in-world potential**: You can have competence at something but not necessarily have the potential to be the best in the world at it (the "curse of competence").
- **Ignoring what you cannot be the best at**: Ego often blinds organizations to their limitations. Wells Fargo had to accept it could not be better than Citicorp in global banking.
- **Having passion for only one or two circles**: Many companies have passion and economic insight but never confront the brutal fact that they cannot be the best at what they are doing.

### Lesson 3: Confront the Brutal Facts About What You Can (and Cannot) Be the Best At

**What to DO:**
Ruthlessly and egolessly assess what your organization has the potential to be the best in the world at—and what it does not. Use this understanding to prune away everything that does not fit.

**Examples:**
- **Abbott Laboratories vs. Upjohn (1964)**: Both companies were almost identical—pharmaceuticals, antibiotics, family management, lagging behind the industry. Abbott confronted the brutal fact that Merck had such a huge research lead that trying to be the best pharmaceutical company was like a high school football team trying to take on the Dallas Cowboys. Abbott pivoted to becoming the best at creating products that contribute to cost-effective health care (hospital nutritional products, diagnostic devices). Upjohn never confronted this reality, diversified into plastics and chemicals where it definitely could not be the best, and was acquired in 1995 with profits less than half of Abbott's.
- **Wells Fargo**: Put aside egos and pulled the plug on the vast majority of international operations, accepting it could not be better than Citicorp in global banking. Turned focus to running a bank like a business in the western United States.
- **Personal example**: A young person with straight A's in calculus and high SAT math scores might appear destined to be a mathematician, but upon encountering peers "genetically encoded for math," realizes they could be a very competent mathematician but never one of the best.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Assemble a group of the right people (see The Council in Lesson 6).
2. Ask the piercing question: "What can we potentially do better than any other company, and what can we not do better than any other company?"
3. If you cannot be the best at something, ask: "Then why are we doing it at all?"
4. Apply the Stockdale Paradox: Maintain faith that you will prevail (there must be something you can become the best at), while confronting the brutal facts of what you cannot be the best at.
5. Prune away all activities, business lines, and investments that do not fit within your true potential.
6. Direct resources exclusively to those few arenas where you could potentially be the best.

**Best Practices:**
- The "best in the world" standard goes far beyond core competence.
- The distinction between "good" and "best" is absolute. Doing what you are good at will only make you good; focusing solely on what you can potentially do better than any other organization is the only path to greatness.
- Recognize that you may not currently be engaged in the activity you could be the best at—your potential may lie outside your current business.
- Accept that your core business may not be the basis of your Hedgehog Concept.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **The curse of competence**: Many people fall into careers where they can never attain complete mastery and fulfillment because they are good at something but never the best. Parents and friends pressure them to continue: "But you're so good at it."
- **Ego-driven decisions**: Organizations pursue arenas that feed their egos rather than areas where they can truly be the best.
- **Growth for growth's sake**: Comparison companies like Eckerd and Great Western pursued growth without the benefit of a Hedgehog Concept, acquiring clumps of stores or entering unrelated industries to satisfy ego and stock price, not because they could be the best.
- **Living with delusion**: Upjohn continued to believe it could beat Merck despite being too small for the big-stakes pharmaceutical game, and lost nearly half the profits of Abbott before being acquired.

### Lesson 4: Identify Your Economic Denominator (Profit per X)

**What to DO:**
Discover the single ratio—profit per x (or cash flow per x in the social sector)—that has the single greatest and most sustainable impact on your economic engine. Use this denominator as the primary metric for decision-making.

**Examples:**
- **Walgreens**: Switched from profit per store to **profit per customer visit**. Convenient locations (nine stores per mile in downtown San Francisco!) are expensive, but increasing profit per customer visit funded the convenience model. Profit per store would have destroyed the concept (the quickest way to increase profit per store is to decrease the number of stores).
- **Wells Fargo**: Moved from standard banker metrics (profit per loan, profit per deposit) to **profit per employee**. This insight drove the shift to stripped-down branches and ATMs.
- **Fannie Mae**: Identified the subtle denominator of **profit per mortgage risk level**, not per mortgage (the "obvious" choice). The real driver is understanding risk of default better than anyone else, then making money selling insurance and managing the spread on that risk.
- **Nucor**: Chose **profit per ton of finished steel** rather than per employee or per fixed cost. This captured the duality of strong-work-ethic culture plus advanced manufacturing technology.
- **Pitney Bowes** and **Nucor**: Both were in bottom 5 percent industries but beat the market by well over five times because they attained profound insights into their economics.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Ask the single question: "If you could pick one and only one ratio—profit per x—to systematically increase over time, what x would have the greatest and most sustainable impact on your economic engine?"
2. List potential x variables (per employee, per store, per customer, per unit, per risk, etc.).
3. Test each candidate against your Hedgehog Concept: Does increasing this ratio reinforce or undermine your core idea?
4. Challenge your team to push for a single denominator rather than three or four. The act of forcing a single choice drives deeper insight.
5. Once identified, make this denominator the primary economic metric that guides resource allocation, pricing, location, and process decisions.
6. Regularly autopsy results: Are actions increasing your denominator? If not, adjust.

**Best Practices:**
- You do not absolutely need a single denominator, but pushing for one tends to produce better insight than letting yourself off the hook with multiple denominators.
- The denominator question is a mechanism to force deeper understanding of the key drivers in your economic engine—not an end in itself.
- Do not accept the "obvious" denominator. Fannie Mae's insight (profit per mortgage risk level) was subtle and unobvious, but brilliant.
- The denominator should align with and reinforce your Hedgehog Concept. Walgreens' profit per customer visit directly supported its convenience concept; profit per store would have destroyed it.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Using standard industry metrics**: Wells Fargo realized standard banker metrics (profit per loan, profit per deposit) would no longer be the key drivers in a deregulated, commoditized banking world.
- **Having multiple denominators**: Comparison companies typically did not identify a single key economic denominator.
- **Choosing a denominator that contradicts your concept**: The quickest way to increase profit per store is to decrease the number of stores and put them in less expensive locations—this would have destroyed Walgreens' convenience concept.
- **Thinking you need a great industry**: You absolutely do not need to be in a great industry to produce sustained great results. Only one of the good-to-great companies had the benefit of being in a top 10 percent industry; five were in bad to terrible industries.

### Lesson 5: Discover Passion—Do Not Manufacture It

**What to DO:**
Identify what genuinely ignites passion in you and your people. Only do those things that you can get passionate about. You cannot manufacture or motivate passion; you can only discover it.

**Examples:**
- **Philip Morris**: Executives were passionate consumers of their own products. Ross Millhiser, vice chairman, said, "I love cigarettes. It's one of the things that makes life really worth living." Board members felt they were "part of something really special." This passion drove great results.
- **Kimberly-Clark**: Executives shifted to paper-based consumer products because they could get more passionate about them. As one executive put it, traditional paper products are okay, "but they just don't have the charisma of a diaper."
- **Gillette**: Chose sophisticated, relatively expensive shaving systems over cheap disposables because executives "just couldn't get excited about cheap disposable razors." CEO talked about shaving systems "with the sort of technical gusto one expects from a Boeing or Hughes engineer." Top business school graduates were not hired if they didn't show enough passion for deodorant.
- **Fannie Mae**: People were not passionate about the mechanical process of packaging mortgages. But they were terrifically motivated by the mission: helping people of all classes realize the American dream of home ownership. Linda Knight said, "This wasn't just any old company getting into trouble; this was a company at the core of making home ownership a reality for thousands of Americans."
- **R.J. Reynolds**: Contrasted with Philip Morris. R.J. Reynolds executives saw tobacco as just a way to make money, diversified into unrelated arenas, lost passion for anything except making themselves rich, and ended in a leveraged buyout.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Honestly assess what activities, products, or missions genuinely excite you and your team—not what you think should excite you.
2. Ask: "What would I do even if I weren't paid for it?" "What makes me look forward to getting up in the morning?"
3. Ask: "Do we feel passionate about the mechanics of the business, or about what the business stands for?" Both are valid sources of passion.
4. If you cannot get passionate about what you are currently doing, recognize this as a signal to reconsider your direction—not as a problem to be solved with motivational programs.
5. Build your Hedgehog Concept around the intersection of genuine passion, not around what you think will look good to analysts or Wall Street.
6. Hire and promote people who share the passion. People who aren't passionate about what you do need not apply.

**Best Practices:**
- Passion is a discovery, not a manufactured motivational tool. Good-to-great companies did not say, "Okay, folks, let's get passionate about what we do." They said, "We should only do those things that we can get passionate about."
- Passion can be focused on what the company stands for (a mission) rather than the mechanics of the business itself. Fannie Mae's passion was about democratizing home ownership, not mortgage packaging.
- Passion must be deep and genuine. It doesn't matter if others find it odd that someone gets jazzed about diapers, deodorant, pharmacies, or postage meters.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Manufacturing passion**: Trying to stimulate passion through slogans, retreats, and motivational speeches when the underlying work does not genuinely ignite passion.
- **Being passionate about only one dimension**: You can be passionate all you want, but if you cannot be the best at it or it doesn't make economic sense, you won't produce great results.
- **Ignoring passion as "soft and fuzzy"**: Passion is an integral part of the strategic framework. Without it, you cannot sustain greatness.
- **Pursuing growth in areas without passion**: R.J. Reynolds diversified into arenas where it had no passion, ultimately losing passion for everything except making themselves rich.

### Lesson 6: Triumph of Understanding Over Bravado

**What to DO:**
Base your goals and strategies on deep understanding, not on ego, hype, or mindless bravado. Accept that getting the Hedgehog Concept takes time and iterative learning—it cannot be forced through off-site retreats.

**Examples:**
- **Prehedgehog vs. Posthedgehog states**: In the prehedgehog state, you are groping through fog, making slow, deliberate progress. With the Hedgehog Concept, you break into a clearing, the fog lifts, and you can see for miles. Comparison companies tried to run in the fog, making bad decisions at forks in the road, then having to reverse course.
- **Great Western vs. Fannie Mae**: Great Western wanted to grow every way it could—finance, leasing, insurance, manufactured houses—"a mite unwieldy." Its CEO said, "Don't worry about what you call us—a bank, an S&L, or a Zebra." Fannie Mae had a simple, crystalline understanding: it could be the best capital markets player in anything related to mortgages. By 1984, one year after clarifying its Hedgehog Concept, Fannie Mae exploded upward while Great Western kept lollygagging. Fannie Mae grew revenues nearly threefold from 1984 through 1996; Great Western grew only 25 percent.
- **Eckerd vs. Walgreens**: In the precise year of Eckerd's American Home Video acquisition (1981), Walgreens and Eckerd had virtually identical revenues ($1.7 billion). Ten years later, Walgreens had grown to over twice the revenues of Eckerd, with net profits $1 billion greater. Twenty years later, Eckerd ceased to exist as an independent company.
- **Joanne Collins personal example**: After years of training and accumulating data (track times, swim splits, race results), Joanne Collins looked at her newspaper one morning and calmly said, "I think I could win the Ironman." There was no bravado, no hype—just quiet recognition of a fact. She quit her job, turned down graduate school, and three years later became world champion at the Hawaii Ironman. She understood her Hedgehog Concept; she did not merely desire it.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Accept that a Hedgehog Concept cannot be created in a two-day off-site retreat with flip charts. Einstein took ten years of groping through the fog to develop the theory of special relativity.
2. Commit to an iterative process: Engage in vigorous dialogue and debate, infused with brutal facts, guided by the three circles, and learn from results.
3. Build a Council (see Lesson 7) to facilitate this process.
4. When your Hedgehog Concept finally emerges, expect it to arrive without fanfare—not with "tiresome, irritating blasts of mindless bravado" but with "the quiet ping of truth, like a single, clear, perfectly struck note hanging in the air in the hushed silence of a full auditorium at the end of a quiet movement of a Mozart piano concerto."
5. Continue going through the cycle of understanding: Ask the right questions, engage in debate, make decisions, autopsy the results, and learn.

**Best Practices:**
- Set goals and strategies based on understanding, not bravado.
- Comparison companies set their goals and strategies from bravado; good-to-great companies set theirs from understanding.
- Recognize that getting a Hedgehog Concept is an iterative process, not an event. It took about four years on average for the good-to-great companies to clarify their concepts.
- Increase the number of times you go through the full cycle of understanding in a given period to accelerate the process.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Trying to jump straight to a Hedgehog Concept**: You can do an off-site retreat, but you probably won't get it right. Insight doesn't happen that way.
- **Setting goals without understanding**: "We want to be the best" is not a Hedgehog Concept. You must understand *what* you can be the best at.
- **Obsession with growth without understanding**: Over two-thirds of comparison companies displayed an obsession with growth without the benefit of a Hedgehog Concept. Not one good-to-great company focused obsessively on growth, yet they created sustained, profitable growth far greater than the comparison companies.
- **Bravado-driven announcements**: Comparison companies produced "tiresome, irritating blasts of mindless bravado." When you get it right, there is no need for hype—the quiet truth speaks for itself.
- **Stopping after initial success**: Hasbro became the best-performing comparison company in the study because it understood all three circles, but it lost the discipline to stay within them after the unexpected death of its CEO. If you successfully apply these ideas and then stop doing them, you will slide backward.

### Lesson 7: Build and Use the Council

**What to DO:**
Assemble a standing Council of the right people to engage in rigorous dialogue and debate, guided by the three circles, to iteratively develop and refine your Hedgehog Concept.

**Examples:**
- Good-to-great companies used councils with benign names like: Long-Range Profit Improvement Committee, Corporate Products Committee, Strategic Thinking Group, and Executive Council.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Select 5 to 12 people for the Council, assembled and used by the leading executive.
2. Ensure each member has the ability to argue and debate in search of understanding, not from the egoistic need to win a point or protect a parochial interest.
3. Ensure each member retains the respect of every other Council member, without exception.
4. Include members from a range of perspectives, but each must have deep knowledge about some aspect of the organization and/or its environment.
5. Include key management team members, but do not limit to management, and do not make every executive automatically a member.
6. Make the Council a standing body, not an ad hoc committee assembled for a specific project.
7. Meet periodically—as much as once a week or as infrequently as once per quarter.
8. Do NOT seek consensus. Recognize that consensus decisions are often at odds with intelligent decisions. The responsibility for the final decision remains with the leading executive.
9. Keep the Council informal—do not list it on any formal organization chart or in formal documents.
10. Name the Council something innocuous and benign.
11. Use the Council to repeatedly go through the cycle: Ask the right questions (guided by the three circles), engage in vigorous debate, make decisions, autopsy the results, and learn.
12. To accelerate the process, increase the number of times you go through this full cycle in a given period.

**Best Practices:**
- The Council exists as a device to gain understanding about important issues facing the organization.
- Members argue to understand, not to win. The goal is insight, not victory.
- The Council does not seek consensus. Intelligent decisions often require going against the grain.
- The final decision authority remains with the leading executive.
- The Council is informal and flexible in its meeting cadence.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Seeking consensus**: Consensus decisions are often at odds with intelligent decisions.
- **Ego-driven debate**: Members arguing to win points or protect parochial interests destroy the Council's purpose.
- **Making it a formal committee**: The Council should be informal and not listed on any organization chart.
- **Having the wrong people**: Members must have deep knowledge and mutual respect. Including people who lack expertise or who cannot debate constructively undermines the process.
- **Using it as a one-time event**: The Council is a standing body for ongoing understanding, not a one-off project team.

### Lesson 8: Avoid the Growth-at-Any-Price Trap

**What to DO:**
Reject mindless growth without a Hedgehog Concept. Focus on building a brilliant economic engine within your three circles; sustainable growth will follow naturally.

**Examples:**
- **Eckerd**: Pursued growth through hodgepodge acquisitions (forty-two units here, thirty-six units there) with no unifying theme. In 1981, while Walgreens became religious about its convenient drugstore concept, Eckerd threw itself into the home video market with its purchase of American Home Video Corporation. The CEO said, "Some feel the purer we are the better we'll be. But I want growth, and the home video industry is only emerging—unlike, say, drugstore chains." Eckerd's home video foray produced $31 million in losses before being sold to Tandy for $72 million below book value.
- **Great Western**: Found itself in finance, leasing, insurance, and manufactured houses, continually acquiring companies in an expansion binge. "Bigger! More!" Grew revenues and earnings only 25 percent from 1984 through 1996, then lost its independence in 1997.
- **R.J. Reynolds**: Diversified away from tobacco into any arena where it could get growth, regardless of passion or ability to be the best. Eventually lost passion for anything except making themselves rich through a leveraged buyout.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. When you hear "growth" as a strategy, ask: "Growth in what? Toward what end?" Growth without a Hedgehog Concept is meaningless.
2. Evaluate every acquisition, expansion, or new initiative against your three circles: Does it fit within what you can be the best at, what drives your economic engine, and what you are passionate about?
3. If an opportunity does not fit within your three circles, reject it—even if it promises rapid growth.
4. Prune away business lines that do not fit, even if they are profitable. Abbott Laboratories had to accept it could no longer be the best pharmaceutical company, even though that was its entire history.
5. Trust that if you have the right Hedgehog Concept and make decisions consistently with it, growth will come as a byproduct.
6. If your main problem becomes how not to grow too fast, you have succeeded.

**Best Practices:**
- "Growth!" is not a Hedgehog Concept. It is a result of having the right Hedgehog Concept.
- Over two-thirds of comparison companies displayed an obsession with growth without the benefit of a Hedgehog Concept.
- Not one good-to-great company focused obsessively on growth, yet they created sustained, profitable growth far greater than the comparison companies that made growth their mantra.
- Profitable growth comes by pruning away all that does not fit with the Hedgehog Concept.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Growth at any price**: Acquiring businesses or entering markets just to grow, with no unifying theme or ability to be the best.
- **Confusing revenue growth with greatness**: Eckerd and Great Western had growth but not sustained, profitable growth. They eventually failed or lost independence.
- **Diversifying into unrelated arenas**: R.J. Reynolds and Upjohn both diversified into arenas where they definitely could not be the best.
- **Thinking diversification equals strength**: Spreading across unrelated industries looks sophisticated but destroys economic momentum and focus.

### Lesson 9: Sustain Discipline Over Time

**What to DO:**
Once you have your Hedgehog Concept, apply it consistently and relentlessly. The only way to remain great is to keep applying the fundamental principles that made you great.

**Examples:**
- **Walgreens**: Applied its simple concept with fanatical consistency for over 25 years—replacing inconvenient locations with convenient ones, pioneering drive-through pharmacies, clustering stores tightly in urban areas, adding high-margin services like one-hour photo developing.
- **Hasbro**: Became the best-performing comparison company in the study because it understood all three circles. However, it lost the discipline to stay within the three circles after the unexpected death of CEO Stephen Hassenfeld, sliding backward from great to good.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. Once your Hedgehog Concept is clear, communicate it constantly and consistently across the organization.
2. Align every major decision—hiring, promotions, resource allocation, acquisitions, and exits—with the three circles.
3. Do not abandon the concept when leadership changes or when short-term pressures mount.
4. Periodically audit whether the organization is still living within the three circles or has drifted.
5. If you drift, have the discipline to pull back and refocus.
6. Remember: The only way to remain great is to keep applying the fundamental principles that made you great.

**Best Practices:**
- Use the Hedgehog Concept as a filter for every opportunity and distraction.
- Build systems and incentives that reinforce the concept.
- Maintain relentless focus. As Carl Reichardt of Wells Fargo said, "There's more money to be made in Modesto than Tokyo."

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Losing discipline after success**: Hasbro proved that understanding the three circles is not enough; you must maintain the discipline to stay within them.
- **Drifting from the concept**: Over time, organizations accumulate initiatives, business lines, and activities that slowly pull them away from their Hedgehog Concept.
- **Leadership transitions**: A change in leadership can disrupt the disciplined application of the concept unless it is embedded in the organization's systems and culture.

## Key Terms for Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Hedgehog Concept** | A simple, crystalline concept derived from the intersection of the three circles that unifies and guides all organizational efforts. It is an understanding of what you can be the best at, not a goal or intention to be the best. |
| **Three Circles** | The framework consisting of: (1) What you can be the best in the world at, (2) What drives your economic engine, and (3) What you are deeply passionate about. |
| **Economic Denominator** | The single ratio—profit per x (or cash flow per x in the social sector)—that has the greatest and most sustainable impact on your economic engine. |
| **Profit per X** | The specific economic denominator chosen by an organization (e.g., profit per customer visit for Walgreens, profit per employee for Wells Fargo, profit per mortgage risk level for Fannie Mae). |
| **Hedgehog** | An organization or person who simplifies a complex world into a single organizing idea and sticks to it relentlessly. |
| **Fox** | An organization or person who pursues many ends simultaneously, sees the world in all its complexity, and remains scattered, diffused, and inconsistent. |
| **Core Competence vs. Hedgehog Concept** | Core competence is what you are good at; the Hedgehog Concept is what you can potentially be the best in the world at. They are not the same thing. |
| **Curse of Competence** | The trap of staying in a career or business where you are competent but can never be the best, leading to unfulfilled potential. |
| **Prehedgehog State** | The period before gaining a Hedgehog Concept, when an organization is groping through fog, making slow, deliberate progress with limited visibility. |
| **Posthedgehog State** | The period after gaining a Hedgehog Concept, when the fog lifts, decisions become easier, and momentum builds swiftly. |
| **The Council** | A standing, informal body of 5–12 key people who engage in rigorous dialogue and debate, guided by the three circles, to iteratively develop the Hedgehog Concept. |
| **Bravado vs. Understanding** | Bravado is setting goals based on ego, hype, and desire; understanding is setting goals based on piercing insight into reality. Good-to-great companies use understanding; comparison companies use bravado. |
| **Stockdale Paradox** | The principle of maintaining faith that you will prevail ("There must be a way for us to prevail") while confronting the brutal facts of your current reality ("and we will find it... and we will not delude ourselves"). |
| **Best-in-World Standard** | The severe standard of excellence required for Circle 1: understanding what your organization truly has the potential to be the very best at, not just what it wants to be the best at. |

## Summary of Key Findings

- **Hedgehogs win**: Despite the fox's greater cunning, the hedgehog always wins because it simplifies and relentlessly executes one big idea.
- **Strategy alone is not enough**: Both good-to-great and comparison companies had strategies. The difference was that good-to-great companies had a simple, crystalline Hedgehog Concept that guided all decisions.
- **Simplicity is the hallmark**: The Hedgehog Concept is "simple, simple, simple"—so simple it sounds ridiculous, yet it is deeply insightful.
- **All three circles are essential**: Missing any one circle produces good results, not great results.
- **Understanding takes time**: It took about four years on average for good-to-great companies to clarify their Hedgehog Concept. It is an iterative process, not an event.
- **Industry does not determine destiny**: You absolutely do not need to be in a great industry. Only one good-to-great company was in a top 10 percent industry; five were in bad to terrible industries.
- **Discipline sustains greatness**: The only way to remain great is to keep applying the fundamental principles that made you great.
