# Good to Great

## Overview

A framework built from a five-year study of 28 companies—11 that made the leap from good to great and 17 comparison companies that did not—using 10.5 people-years of research, systematic coding of nearly 6,000 articles, and 384 million bytes of data. Jim Collins and his research team studied these companies to uncover principles that distinguish organizations achieving sustained, above-market returns (at least 3× the market over 15+ years) from organizations that remain merely good. The findings defy conventional wisdom: greatness is not about charismatic CEOs, visionary strategy, or revolutionary technology, but about disciplined application of a simple framework built on Level 5 leadership, the right people, confronting brutal facts, the Hedgehog Concept, a culture of discipline, and the flywheel effect.

## When to Follow Book Teachings

- When you need to transform a good company into a great one with sustained, remarkable performance
- When working with executive teams to assess leadership using the Level 5 Leadership framework (evaluating humility, professional will, window-and-mirror behavior, and track record) and to develop Level 5 traits through targeted coaching focused on humility and professional will, structured 360-degree feedback with specific behavioral rubrics, and deliberate weekly practice of window-and-mirror behaviors
- When building or restructuring an organization and need to decide who belongs on the team based on character, work ethic, and demonstrated Level 5 traits rather than specific skills or knowledge
- When developing strategy and need the Good to Great framework grounded in empirical evidence rather than management fads
- When the organization is facing adversity, crisis, or significant change and needs the Stockdale Paradox framework to maintain faith in eventual success while confronting brutal facts of current reality
- When making people decisions—hiring, promoting, or removing executives—and want to apply the "First Who, Then What" discipline, including character-based hiring, Packard's Law, and rigorous evaluation criteria focused on sustained results, character, and cultural fit, applied consistently without emotion
- When the user asks about what separates great companies from good ones over the long term
- When evaluating technology investments and want to avoid the trap of technology-driven hype

## Lessons From Book

### Lesson 1: Understand That Good Is the Enemy of Great

#### Examples

**Example 1: Walgreens Transformation**
Walgreens bumped along as a very average company for over forty years, underperforming the general market by approximately 0–10% annually over the prior two decades. Then in 1975, seemingly out of nowhere, it began to climb. From December 31, 1975, to January 1, 2000, $1 invested in Walgreens beat $1 invested in Intel by nearly two times, General Electric by nearly five times, Coca-Cola by nearly eight times, and the general stock market by over fifteen times.

**Example 2: Kimberly-Clark Under Darwin Smith**
Darwin Smith became CEO of Kimberly-Clark in 1971. The company's stock had fallen 36 percent behind the general market over the previous twenty years. Over the next twenty years, Smith created a stunning transformation, turning Kimberly-Clark into the leading paper-based consumer products company in the world, generating cumulative stock returns 4.1 times the general market.

**Example 3: The Research Discovery**
The research identified 11 good-to-great companies from an initial screening of 1,435 companies on the Fortune 500 list from 1965 to 1995. These companies attained extraordinary results, averaging cumulative stock returns 6.9 times the general market in the fifteen years following their transition points. If you had invested $1 in a mutual fund of the good-to-great companies in 1965 and held until January 1, 2000, your $1 would have multiplied 471 times, compared to a 56-fold increase in the market.

#### Step 1: Accept That Good Is the Enemy of Great

The vast majority of companies never become great precisely because the vast majority become quite good—and that is their main problem. We do not have great schools principally because we have good schools. We do not have great government principally because we have good government. The disease of "just being good" is incurable unless you make an explicit decision to pursue greatness and commit to the disciplined application of the framework.

#### Step 2: Embrace the Possibility of Transformation

The good-to-great transformations happened in the most unlikely of situations. The majority of the 11 good-to-great companies had no significant market presence, minimal growth, and no widely recognized brand before their transformation points. This teaches that greatness is not a function of circumstance. Greatness is largely a matter of conscious choice and disciplined application of timeless principles.

#### Step 3: Understand the Research Method

The research team spent 10.5 people years of effort, read and systematically coded nearly 6,000 articles, generated more than 2,000 pages of interview transcripts, and created 384 million bytes of computer data. Every primary concept in the final framework showed up as a change variable in 100 percent of the good-to-great companies and in less than 30 percent of the comparison companies during the pivotal years. Any insight that failed this test did not make it into the book as a chapter-level concept.

#### Step 4: Apply the Framework Consistently

The framework is organized around three broad stages: disciplined people, disciplined thought, and disciplined action. Within each stage are two key concepts: (1) Disciplined People: Level 5 Leadership and First Who, Then What; (2) Disciplined Thought: Confront the Brutal Facts and the Hedgehog Concept; (3) Disciplined Action: Culture of Discipline and the Flywheel Effect. Wrapping around the entire framework is the flywheel, which captures the gestalt of the entire process of going from good to great.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Accept that greatness requires conscious choice and sustained discipline, not luck or circumstance
- ✅ Study the evidence directly rather than relying on conventional wisdom or management fads
- ✅ Look for what the good-to-great companies did NOT do (the "dogs that did not bark") as well as what they did
- ✅ Compare good-to-great companies to carefully selected control groups to distinguish what truly matters
- ❌ Do not assume that a company in a great industry or with a charismatic CEO is destined for greatness
- ❌ Do not dismiss the possibility of transformation because current results are unremarkable

#### Keep In Mind

- The framework is built from empirical evidence, not opinion or ideology. Every finding met a rigorous standard before inclusion.
- Technology, mergers, executive compensation structure, and charismatic leadership were all found NOT to be distinguishing factors in the transformation from good to great.
- The principles apply to any type of organization—schools, churches, government agencies, nonprofits, not just corporations.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Not applicable.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Assuming a company needs a revolutionary, dramatic change program to transform.
  **Solution:** Good-to-great transformations never happened in one fell swoop. There was no single defining action, no grand program, no one killer innovation, no solitary lucky break, no miracle moment.
- **Problem:** Believing the company is in the wrong industry or lacks the right circumstances to become great.
  **Solution:** Five of the eleven good-to-great companies were in terrible industries. Greatness is not a function of circumstance.
- **Problem:** Looking for a quick fix or silver bullet.
  **Solution:** The process resembles relentlessly pushing a giant heavy flywheel in one direction, turn upon turn, building momentum until a point of breakthrough.

### Lesson 2: Develop Level 5 Leadership—Humility Plus Professional Will

#### Examples

**Example 1: Darwin Smith at Kimberly-Clark**
Darwin Smith, the company's mild-mannered in-house lawyer, became CEO in 1971. He carried no airs of self-importance, found his favorite companionship among plumbers and electricians, and spent vacations rummling around his Wisconsin farm in the cab of a backhoe. Yet when he made the most dramatic decision in the company's history—selling the mills and investing in Huggies and Kleenex—the business media called the move stupid and Wall Street analysts downgraded the stock. Smith never wavered. Twenty-five years later, Kimberly-Clark owned Scott Paper outright and beat Procter & Gamble in six of eight product categories.

**Example 2: Colman Mockler at Gillette**
During Mockler's tenure, Gillette faced three attacks that threatened to destroy the company's opportunity for greatness: two hostile takeover bids from Revlon's Ronald Perelman and a proxy battle from Coniston Partners. Had Gillette been flipped to Perelman, shareowners would have reaped an instantaneous 44 percent gain. Most executives would have capitulated. Mockler did not. He fought for the future greatness of Gillette, even though he himself would have pocketed a substantial sum on his own shares. In the end, Mockler and the board were proved right, stunningly so—a shareflipper who accepted the 44 percent premium and invested in the general market for ten years would have come out three times worse off than a shareholder who stayed with Mockler and Gillette.

**Example 3: George Cain at Abbott Laboratories**
When George Cain became CEO, Abbott sat in the bottom quartile of the pharmaceutical industry. Cain didn't have an inspiring personality, but he had something much more powerful: inspired standards. He could not stand mediocrity and was utterly intolerant of anyone who would accept the idea that good is good enough. Cain systematically rebuilt both the board and the executive team with the best people he could find, making it clear that neither family ties nor length of tenure would have anything to do with whether you held a key position. He was an eighteen-year veteran insider and a family member—the son of a previous Abbott president. Holiday gatherings were probably tense for a few years in the Cain clan. From its transition date in 1974 to 2000, Abbott created shareholder returns that beat the market 4.5 to 1.

#### Step 1: Cultivate the Dual Nature of Level 5 Leadership

Level 5 leadership is a paradoxical blend of personal humility and professional will. You must be modest and self-effacing in demeanor while simultaneously being ferociously resolved to do whatever it takes to build a great company. Self-assessment: Over the past 90 days, review all decisions with financial or reputational impact exceeding $10,000 or affecting more than 5 people. For each, note whether the primary driver was organizational benefit or personal ego, and record the percentage split if motives were mixed. Cultivate humility by immediately deflecting credit to all team members who contributed at least 5% of the effort toward the result in all public communications and by accepting full blame for failures in private. Build professional will by setting quarterly targets for a trailing 4-quarter average metric (e.g., profit per customer visit) that must meet or exceed the prior year's same-quarter performance, then reviewing progress weekly with an intolerance for mediocrity.

#### Step 2: Practice the "Window and the Mirror" Principle

Level 5 leaders look out the window to apportion credit to factors outside themselves when organizational outcomes improve (attributing success to luck, great colleagues, or external factors). When organizational outcomes decline, they look in the mirror and take full responsibility, never blaming bad luck. After a success, before attributing results to your own brilliance, list at least three external factors or people who contributed. After a failure, do not blame market conditions, competitors, or bad luck. Ask yourself what you personally could have done differently. Credit luck generously when organizational outcomes improve.

#### Step 3: Set Up Your Successors for Success

Level 5 leaders want the company to be even more successful in the next generation and are comfortable with the idea that most people won't know the roots of that success trace back to their efforts. Identify successor talent early—start grooming capable successors at least 3–5 years before you plan to leave. Choose the best person for the job, prioritizing capability over loyalty or personal chemistry. Prepare the ground by ensuring the company has strong systems, depth of management, and strategic clarity that will outlast your tenure. Retire while you are still at the top of your game, not after decline has set in.

#### Step 4: Display Compelling Modesty

Level 5 leaders are self-effacing, understated, and modest. They deflect discussion about their own contributions, deflect praise onto others, and never seek hero status or executive celebrity. When complimented, immediately credit your team, your predecessors, or good fortune. Avoid self-promotion—do not seek speaking engagements, magazine covers, or autobiographical books about your achievements. Listen more than you talk in meetings, focusing on the company and others' contributions rather than your own.

#### Step 5: Display Unwavering Resolve to Do What Must Be Done

Level 5 leaders are fanatically driven with an incurable need to produce sustained results. They will make the hardest decisions—selling core businesses, fighting raiders—because it is what the company needs. To identify the brutal facts, gather data by: (1) conducting anonymous frontline surveys with no managerial review, (2) recording customer complaints verbatim without editing, and (3) pulling financial metrics directly from the source system without rounding or management commentary; conduct "autopsies without blame" using a written template that asks only: (1) what happened, (2) what was the root cause, (3) what will we change, and (4) what did I personally do to contribute to the outcome. No names may be mentioned except the leader's own. Then make the decision decisively, communicate it relentlessly, resist second-guessing, and accept short-term pain for long-term gain.

#### Step 6: Be a Plow Horse, Not a Show Horse

Level 5 leaders display workmanlike diligence. They are consistent, steady, and focused on results rather than personal glory. Focus on the work—prioritize execution and results over image and publicity. Reject celebrity. Maintain steady performance. Measure yourself by results, not magazine covers.

#### Step 7: Prefer Inside Leaders; Avoid the Outside Savior Syndrome

Ten of eleven good-to-great CEOs came from inside the company, whereas the comparison companies tried outside CEOs six times more often and failed. Look internally first before searching externally for a CEO. Evaluate internal candidates using a structured rubric: (1) track record of delivering sustained results, (2) evidence of Level 5 traits (window-and-mirror behavior, humility, professional will), (3) understanding of the Hedgehog Concept, and (4) cultural fit. Resist board pressure to hire a "big name" outsider. Build a deep bench of potential successors so you never need an external savior.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Cultivate both humility and will—they are complementary, not opposing
- ✅ Look out the window for credit, in the mirror for blame
- ✅ Set up successors for even greater success than you achieved
- ✅ Display compelling modesty—let results speak for themselves
- ✅ Be a plow horse, not a show horse
- ✅ Prefer inside leaders who know the culture and business
- ❌ Do not mistake humility for weakness or meekness
- ❌ Do not let personal financial gain override long-term institutional success
- ❌ Do not choose weak successors or overstay your welcome
- ❌ Do not hire outside celebrity CEOs to please the board or Wall Street

#### Keep In Mind

- Level 5 is not "nice" or "weak." The humility coexists with ironclad resolve.
- There is no "Ten-Step List to Level 5." The inner development of a person to Level 5 cannot be reduced to a formula. The best way to begin developing Level 5 traits is to practice the other good-to-great disciplines.
- Potential Level 5 leaders are highly prevalent in society. Look for situations where extraordinary results exist but where no individual steps forth to claim excess credit.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Not applicable.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Mistaking humility for weakness or meekness.
  **Solution:** Level 5 leaders will sell the mills or fire their brother if that's what it takes to make the company great. Their modesty coexists with ferocious resolve.
- **Problem:** Letting personal financial gain override long-term institutional success.
  **Solution:** Before making any major decision, ask: "Am I doing this for the company's long-term success or for my personal gain?"
- **Problem:** Choosing a weak successor or overstaying your welcome.
  **Solution:** Retire while still at the top of your game. Measure your legacy by how the company performs after you leave.
- **Problem:** Believing that charisma or showmanship is required for great leadership.
  **Solution:** Alan Wurtzel's "plow horse" approach produced results six times better than Jack Welch's "show horse" at General Electric.

### Lesson 3: First Get the Right People on the Bus—Then Decide Where to Drive It

#### Examples

**Example 1: Wells Fargo's Talent-First Strategy**
When Dick Cooley became CEO of Wells Fargo, he foresaw that the banking industry would undergo wrenching change, but he did not pretend to know what form that change would take. So instead of mapping out a strategy for change, he and chairman Ernie Arbuckle focused on "injecting an endless stream of talent" directly into the veins of the company. They hired A players—people with a track record of delivering A+ effort and results in prior roles, verified through 2+ references—whenever and wherever they found them, often without any specific job in mind. "That's how you build the future," he said. "If I'm not smart enough to see the changes that are coming, they will. And they'll be flexible enough to deal with them." When deregulation hit, no bank handled those challenges better than Wells Fargo. At a time when its sector of the banking industry fell 59 percent behind the general stock market, Wells Fargo outperformed the market by over three times. Nearly every person on the Wells Fargo executive team went on to become CEO of a major company.

**Example 2: Fannie Mae Under David Maxwell**
When David Maxwell became CEO of Fannie Mae in 1981, the company was losing $1 million every single business day. Despite immense pressure to act, to do something dramatic, Maxwell focused first on getting the right people on the Fannie Mae management team. His first act was to interview all the officers. He made it absolutely clear that there would only be seats for A players who were going to put forth an A+ effort, and if you weren't up for it, you had better get off the bus. In all, fourteen of twenty-six executives left the company, replaced by some of the best, smartest, and hardest-working executives in the entire world of finance. "We had a saying, 'You can't fake it at Fannie Mae,'" said one executive. "Either you knew your stuff or you didn't, and if you didn't, you'd just blow out of here."

**Example 3: Nucor's Character-First Hiring**
Nucor built its entire system on the idea that you can teach farmers how to make steel, but you can't teach a farmer work ethic to people who don't have it in the first place. So instead of setting up mills in traditional steel towns like Pittsburgh and Gary, it located its plants in places like Crawfordsville, Indiana; Norfolk, Nebraska; and Plymouth, Utah—places full of real farmers who got to bed early, rose at dawn, and got right to work without fanfare. Nucor ejected people who did not share this work ethic, generating as high as 50 percent turnover in the first year of a plant, followed by very low turnover as the right people settled in for the long haul. To attract and keep the best workers, Nucor paid its steelworkers more than any other steel company in the world.

#### Step 1: Adopt the "First Who, Then What" Discipline

The executives who ignited the transformations from good to great did not first figure out where to drive the bus and then get people to take it there. No, they first got the right people on the bus (and the wrong people off the bus) and then figured out where to drive it. If you begin with "who" rather than "what," you can more easily adapt to a changing world. If people join the bus primarily because of where it is going, what happens if you get ten miles down the road and need to change direction? You have a problem. But if people are on the bus because of who else is on the bus, then it is much easier to change direction.

#### Step 2: Apply Packard's Law to Growth

Packard's Law: No company can grow revenues consistently faster than its ability to get enough of the right people to implement that growth and still become a great company. If your growth rate in revenues consistently outpaces your growth rate in people, you simply will not—indeed cannot—build a great company. Those who build great companies understand that the ultimate throttle on growth for any great company is not markets, or technology, or competition, or products. It is one thing above all others: the ability to get and keep enough of the right people.

#### Step 3: When in Doubt, Don't Hire—Keep Looking

One of the immutable laws of management physics is that when you know you need to make a people change, act. If you must review more than one decision per month per direct report, or if you must explain how to do the work rather than what outcome to achieve, you have made a hiring mistake. The best people don't need to be managed. Guided, taught, led—yes. But not micromanaged. When in doubt, don't hire—keep looking. A company should limit its growth based on its ability to attract enough of the right people.

#### Step 4: When You Know You Need to Make a People Change, Act

Letting the wrong people hang around is unfair to all the right people, as they inevitably find themselves compensating for the inadequacies of the wrong people. Worse, it can drive away the best people. Strong performers are intrinsically motivated by performance, and when they see their efforts impeded by carrying extra weight, they eventually become frustrated. Waiting too long before acting is equally unfair to the people who need to get off the bus. The good-to-great companies showed a bipolar pattern at the top management level: people either stayed on the bus for an extended period (typically a decade or more) or got off the bus promptly (within 12–18 months of a mismatch being identified). The good-to-great leaders did not pursue an expedient "try a lot of people and keep who works" model. Instead, they adopted the approach: "Let's take the time to make rigorous A+ selections right upfront. If we get it right, we'll do everything we can to try to keep them onboard for a long time. If we make a mistake, then we'll confront that fact."

#### Step 5: Put Your Best People on Your Biggest Opportunities, Not Your Biggest Problems

The good-to-great companies made a habit of putting their best people on their best opportunities, not their biggest problems. The comparison companies had a penchant for doing just the opposite, failing to grasp the fact that managing your problems can only make you good, whereas building your opportunities is the only way to become great. When Joe Cullman at Philip Morris identified international markets as the single best opportunity for long-term growth, he pulled his number one executive, George Weissman, off the primary domestic business and put him in charge of international. At the time, international amounted to almost nothing. Yet Weissman built international into the largest and fastest-growing part of the company, and under his stewardship, Marlboro became the best-selling cigarette in the world three years before it became number one in the United States.

#### Step 6: Rigorous, Not Ruthless, in People Decisions

To be ruthless means hacking and cutting, especially in difficult times, or wantonly firing people without any thoughtful consideration. To be rigorous means consistently applying exacting standards at all times and at all levels, especially in upper management. The Wells Fargo team concluded right upfront that the vast majority of Crocker managers would be the wrong people on the bus. Wells Fargo terminated most of the Crocker management team—1,600 Crocker managers gone on day one. But Wells Fargo also sent some of its own managers packing in cases where the Crocker managers were judged as better qualified. When it came to management, the Wells Fargo standards were ferocious and consistent. To let people languish in uncertainty for months or years, stealing precious time in their lives that they could use to move on to something else, when in the end they aren't going to make it anyway—that would be ruthless. To deal with it right upfront and let people get on with their lives—that is rigorous.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Put "who" questions before "what" questions—before vision, before strategy, before organization structure, before tactics
- ✅ Hire for character attributes (work ethic, basic intelligence, dedication) over specific knowledge or skills—these are more teachable
- ✅ Limit company growth based on ability to attract enough of the right people
- ✅ Put your best people on your biggest opportunities, not your biggest problems
- ✅ If you sell off your problems, don't sell off your best people
- ✅ Be rigorous, not ruthless, in people decisions
- ✅ Build a management team that debates vigorously in search of the best answers, yet unifies behind decisions
- ❌ Do not rely on layoffs and restructuring as a primary strategy for improving performance
- ❌ Do not follow the "genius with a thousand helpers" model—when the genius leaves, the helpers are lost
- ❌ Do not hire for specific roles instead of for shared values and character

#### Keep In Mind

- The old adage "People are your most important asset" is wrong. People are not your most important asset. The right people are.
- Whether someone is the "right person" has more to do with character traits and innate capabilities than with specific knowledge, background, or skills.
- The good-to-great management teams consist of people who debate vigorously in search of the best answers, yet who unify fully behind a decision, regardless of parochial interests.
- Nucor rejected the old adage that people are your most important asset. In a good-to-great transformation, people are not your most important asset. The right people are.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Not applicable.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Hiring the wrong people because you're under pressure to fill positions quickly.
  **Solution:** When in doubt, don't hire—keep looking. As Alan Wurtzel said, "You don't compromise. We find another way to get through until we find the right people."
- **Problem:** Keeping the wrong people on the bus out of loyalty, convenience, or fear of conflict.
  **Solution:** Two key questions can help: First, if it were a hiring decision (rather than a "should this person get off the bus?" decision), would you hire the person again? Second, if the person came to tell you that he or she is leaving to pursue an exciting new opportunity, would you feel terribly disappointed or secretly relieved?
- **Problem:** Putting your best people on your biggest problems instead of your biggest opportunities.
  **Solution:** Managing your problems can only make you good; building your opportunities is the only way to become great.
- **Problem:** Following the "genius with a thousand helpers" model.
  **Solution:** The geniuses seldom build great management teams, for the simple reason that they don't need one and often don't want one. When the genius leaves, the helpers are often lost.
- **Problem:** Using layoffs as a primary strategy.
  **Solution:** Six of the eleven good-to-great companies recorded zero layoffs from ten years before the breakthrough date all the way through 1998, and four others reported only one or two layoffs. The comparison companies used layoffs five times more frequently.

### Lesson 4: Confront the Brutal Facts—Yet Never Lose Faith

#### Examples

**Example 1: Kroger vs. A&P**
From 1959 to 1973, both Kroger and A&P lagged behind the market, with Kroger pulling just a bit ahead of A&P. After that, the two companies completely diverged. Over the next twenty-five years, Kroger generated cumulative returns ten times the market and eighty times better than A&P. Both companies had knowledge of how the world around them was changing. Yet one confronted the brutal facts of reality head-on and completely changed its entire system in response; the other stuck its head in the sand. A&P opened a new store called The Golden Key where it could experiment with new methods. Customers really liked it and A&P executives began to discover the answer to why they were losing market share. What did A&P executives do with The Golden Key? They didn't like the answers that it gave, so they closed it.

**Example 2: Pitney Bowes vs. Addressograph**
Until 1973, Pitney Bowes and Addressograph had similar revenues, profits, numbers of employees, and stock charts. Both faced the imminent reality of losing their monopolies. By 2000, Pitney Bowes had grown to over 30,000 employees and revenues in excess of $4 billion, compared to Addressograph, which had less than $100 million and only 670 employees. For the shareholder, Pitney Bowes outperformed Addressograph 3,581 to 1. The key difference: Pitney Bowes executives were blunt, neurotic, and compulsive about the company's position in the world. They conducted searing reviews where the entire management team would lay itself open to questions and challenges from salespeople who dealt directly with customers.

**Example 3: Fannie Mae's Stockdale Paradox**
When Fannie Mae began its transition in the early 1980s, almost no one gave it high odds for success. Fannie Mae had $56 billion of loans that were losing money. It received about 9 percent interest on its mortgage portfolio but had to pay up to 15 percent on the debt it issued. Most analysts responded with derision. "When you've got $56 billion worth of loans in place and underwater, talking about new programs is a joke," said one analyst. David Maxwell and his team never wavered in their faith. "It was never an issue internally," Maxwell said. "Of course, we had to stop doing a lot of stupid things, and we had to invent a completely new set of financial devices. But we never entertained the possibility that we would fail. We were going to use the calamity as an opportunity to remake Fannie Mae into a great company."

#### Step 1: Lead with Questions, Not Answers

Alan Wurtzel, who transformed Circuit City from near bankruptcy to a company that beat the market twenty-two times, began with a remarkable answer to the question of where to take the company: "I don't know." Unlike leaders such as Roy Ash of Addressograph, Wurtzel resisted the urge to walk in with "the answer." Instead, once he put the right people on the bus, he began not with answers, but with questions. "Alan was a real spark," said a board member. "He had an ability to ask questions that were just marvelous." Wurtzel stands as one of the few CEOs in a large corporation who put more questions to his board members than they put to him. He used the same approach with his executive team, constantly pushing and probing and prodding with questions.

#### Step 2: Engage in Dialogue and Debate, Not Coercion

Like Nucor's Ken Iverson, all the good-to-great leaders operated with a somewhat Socratic style. They used questions for one and only one reason: to gain understanding. They didn't use questions as manipulation or as a way to blame or put down others. Nucor established an ongoing series of general manager meetings where Iverson's role was more as a mediator. "They were chaos. We would stay there for hours, ironing out the issues, until we came to something. At times, the meetings would get so violent that people almost went across the table at each other. People yelled. They waved their arms around and pounded on tables. Faces would get red and veins bulged out." The good-to-great companies had a penchant for intense dialogue. Phrases like "loud debate," "heated discussions," and "healthy conflict" peppered the articles and interview transcripts from all the companies.

#### Step 3: Conduct Autopsies Without Blame

When Philip Morris acquired Seven-Up and had to sell it eight years later at a loss, the executives brought up the debacle on their own and discussed it openly. Instead of hiding their big, ugly mistake, they seemed to feel an almost therapeutic need to talk about it. In his book, Joe Cullman dedicates five pages to dissecting the 7UP disaster. He doesn't hold back the embarrassing truth about how flawed the decision was. No one pointed fingers to single out blame. There is only one exception: Joe Cullman, standing in front of the mirror, pointing the finger right at himself. "[It]... became apparent that this was another Joe Cullman plan that didn't work," he writes. He goes out of his way to give credit to those who were right in retrospect, naming those specific individuals who were more prescient than himself.

#### Step 4: Build "Red Flag" Mechanisms

The key to turning information into information that cannot be ignored is through red flag mechanisms—systems that force attention to critical data. Examples include: (1) giving customers full discretion to adjust invoices based on satisfaction (Graniterock's "short pay"), where the customer circles the offending item on the invoice, deducts it from the total, and sends a check for the balance; (2) requiring real-time dashboards of key metrics visible to all employees; and (3) establishing a "red flag" meeting protocol where any negative data point must be addressed within 48 hours. Bruce Woolpert instituted a particularly powerful device called short pay at his company Graniterock. Short pay gives the customer full discretionary power to decide whether and how much to pay on an invoice based upon his own subjective evaluation of how satisfied he feels with a product or service. With short pay, you absolutely have to pay attention to the data. You often don't know that a customer is upset until you lose that customer entirely. Short pay acts as an early warning system that forces you to adjust quickly, long before you would lose that customer.

#### Step 5: Embrace the Stockdale Paradox

You must maintain unwavering faith that you can and will prevail in the end, regardless of the difficulties, AND at the same time have the discipline to confront the most brutal facts of your current reality, whatever they might be. The optimists—those who said "We're going to be out by Christmas"—died of a broken heart. You must never confuse faith that you will prevail in the end with the discipline to confront the most brutal facts of your current reality.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Lead with questions, not answers
- ✅ Engage in dialogue and debate, not coercion
- ✅ Conduct autopsies without blame
- ✅ Build red flag mechanisms that turn information into information that cannot be ignored
- ✅ Maintain the Stockdale Paradox: unwavering faith AND brutal realism
- ✅ Create a culture where the truth is heard and brutal facts are confronted
- ❌ Do not hold out false hopes soon to be swept away by events
- ❌ Do not allow yourself or a charismatic leader to become the primary reality people worry about rather than reality itself
- ❌ Do not spend time and energy trying to "motivate" people—if you have the right people, they will be self-motivated

#### Keep In Mind

- When you have the right people on the bus, you will not need to spend time and energy "motivating" people. The right people will be self-motivated. The real question is how to manage in such a way as not to de-motivate people.
- One of the single most de-motivating actions you can take is to hold out false hopes, soon to be swept away by events.
- Spending time and energy trying to "motivate" people is a waste of effort. The real question is not, "How do we motivate our people?" If you have the right people, they will be self-motivated. The key is to not de-motivate them.
- Good-to-great companies faced just as much adversity as the comparison companies, but responded to that adversity differently. They hit the realities of their situation head-on. As a result, they emerged from adversity even stronger.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Not applicable.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Holding out false hopes or a compelling vision that ignores brutal facts.
  **Solution:** Confront the brutal facts first, then build your vision on reality, not fantasy. Roy Ash of Addressograph refused to confront mounting evidence that his plan was doomed, and the company filed for bankruptcy.
- **Problem:** Allowing a strong, charismatic personality to become the primary reality people worry about.
  **Solution:** Create mechanisms (like red flags) that ensure reality—not the leader's ego—remains the primary reality. If people worry more about what the leader will think than about external reality, you have a recipe for mediocrity.
- **Problem:** Using questions as manipulation rather than as a tool for genuine understanding.
  **Solution:** Ask questions with the sole purpose of gaining understanding. Don't ask "Don't you agree with me?" or "Why did you mess this up?"
- **Problem:** Conducting autopsies with blame rather than learning.
  **Solution:** When you conduct autopsies without blame, you go a long way toward creating a climate where the truth is heard. If you have the right people on the bus, you should almost never need to assign blame but need only to search for understanding and learning.
- **Problem:** Being an optimist who expects breakthrough by Christmas and dies of a broken heart when it doesn't happen.
  **Solution:** Maintain the Stockdale Paradox: absolute faith that you will prevail AND discipline to confront the brutal facts of your current reality.

### Lesson 5: Develop Your Hedgehog Concept—Simplicity Within the Three Circles

#### Examples

**Example 1: Walgreens—The Best, Most Convenient Drugstores**
Walgreens' Hedgehog Concept was simple: "The best, most convenient drugstores, with high profit per customer visit." This simple concept beat Intel, GE, Coca-Cola, and Merck over 25 years. Convenient locations (nine stores per mile in downtown San Francisco!) are expensive, but increasing profit per customer visit funded the convenience model. Profit per store would have destroyed the concept (the quickest way to increase profit per store is to decrease the number of stores).

**Example 2: Fannie Mae—Best Capital Markets Player in Mortgages**
Fannie Mae identified the subtle denominator of profit per mortgage risk level, not per mortgage (the "obvious" choice). The real driver is understanding risk of default better than anyone else, then making money selling insurance and managing the spread on that risk. Fannie Mae beat the market nearly eight times over fifteen years.

**Example 3: Abbott Laboratories—Cost-Effective Health Care**
Abbott Laboratories confronted the brutal fact that Merck had such a huge research lead that trying to be the best pharmaceutical company was like a high school football team trying to take on the Dallas Cowboys. Abbott pivoted to becoming the best at creating products that contribute to cost-effective health care (hospital nutritional products, diagnostic devices). Upjohn never confronted this reality, diversified into plastics and chemicals where it definitely could not be the best, and was acquired in 1995 with profits less than half of Abbott's.

#### Step 1: Adopt the Hedgehog Mindset—Simplify to One Big Idea

Reduce all challenges and strategies to a single, crystalline concept that unifies and guides every decision. Write down every major strategic initiative, product line, and market your organization currently pursues. For each item, ask: Does this directly relate to one unifying concept, or is it a distraction? To identify the one big idea, assemble a Council of 5–12 key people and engage in rigorous dialogue and debate over 4–8 weeks, testing each candidate concept against the three circles until one emerges that satisfies all three. Test whether this idea can guide every major decision—hiring, resource allocation, acquisitions, and exits. Ruthlessly eliminate anything that does not fit within this single concept. The Hedgehog Concept should be simple enough to state in one sentence. It should be obvious and straightforward—"so simple it sounds ridiculous to talk about it."

#### Step 2: Master the Three Circles Framework

Ensure your Hedgehog Concept sits at the intersection of all three circles:

1. **What you can be the best in the world at** (and equally important, what you cannot be the best at)
2. **What drives your economic engine**
3. **What you are deeply passionate about**

Circle 1 Assessment: Honestly evaluate what your organization has the demonstrated potential to do better than any other organization in the world, defined as having won a head-to-head competition against at least three direct competitors in the past 5 years, or having earned a top-quartile market share in a defined segment for 3+ consecutive years, based on current capabilities, track record, and resources, and explicitly identify what you absolutely cannot be the best at—even if it is your current core business. Circle 2 Assessment: Identify the single denominator (profit per x) that has the greatest and most sustainable impact on your economic engine. Circle 3 Assessment: Discover what genuinely ignites passion in you and your team, measured by at least 70% of team members rating the activity 9/10 or higher on an energy survey and the team continuing the activity voluntarily for 6+ months without manager prompting. Do not manufacture passion—uncover it. Verify that your Hedgehog Concept lies at the true intersection of all three circles.

#### Step 3: Confront the Brutal Facts About What You Can (and Cannot) Be the Best At

Ruthlessly and egolessly assess what your organization has the potential to be the best in the world at—and what it does not. Assemble a Council of 5–12 people who: (1) have deep knowledge of the organization and its environment, (2) can debate rigorously without ego, and (3) represent diverse functional perspectives (e.g., finance, operations, customer-facing). Ask the piercing question: "What can we potentially do better than any other company on Earth, and what can we not do better than any other company?" If you cannot be the best at something, ask: "Then why are we doing it at all?" Apply the Stockdale Paradox: Maintain faith that you will prevail (there must be something you can become the best at), while confronting the brutal facts of what you cannot be the best at. Prune away all activities, business lines, and investments that do not fit within your true potential.

#### Step 4: Identify Your Economic Denominator (Profit per X)

Discover the single ratio—profit per x—that has the single greatest and most sustainable impact on your economic engine. Ask: "If you could pick one and only one ratio—profit per x—to systematically increase over time, what x would have the greatest and most sustainable impact on your economic engine?" List potential x variables (per employee, per store, per customer, per unit, per risk, per transaction, per location, per doctor, per bed, per student). Test each candidate against your Hedgehog Concept. Challenge your team to push for a single denominator rather than three or four. Once identified, make this denominator the primary economic metric that guides resource allocation, pricing, location, and process decisions. Do not accept the first denominator that comes to mind; challenge your team to identify the true economic driver by testing each candidate against the three circles.

#### Step 5: Discover Passion—Do Not Manufacture It

Identify what genuinely ignites passion in you and your people. Only do those things that you can get passionate about. Honestly assess what activities, products, or missions genuinely excite you and your team—not what you think should excite you. Ask: "What would I do even if I weren't paid for it?" If you cannot get passionate about what you are currently doing, recognize this as a signal to reconsider your direction—not as a problem to be solved with motivational programs. Build your Hedgehog Concept around the intersection of genuine passion. Hire and promote people who share the passion.

#### Step 6: Triumph of Understanding Over Bravado

Base your goals and strategies on deep understanding, not on ego, hype, or mindless bravado. Accept that getting the Hedgehog Concept takes time and iterative learning—it cannot be forced through off-site retreats. Commit to an iterative process: Engage in vigorous dialogue and debate, infused with brutal facts, guided by the three circles, and learn from results. Build a standing Council of 5–12 key people who can argue and debate in search of understanding, meeting monthly to iteratively develop and refine your Hedgehog Concept. When your Hedgehog Concept finally emerges, expect it to arrive without fanfare. Set goals and strategies based on understanding, not bravado. Increase the number of times you go through the full cycle of understanding in a given period to accelerate the process.

#### Step 7: Build and Use the Council

Assemble a standing Council of the right people (5 to 12) to engage in rigorous dialogue and debate, guided by the three circles, to iteratively develop and refine your Hedgehog Concept. Select members who: (1) have demonstrated ability to argue ideas without ego, assessed through documented past behavior in meetings (specifically whether the person has changed their mind when presented with better evidence, avoided dominating discussions, and supported positions they originally opposed when the team decided otherwise), (2) retain the respect of all other Council members, and (3) represent at least three distinct perspectives (e.g., finance, operations, customer-facing). Each member must have deep knowledge of some aspect of the organization or environment. Make the Council a standing body, not an ad hoc committee. Meet monthly for 2–3 hours. Do NOT seek consensus—the responsibility for the final decision remains with the leading executive. Keep the Council informal—do not list it on any formal organization chart. Name the Council something innocuous and benign (e.g., "Strategic Insights Group" or "The Tuesday Forum"). Use the Council to repeatedly go through the cycle: Ask the right questions, engage in vigorous debate, make decisions, autopsy the results, and learn.

#### Step 8: Avoid the Growth-at-Any-Price Trap

Reject mindless growth without a Hedgehog Concept. When you hear "growth" as a strategy, ask: "Growth in what? Toward what end?" Evaluate every acquisition, expansion, or new initiative against your three circles: Does it fit within what you can be the best at, what drives your economic engine, and what you are passionate about? If an opportunity does not fit within your three circles, reject it—even if it promises rapid growth. Prune away business lines that do not fit, even if they are profitable. Trust that if you have the right Hedgehog Concept and make decisions consistently with it, growth will come as a byproduct. If your main problem becomes how not to grow too fast, you have succeeded.

#### Step 9: Sustain Discipline Over Time

Once you have your Hedgehog Concept, apply it consistently and relentlessly. Communicate it constantly and consistently across the organization. Align every major decision—hiring, promotions, resource allocation, acquisitions, and exits—with the three circles. Do not abandon the concept when leadership changes or when short-term pressures mount. Conduct a quarterly audit: for each significant active initiative consuming meaningful resources or executive attention, ask "Does this fit within our three circles?" If more than 20% of initiatives have drifted outside the three circles, convene the Council to reallocate resources according to a defined process. Hasbro became the best-performing comparison company in the study because it understood all three circles, but it lost the discipline to stay within them after the unexpected death of its CEO. The only way to remain great is to keep applying the fundamental principles that made you great.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Reduce all strategies to a single, crystalline Hedgehog Concept
- ✅ All three circles are required—missing any one produces good results, not great ones
- ✅ Confront the brutal facts about what you can and cannot be the best at
- ✅ Identify a single economic denominator and make it the primary metric
- ✅ Discover genuine passion—don't manufacture it
- ✅ Triumph of understanding over bravado
- ✅ Build a Council to iteratively develop your Hedgehog Concept
- ✅ Reject growth-at-any-price
- ✅ Sustain discipline over time
- ❌ Do not confuse core business with Hedgehog Concept—just because something is your core business doesn't mean you can be the best in the world at it
- ❌ Do not confuse core competence with best-in-world potential
- ❌ Do not ignore what you cannot be the best at—ego often blinds organizations to their limitations
- ❌ Do not set goals without understanding—"We want to be the best" is not a Hedgehog Concept
- ❌ Do not seek consensus in the Council—consensus decisions are often at odds with intelligent decisions

#### Keep In Mind

- "Growth!" is NOT a Hedgehog Concept. If you have the right Hedgehog Concept, your main problem will not be how to grow, but how not to grow too fast.
- The "best in the world" standard goes far beyond core competence. The distinction between "good" and "best" is absolute. Doing what you are good at will only make you good; focusing solely on what you can potentially do better than any other organization is the only path to greatness.
- The Hedgehog Concept is an understanding, not a goal, strategy, or intention to be the best.
- You absolutely do not need to be in a great industry to produce sustained great results. Only one of the good-to-great companies had the benefit of being in a top 10 percent industry; five were in bad to terrible industries.
- Getting a Hedgehog Concept is an iterative process, not an event. It took about four years on average for the good-to-great companies to clarify their concepts.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Not applicable.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Being a fox—scattered, diffused, moving on many levels, never integrating thinking into one overall vision.
  **Solution:** Simplify to one big idea. Hedgehogs win because they simplify and relentlessly execute one big idea.
- **Problem:** Confusing core business with Hedgehog Concept.
  **Solution:** Just because something is your core business—just because you've been doing it for years—does not mean you can be the best in the world at it.
- **Problem:** The curse of competence—staying in a career or business where you are competent but can never be the best.
  **Solution:** Ruthlessly confront the question: "What can we potentially be the best in the world at?" If the answer is not your current business, pivot.
- **Problem:** Growth for growth's sake.
  **Solution:** Eckerd and Great Western both had growth but not sustained, profitable growth. They eventually failed or lost independence because they lacked a Hedgehog Concept.
- **Problem:** Trying to jump straight to a Hedgehog Concept through an off-site retreat.
  **Solution:** Insight doesn't happen that way. It took Einstein ten years to develop the theory of special relativity. Commit to an iterative process.
- **Problem:** Setting goals from bravado rather than understanding.
  **Solution:** Comparison companies produced "tiresome, irritating blasts of mindless bravado." When you get it right, there is no need for hype—the quiet truth speaks for itself.

### Lesson 6: Build a Culture of Discipline—Freedom and Responsibility Within a Framework

#### Examples

**Example 1: Abbott Laboratories—Disciplined Entrepreneurship**
Abbott Laboratories reduced its administrative costs as a percentage of sales to the lowest in the industry (by a significant margin) and at the same time became a new product innovation machine like 3M, deriving up to 65 percent of revenues from new products introduced in the previous four years. Abbott recruited entrepreneurial leaders and gave them freedom to determine the best path to achieving their objectives. At the same time, individuals had to commit fully to the Abbott system and were held rigorously accountable for their objectives. They had freedom, but freedom within a framework. "Abbott developed a very disciplined organization, but not in a linear way of thinking," said George Rathmann. "We used financial discipline as a way to provide resources for the really creative work."

**Example 2: Wells Fargo vs. Bank of America**
Carl Reichardt of Wells Fargo never doubted that Wells Fargo could emerge from bank deregulation as a stronger company, not a weaker one. He saw that the key to becoming a great company rested not with brilliant new strategies but with the sheer determination to rip a hundred years of banker mentality out of the system. Reichardt set a clear tone at the top: We're not going to ask everyone else to suffer while we sit on high. We will start by rinsing our own cottage cheese, right here in the executive suite. He froze executive salaries for two years (despite the fact that Wells Fargo was enjoying some of the most profitable years in its history). He shut the executive dining room and replaced it with a college dorm food-service caterer. He closed the executive elevator, sold the corporate jets, and banned green plants from the executive suite as too expensive to water. He removed free coffee from the executive suite. He eliminated Christmas trees for management. Across the street at Bank of America, executives also faced deregulation and recognized the need to eliminate waste. However, unlike Wells Fargo, BofA executives didn't have the discipline to rinse their own cottage cheese. They preserved their posh executive kingdom in its imposing tower in downtown San Francisco. After losing $1.8 billion across three years in the mid-1980s, BofA eventually made the necessary changes, but even in the darkest days, BofA could not bring itself to get rid of the perks that shielded its executives from the real world.

**Example 3: Nucor—Rinsing Your Cottage Cheese**
Nucor grew into a $3.5 billion Fortune 500 company with only four layers of management and a corporate headquarters staff of fewer than twenty-five people—crammed into a rented office the size of a small dental practice. Cheap veneer furniture adorned the lobby, which itself was not much larger than a closet. Instead of a corporate dining room, executives hosted visiting dignitaries at Phil's Diner, a strip mall sandwich shop across the street. All 7,000 employees' names appeared in the annual report, not just officers' and executives'. Everyone except safety supervisors and visitors wore the same color hard hats. When Nucor had a highly profitable year, everyone in the company would have a very profitable year. In the 1982 recession, worker pay went down 25 percent, officer pay went down 60 percent, and the CEO's pay went down 75 percent. From 1966 to 1999, Nucor posted thirty-four consecutive years of positive profitability, while Bethlehem lost money twelve times and its cumulative profitability added up to less than zero.

#### Step 1: Build a Culture of Freedom and Responsibility Within a Framework

Build a culture of freedom and responsibility within a framework by: (1) establishing explicit, non-negotiable constraints specific to your organization (e.g., safety standards, debt-to-equity ratios, code-of-conduct boundaries), (2) hiring self-disciplined people who don't need to be managed, and (3) delegating decision-making authority within those constraints to frontline employees. Like an airline pilot who follows a preflight checklist and air traffic control protocols but makes final decisions in a storm, employees should know the boundaries of their autonomy and have the responsibility to act within them. The good-to-great companies built a consistent system with clear constraints, but they also gave people freedom and responsibility within the framework of that system. They hired self-disciplined people who didn't need to be managed, and then managed the system, not the people.

#### Step 2: Start a "Stop Doing" List

The good-to-great companies made as much use of "stop doing" lists as "to do" lists. When Darwin Smith became CEO of Kimberly-Clark, he saw that playing the annual forecast game with Wall Street focused people too much on the short term, so he just stopped doing it. "On balance, I see no net advantage to our stockholders when we annually forecast future earnings," said Smith. "We will not do it." He saw "title creep" as a sign of class-consciousness and bureaucratic layering, so he simply unplugged titles. He saw increasing layers as the natural result of empire building. So he simply unplugged a huge stack of layers with a simple elegant mechanism: If you cannot justify to your peers the need for the number of direct reports required to fulfill your responsibilities, then reduce your direct reports to zero. The principle is: each layer must add clear value, not create bureaucracy. The purpose of budgeting in a good-to-great company is not to decide how much each activity gets, but about determining which activities best support the Hedgehog Concept and should be fully strengthened and which should be eliminated entirely.

#### Step 3: Practice Fanatical Adherence to the Hedgehog Concept

The single most important form of discipline for sustained results is fanatical adherence to the Hedgehog Concept and the willingness to shun opportunities that fall outside the three circles. The good-to-great companies institutionalized the discipline of "stop doing" through a budget mechanism that allocates resources based on alignment with the Hedgehog Concept: fully fund activities that fit the three circles, partially fund transitional activities, and completely eliminate activities that do not fit. Kimberly-Clark didn't just reallocate resources from the paper business to the consumer business. It completely eliminated the paper business, sold the mills, and invested all the money into the emerging consumer business. The good-to-great companies at their best followed a simple mantra: "Anything that does not fit with our Hedgehog Concept, we will not do. We will not launch unrelated businesses. We will not make unrelated acquisitions. We will not do unrelated joint ventures. If it doesn't fit, we don't do it. Period."

#### Step 4: Rinse Your Own Cottage Cheese First

The "rinsing your cottage cheese" factor comes from Dave Scott, the six-time Hawaii Ironman Triathlon winner, who literally rinsed his cottage cheese to get the extra fat off, even though he burned at least 5,000 calories a day in training. Dave Scott didn't have a weight problem! Yet he believed that a low-fat, high-carbohydrate diet would give him an extra edge. The point is that rinsing his cottage cheese was simply one more small step that he believed would make him just that much better. In the same way, the good-to-great companies became like Dave Scott. Much of the answer to the question of "good to great" lies in the discipline to do whatever it takes to become the best within carefully selected arenas and then to seek continual improvement from there.

#### Step 5: Distinguish Culture of Discipline from Tyrannical Disciplinarian

Do not confuse a culture of discipline with a tyrant who disciplines—they are very different concepts, one highly functional, the other highly dysfunctional. Savior CEOs who personally discipline through sheer force of personality usually fail to produce sustained results. Consider Ray MacDonald at Burroughs. He got things done through sheer force of personality using a form of pressure that came to be known as "The MacDonald Vise." He produced remarkable results during his reign—every dollar invested in 1964, the year he became president, and taken out at the end of 1977, produced returns 6.6 times better than the general market. However, the company had no culture of discipline to endure beyond him. After he retired, his helper minions were frozen by indecision, leaving the company "with an inability to do anything." Burroughs then began a long slide, with cumulative returns falling 93 percent below the market. Where the good-to-great companies had Level 5 leaders who built an enduring culture of discipline, the unsustained comparisons had Level 4 leaders who personally disciplined the organization through sheer force.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Build a culture around the idea of freedom and responsibility within a framework
- ✅ Fill the culture with self-disciplined people who are willing to go to extreme lengths to fulfill their responsibilities
- ✅ Adhere with great consistency to the Hedgehog Concept, exercising an almost religious focus on the intersection of the three circles
- ✅ Create a "stop doing" list and systematically unplug anything extraneous
- ✅ Rinse your own cottage cheese—start at the top
- ✅ Manage the system, not the people
- ✅ Budget to determine which activities to fully fund and which to eliminate entirely
- ❌ Do not confuse a culture of discipline with a tyrannical disciplinarian
- ❌ Do not build bureaucracy to compensate for incompetence—get the right people instead
- ❌ Do not rely on layoffs and restructuring as a primary strategy
- ❌ Do not allow the company to die of indigestion from too much opportunity rather than starvation from too little

#### Keep In Mind

- All companies have a culture, some companies have discipline, but few companies have a culture of discipline.
- When you have disciplined people, you don't need hierarchy. When you have disciplined thought, you don't need bureaucracy. When you have disciplined action, you don't need excessive controls.
- When you combine a culture of discipline with an ethic of entrepreneurship, you get the magical alchemy of great performance.
- The more an organization has the discipline to stay within its three circles, with almost religious consistency, the more it will have attractive opportunities for growth. A great company is much more likely to die of indigestion from too much opportunity than starvation from too little.
- The fact that something is a "once-in-a-lifetime opportunity" is irrelevant if it doesn't fit within the three circles. A great company will have many once-in-a-lifetime opportunities.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Not applicable.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Building bureaucracy to compensate for having the wrong people on the bus.
  **Solution:** If you get the right people on the bus, and the wrong people off, you don't need stultifying bureaucracy. Bureaucratic cultures arise to compensate for incompetence and lack of discipline, which arise from having the wrong people on the bus in the first place.
- **Problem:** Being a tyrannical disciplinarian instead of building a culture of discipline.
  **Solution:** The difference is crucial. A tyrannical disciplinarian produces results only while present. A culture of discipline endures beyond any single leader.
- **Problem:** Confusing growth with disciplined expansion.
  **Solution:** Over two-thirds of comparison companies displayed an obsession with growth without the benefit of a Hedgehog Concept. Not one good-to-great company focused obsessively on growth, yet they created sustained, profitable growth far greater than the comparison companies.
- **Problem:** Failing to "rinse your own cottage cheese" at the top.
  **Solution:** Discipline in a good-to-great company applies first at the top, focused on those who hold the largest burden of responsibility.
- **Problem:** Trying to discipline the wrong people into the right behaviors.
  **Solution:** The transition begins not by trying to discipline the wrong people into the right behaviors, but by getting self-disciplined people on the bus in the first place. Disciplined action without self-disciplined people is impossible to sustain, and disciplined action without disciplined thought is a recipe for disaster.

### Lesson 7: Use Technology as an Accelerator, Not a Creator, of Momentum

#### Examples

**Example 1: Walgreens—Crawl, Walk, Run**
At the high point of the Internet frenzy, drugstore.com issued its challenge to Walgreens. Walgreens' stock suffered from the invasion of the dotcoms, losing over 40 percent of its price in the months leading up to the drugstore.com public offering. Analysts downgraded Walgreens' stock, and nearly $15 billion in market value evaporated. Walgreens' response? "We're a crawl, walk, run company," Dan Jorndt told Forbes in describing his deliberate, methodical approach to the Internet. Instead of reacting like Chicken Little, Walgreens executives decided to pause and reflect. They decided to use their brains. They decided to think! Slow at first (crawl), Walgreens began experimenting with a Website while engaging in intense internal dialogue and debate about its implications, within the context of its own peculiar Hedgehog Concept. Then a little faster (walk), Walgreens began to find ways to tie the Internet directly to its sophisticated inventory-and-distribution model and ultimately its convenience concept. Then, finally (run!), Walgreens bet big, launching an Internet site as sophisticated and well designed as most pure dot-coms. Precisely one year after the Forbes article, Walgreens had figured out how to harness the Internet to accelerate momentum. From its low point in 1999, Walgreens' stock price nearly doubled within a year. And what of drugstore.com? Continuing to accumulate massive losses, it announced a layoff to conserve cash. At its high point, little more than a year earlier, drugstore.com traded at a price twenty-six times higher than at the time of this writing.

**Example 2: Nucor—Technology as a Secondary Factor**
Nucor became widely known as one of the most aggressive pioneers in the application of mini-mill steel manufacturing, with dozens of articles and two books that celebrated its bold investments in continuous thin slab casting and electric arc furnaces. But when Ken Iverson, CEO of Nucor during its transition, was asked to name the top five factors in the shift from good to great, where do you think he put technology? First? No. Second? No. Third? Nope. Fourth? Not even. Fifth? Sorry, but no. "The primary factors," said Ken Iverson, "were the consistency of the company, and our ability to project its philosophies throughout the whole organization, enabled by our lack of layers and bureaucracy." One Nucor executive summed up: "Twenty percent of our success is the new technology that we embrace... but eighty percent of our success is in the culture of our company."

**Example 3: Kroger—Barcode Scanners as Economic Accelerators**
Kroger was an early pioneer in the application of barcode scanners, which helped it accelerate past A&P by linking frontline purchases to backroom inventory management. Kroger modernized and turned over all its stores, improved the customer's shopping experience, radically expanded the variety of products offered, and paid off billions of dollars of debt. Kroger's use of scanning technology to take hundreds of millions of crisp and crinkly dollar bills out of the warehouse and put them to better use became a key element in its ability to pull off its magic trick.

#### Step 1: Determine If the Technology Fits Your Hedgehog Concept

The key question about any technology is: Does the technology fit directly with your Hedgehog Concept? A technology fits directly if it can be linked to one of the three circles with a written one-sentence causal statement; for example, "Barcode scanners [technology] reduce inventory cost per store [economic denominator] by improving stock accuracy." If yes, then you need to become a pioneer in the application of that technology. If no, then ask: Do you need this technology at all? If yes, then all you need is parity. (You don't necessarily need the world's most advanced phone system to be a great company.) If no, then the technology is irrelevant, and you can ignore it. When used right—when linked to a simple, clear, and coherent concept rooted in deep understanding—technology is an essential driver in accelerating forward momentum. But when used wrong—when grasped as an easy solution, without deep understanding of how it links to a clear and coherent concept—technology simply accelerates your own self-created demise.

#### Step 2: Adopt the "Crawl, Walk, Run" Approach

Good-to-great companies used a "crawl, walk, run" approach even during times of rapid and radical technological change. Walgreens didn't adopt all of its advanced technology just for the sake of advanced technology or in fearful reaction to falling behind. No, it used technology as a tool to accelerate momentum after hitting breakthrough, and tied technology directly to its Hedgehog Concept of convenient drugstores increasing profit per customer visit. Technology was a key part of what Fannie Mae leaders called "the second wind" of the transformation and acted as an accelerating factor. The pioneering application of technology usually came late in the transition and never at the start.

#### Step 3: Avoid the Technology Trap

Thoughtless reliance on technology is a liability, not an asset. The evidence from the study does not support the idea that technological change plays the principal role in the decline of once-great companies. Certainly, technology is important—you can't remain a laggard and hope to be great. But technology by itself is never a primary cause of either greatness or decline. You could have taken the exact same leading-edge technologies pioneered at the good-to-great companies and handed them to their direct comparisons for free, and the comparisons still would have failed to produce anywhere near the same results. Like the Daytona 500, the primary variable in winning is not the car, but the driver and his team. Not that the car is unimportant, but it is secondary.

#### Step 4: Respond to Technology with Thoughtfulness, Not Fear

Those who built the good-to-great companies weren't motivated by fear. They weren't driven by fear of what they didn't understand. They weren't driven by fear of looking like a chump. They weren't driven by fear of watching others hit it big while they didn't. They weren't driven by the fear of being hammered by the competition. Those who turn good into great are motivated by a deep creative urge and an inner compulsion for sheer unadulterated excellence for its own sake. Those who build and perpetuate mediocrity, in contrast, are motivated more by the fear of being left behind. How a company reacts to technological change is a good indicator of its inner drive for greatness versus mediocrity. Great companies respond with thoughtfulness and creativity, driven by a compulsion to turn unrealized potential into results; mediocre companies react and lurch about, motivated by fear of being left behind.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Use technology as an accelerator of momentum, not a creator of it
- ✅ Ask: Does the technology fit directly with your Hedgehog Concept?
- ✅ If yes, become a pioneer in the application of that technology
- ✅ If no, settle for parity or ignore it entirely
- ✅ Adopt the "crawl, walk, run" approach
- ✅ Respond to technology with thoughtfulness and creativity, not fear
- ❌ Do not begin your transformation with pioneering technology
- ❌ Do not grasp technology as an easy solution without deep understanding
- ❌ Do not lurch about in fearful, frantic reaction to technological change
- ❌ Do not believe that technology alone can turn a good enterprise into a great one

#### Keep In Mind

- No technology—not computers, not telecommunications, not robotics, not the Internet—can by itself ignite a shift from good to great.
- No technology can make you Level 5. No technology can turn the wrong people into the right people. No technology can instill the discipline to confront brutal facts of reality. No technology can supplant the need for deep understanding of the three circles.
- The good-to-great companies used technology as just one more way in which they remained disciplined within the frame of their Hedgehog Concept.
- Throughout business history, early technology pioneers rarely prevail in the end. IBM did not have the early lead in computers. Boeing did not pioneer the commercial jet. The first portable computers came from now-dead companies.
- The United States had the most technologically advanced fighting force the world has ever known in Vietnam and still did not succeed. Technology cannot turn a good enterprise into a great one, nor by itself prevent disaster.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Not applicable.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Reacting to technology with fear and frantic lurching.
  **Solution:** Think before acting. Ask how the technology connects to your Hedgehog Concept. If it doesn't fit, ignore it.
- **Problem:** Using technology as the primary means of igniting a transformation.
  **Solution:** Technology cannot cause a transformation. It can only accelerate a transformation that is already underway.
- **Problem:** Being an early technology pioneer and assuming that guarantees success.
  **Solution:** VisiCalc was the first major personal computer spreadsheet. Where is VisiCalc today? The first portable computers came from now-dead companies. Technology alone cannot make you great.
- **Problem:** Jumping on every technology bandwagon.
  **Solution:** Good-to-great organizations avoid technology fads and bandwagons, yet they become pioneers in the application of carefully selected technologies.
- **Problem:** Believing that you need the world's most advanced technology to be great.
  **Solution:** You don't necessarily need the world's most advanced phone system to be a great company. If the technology doesn't fit squarely within your three circles, ignore all the hype and fear and just go about your business.

### Lesson 8: Apply the Flywheel Effect—Build Momentum Turn by Turn

#### Examples

**Example 1: Circuit City—Over a Decade in the Making**
On August 27, 1984, Forbes magazine published the first national-level profile ever on Circuit City. It wasn't that big of an article, just two pages, and it questioned whether Circuit City's recent growth could continue. This particular overnight success story, however, had been more than a decade in the making. Alan Wurtzel had inherited CEO responsibility from his father in 1973, with the firm close to bankruptcy. First, he rebuilt his executive team and undertook an objective look at the brutal facts of reality. In 1974, Wurtzel and his team began to experiment with a warehouse showroom style of retailing and built a prototype in Richmond, Virginia. In 1976, the company began to experiment with selling consumer electronics. In 1977, it transformed the concept into the first-ever Circuit City store. In 1982—with nine years of accumulated turns on the flywheel—Wurtzel and his team committed fully to the concept of the Circuit City superstore. From 1982 to 1999, Circuit City generated cumulative stock returns twenty-two times better than the market.

**Example 2: Nucor—Ten Years of Invisible Buildup**
Ken Iverson and Sam Siegel began turning the Nucor flywheel in 1965. For ten years, no one paid any attention, certainly not the financial press or the other steel companies. If you had asked executives at Bethlehem Steel or U.S. Steel about "The Nucor Threat" in 1970, they would have laughed, if they even recognized the company name at all. By 1975, the year of its transition point on the stock chart, Nucor had already built its third mini-mill, long established its unique culture of productivity, and was well on its way to becoming the most profitable steel company in America. Yet the first major article in Business Week did not appear until 1978, thirteen years after the start of the transition.

**Example 3: UCLA Bruins Under John Wooden**
Coach John Wooden coached the Bruins for fifteen years, from 1948 to 1963, working in relative obscurity before winning his first NCAA Championship in 1964. Year by year, Coach Wooden built the underlying foundations, developing a recruiting system, implementing a consistent philosophy, and refining the full-court-press style of play. No one paid too much attention to the quiet, soft-spoken coach and his team until—wham!—they hit breakthrough and systematically crushed every serious competitor for more than a decade. From 1964 to 1975, the Bruins won ten NCAA Championships in twelve years, at one point assembling a sixty-one-game winning streak.

#### Step 1: Understand the Flywheel Metaphor

Picture a huge, heavy flywheel—a massive metal disk mounted horizontally on an axle, about 30 feet in diameter, 2 feet thick, and weighing about 5,000 pounds. Your task is to get the flywheel rotating on the axle as fast and long as possible. Pushing with great effort, you get the flywheel to inch forward, moving almost imperceptibly at first. You keep pushing and, after two or three hours of persistent effort, you get the flywheel to complete one entire turn. You keep pushing, and the flywheel begins to move a bit faster. Three turns, four, five, six—the flywheel builds up speed. Twenty, thirty, fifty, a hundred. Then, at some point—breakthrough! The momentum of the thing kicks in in your favor, hurling the flywheel forward, turn after turn. You're pushing no harder than during the first rotation, but the flywheel goes faster and faster. Each turn of the flywheel builds upon work done earlier, compounding your investment of effort.

#### Step 2: Push Consistently in One Direction

Good-to-great transformations never happened in one fell swoop. There was no single defining action, no grand program, no one killer innovation, no solitary lucky break, no miracle moment. Good to great comes about by a cumulative process—step by step, action by action, decision by decision, turn by turn of the flywheel—that adds up to sustained and spectacular results. The good-to-great companies had no name for their transformations. There was no launch event, no tagline, no programmatic feel whatsoever. Some executives said that they weren't even aware that a major transformation was under way until they were well into it.

#### Step 3: Follow the Buildup-Breakthrough Pattern

Every good-to-great transformation followed the same basic pattern—accumulating momentum, turn by turn of the flywheel—until buildup transformed into breakthrough. At Circuit City, the buildup stage lasted nine years. At Nucor, ten years. At Gillette, it took only five years. At Fannie Mae, only three years. At Pitney Bowes, about two years. But no matter how short or long it took, every good-to-great transformation followed the same basic pattern. Following the buildup-breakthrough flywheel model is not just a luxury of circumstance. The good-to-great companies followed this model no matter how dire the short-term circumstances—deregulation in the case of Wells Fargo, looming bankruptcy in the cases of Nucor and Circuit City, potential takeover threats in the cases of Gillette and Kroger, or million-dollar-a-day losses in the case of Fannie Mae.

#### Step 4: Manage Wall Street with the Flywheel

All the good-to-great companies effectively managed Wall Street during their buildup-breakthrough years by practicing under-promising and over-delivering, using mechanisms like Abbott Laboratories' "Blue Plans" (setting internal targets 25–30% above public guidance and channeling the difference into future investments), and refusing to play the annual earnings forecast game when it created short-term pressure. They simply focused on accumulating results, often practicing the time-honored discipline of under-promising and overdelivering. Abbott Laboratories used a brilliant mechanism called the Blue Plans. Each year, Abbott would tell Wall Street analysts that it expected to grow earnings a specified amount—for example, 15 percent. At the same time, it would set an internal goal of a much higher growth rate—for example, 25 percent, or even 30 percent. Toward the end of the year, Abbott would pick a number that exceeded analyst expectations but that fell short of its actual growth. It would then take the difference between the "make the analysts happy" growth and the actual growth and channel those funds into the Blue Plans. It was a brilliant mechanism for managing short-term pressures while systematically investing in the future.

#### Step 5: Harness the Flywheel Effect for Alignment

When you build consistent, visible momentum through disciplined action, you don't need to fervently communicate goals or "create alignment." People will naturally commit when they see tangible progress. Focus on demonstrating results by publishing a flywheel dashboard every 30 days showing: (1) actions completed (number of discrete initiatives executed that align with the Hedgehog Concept), (2) momentum gained (metric improvement vs. prior period on the economic denominator), and (3) progress toward breakthrough (current value vs. target value on the primary outcome metric). People can extrapolate from the momentum for themselves: "Hey, if we just keep doing this, look at where we can go!" As people decide among themselves to turn the fact of potential into the fact of results, the goal almost sets itself. The good-to-great companies spent essentially no energy trying to "create alignment," "motivate the troops," or "manage change." Under the right conditions, the problems of commitment, alignment, motivation, and change just melt away. They largely take care of themselves. When the right people see a simple plan born of confronting the brutal facts—a plan developed from understanding, not bravado—they are likely to say, "That'll work. Count me in."

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Push on the flywheel consistently in one direction
- ✅ Follow the buildup-breakthrough pattern—don't skip buildup
- ✅ Understand that there is no single defining action, no miracle moment
- ✅ Manage Wall Street with under-promising and overdelivering
- ✅ Let the flywheel do the talking for alignment
- ✅ Start before anyone is paying attention
- ✅ Celebrate small, consistent turns of the flywheel
- ❌ Do not seek a single "killer app" or revolutionary idea instead of building consistent momentum
- ❌ Do not expect immediate results and declare failure too early
- ❌ Do not launch dramatic change programs with great fanfare
- ❌ Do not compare yourself to organizations that appear to have "exploded" onto the scene
- ❌ Do not let new leaders stop a spinning flywheel and throw it in a new direction

#### Keep In Mind

- The flywheel effect applies not only to outside investors but also to internal groups such as employees, board members, and key customers.
- Media accounts skew our perception of how transformations happen, making it seem as if companies jumped right to breakthrough as some sort of overnight metamorphosis. From the inside, they feel completely different, more like an organic development process.
- Those inside the good-to-great companies were often unaware of the magnitude of their transformation at the time; only later, in retrospect, did it become clear. They had no name, tagline, launch event, or program to signify what they were doing.
- The short-term pressures of Wall Street were not inconsistent with following this model. The flywheel effect is not in conflict with these pressures. Indeed, it is the key to managing them.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Not applicable.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Falling into the Doom Loop—lurching back and forth between strategies.
  **Solution:** Push the flywheel in a consistent direction. Consider Warner-Lambert, which from 1979 through 1998 underwent three major restructurings—one per CEO—hacking away 20,000 people in search of quick breakthrough results. Time and again, the company would attain a burst of results, then slacken, never attaining the sustained momentum of a buildup-breakthrough flywheel.
- **Problem:** Using acquisitions to jump to breakthrough rather than accelerate an already spinning flywheel.
  **Solution:** The good-to-great companies used large acquisitions after breakthrough, to accelerate momentum in an already fast-spinning flywheel. The comparison companies frequently tried to jump right to breakthrough via an acquisition or merger. It never worked. Two big mediocrities joined together never make one great company.
- **Problem:** New leaders stopping a spinning flywheel and throwing it in a new direction.
  **Solution:** Consider Harris Corporation. From the end of 1973 to the end of 1978, Harris beat the market by more than five times. Then the flywheel came to a grinding halt when Joseph Boyd became CEO and threw a giant wrench into the flywheel by divesting the printing business. From the end of 1978 to the end of 1983, Harris fell 39 percent behind the market, and by 1988 it had fallen over 70 percent behind.
- **Problem:** Expecting immediate breakthrough and declaring failure when it doesn't come.
  **Solution:** It might not happen today, or tomorrow, or next week. It might not even happen next year. But it will happen if you keep pushing in a consistent direction.
- **Problem:** Giving up during the buildup phase because results aren't visible yet.
  **Solution:** John Wooden coached the UCLA Bruins for fifteen years before his first NCAA Championship. Year by year, he built the underlying foundations. No one paid too much attention until—wham!—they hit breakthrough.

### Lesson 9: Integrate Good to Great with Built to Last—Create Enduring Greatness

#### Examples

**Example 1: Good to Great as Prequel to Built to Last**
Good to Great is not a sequel to Built to Last; it is the prequel. This book is about how to turn a good organization into one that produces sustained great results. Built to Last is about how you take a company with great results and turn it into an enduring great company of iconic stature. The Built to Last companies (Procter & Gamble, American Express, Johnson & Johnson, GE, Wal-Mart, HP) actually followed the Good to Great framework during their formative years, but as entrepreneurs building from scratch rather than as CEOs transforming established firms.

**Example 2: Wal-Mart—Flywheel for Decades**
Sam Walton opened a single dime store in 1945. His second store didn't come until seven years later. It took a quarter of a century to grow from that single dime store to 38 Wal-Marts. From 1970 to 2000, Wal-Mart exploded to over 3,000 stores and $150 billion in revenues—but only after decades of flywheel building. Walton himself noted: "Like most overnight successes, it was about twenty years in the making."

**Example 3: Hewlett-Packard—Clock Building from Day One**
Bill Hewlett and David Packard spent months "stumbling around" trying to find what to manufacture—yacht transmitters, air-conditioning controls, bowling alley sensors, electronic shock jiggle machines. None of it mattered much in the early days; what mattered was building the company with the right people. HP scaled up by hiring extraordinary people even when they had no specific role in mind. Bill Hewlett said he was most proud of "having helped create a company that by virtue of its values, practices, and success has had a tremendous impact on the way companies are managed around the world." The "HP Way" distinguished the company more than any of its products.

**Example 4: Merck—Profits Follow from Purpose**
Merck developed and distributed a drug that cured river blindness, giving it away free to millions of poor people in remote places. Despite this, Merck was highly profitable—outperforming the market ten-to-one from 1946 to 2000. George Merck II said: "Medicine is for the patient. It is not for the profits. The profits follow, and if we have remembered that, they have never failed to appear. The better we have remembered it, the larger they have been."

#### Step 1: Apply Good to Great First, Then Apply Built to Last

A company must first achieve sustained great results using the Good to Great framework before it can become an enduring great company. Apply the Good to Great framework to build breakthrough momentum: Get the right people on the bus (first who), confront the brutal facts of your reality, develop your Hedgehog Concept, build your flywheel turn by turn with disciplined action, and practice Level 5 leadership. Once you have achieved sustained great performance (the flywheel is turning with real momentum), layer on Built to Last principles to transform it into an enduring great company. Do not try to apply Built to Last concepts before the GtG foundation is in place—you will lack the sustained performance required to support an enduring core.

#### Step 2: Define and Build Your Core Ideology

Enduring great companies are guided by a core ideology—a set of core values and a core purpose that goes beyond making money. This ideology acts as the North Star across generations of leaders, product cycles, and market changes. To articulate your core values: gather 8–12 people with the deepest institutional knowledge, selected by: (1) tenure of 10+ years, (2) having led at least two major strategic changes, and (3) being cited by at least three other long-tenured employees as a keeper of institutional history, for a 2-hour workshop; ask each person to write down the principles they would never compromise, even if it cost the company money; identify common themes across participants and test each candidate value for durability ("Would this still be valid 100 years from now?"). To articulate your core purpose: ask "Why do we exist beyond just making money?" and continue asking "Why is that important?" five times to reach the fundamental purpose. Make your ideology explicit: Write it down in one page, share it widely, and build it into every process. Ensure everyone knows it—from the newest hire to the CEO. Preserve the core: Treat your core ideology as an anchor point. Do not change it lightly. Stimulate progress in everything else: Strategies, practices, products, and structures should adapt endlessly to a changing world. The only things that stay fixed are core values and purpose.

#### Step 3: Master the "Preserve the Core / Stimulate Progress" Dynamic

The fundamental challenge of enduring greatness is the tension between preservation and change. Enduring great companies resolve this through a dual approach: hold core values and purpose fixed while relentlessly adapting strategies, practices, and innovations. Walt Disney held firm to core values (passionate belief in creative imagination, fanatic attention to detail, abhorrence of cynicism, preservation of "Disney Magic") while constantly changing strategies—from hand-drawn animation to full-length feature films, from cartoons to television, and from animation to theme parks. The core stayed the same; the manifestations changed endlessly. In the non-core domain, practice continuous improvement by running one 90-day experiment per quarter in an area that does not directly support your Hedgehog Concept, with a budget proportionate to your size (suggested range: 2–5% of operating expenses for organizations over $10M revenue; up to 10% for organizations under $10M revenue), a written hypothesis, and a go/no-go decision at the end based on whether the experiment moved your primary economic denominator metric (profit per X) by at least 5%, while testing every change against your core ideology to ensure it does not violate your core values. When making any change, test it against the core: does this change preserve or violate our core ideology? When facing new opportunities or threats, ask: "Can we pursue this while remaining true to our core?" If yes, find a way. If no, walk away.

#### Step 4: Set Good BHAGs—Grounded in Understanding, Not Bravado

A BHAG (Big Hairy Audacious Goal) is a huge, daunting, compelling goal that galvanizes an organization forward. However, not all BHAGs are created equal. A good BHAG is set with understanding of the three Hedgehog circles. A bad BHAG is set with bravado—big, daring, but disconnected from reality. Boeing's BHAG in 1952 was to build a commercial jetliner (the 707), committing 25% of the company's entire net worth to a prototype. This was audacious—at the time, Boeing had virtually no presence in commercial aviation and airlines openly mocked them. But Boeing's BHAG was not bravado. It was grounded in understanding: Boeing's people were deeply passionate about building commercial aircraft, Boeing understood its cumulative experience with large aircraft made it uniquely capable of building the best commercial jet, and commercial aircraft economics would vastly outperform the military market. The result: Boeing produced five of the most successful commercial jets in history and dominated the industry for decades.

#### Step 5: Practice "Clock Building, Not Time Telling"

Build an organization that endures through many leaders and many product life cycles, rather than building around a single great leader or a single great idea. The genius of AND means embracing both extremes simultaneously—purpose AND profit, continuity AND change, freedom AND responsibility. Hewlett-Packard was built as a company that would endure beyond Bill Hewlett and David Packard. They hired extraordinary people even when they had no specific role in mind, building a deep bench of talent. The "HP Way" distinguished the company more than any of its products. When you build a clock, you build something that tells time generation after generation. When you merely tell time, you build something that works only as long as you are there to tell it.

#### Step 6: Choose Meaningful Work

The deepest driver of enduring greatness is not money, size, or status—it is meaningful work. The question "Why greatness?" is almost nonsensical if you are doing work you truly care about. If you have to ask why you should try for greatness, you may be in the wrong line of work. Align your work with your personal three Hedgehog circles: What are you passionate about? What can you be great at? What drives your economic engine? If your current work doesn't meet this test, search for meaningful work. Engage in something you care about so much that you want to make it the greatest it can possibly be—not for what you will get, but because it can be done. When you do meaningful work, you will naturally grow toward Level 5 leadership—because you will have the drive and discipline to practice all the other GtG findings.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Apply Good to Great first, then Built to Last
- ✅ Define and preserve your core ideology (core values + core purpose)
- ✅ Stimulate progress in everything that is not core
- ✅ Set good BHAGs grounded in understanding of the three circles
- ✅ Practice "clock building, not time telling"
- ✅ Embrace the Genius of AND
- ✅ Choose meaningful work aligned with your personal three circles
- ✅ Apply all key concepts consistently over time
- ❌ Do not try to build an enduring great company without first achieving sustained great results
- ❌ Do not confuse core ideology with strategy or practices
- ❌ Do not change core values to chase short-term opportunities
- ❌ Do not set BHAGs based on hype, fear, or competition rather than genuine understanding
- ❌ Do not cherry-pick concepts from GtG or BtL and ignore others

#### Keep In Mind

- Good to Great is not a sequel to Built to Last; it is the prerequisite. As Collins states: "Apply the findings in this book to create sustained great results... and then apply the findings in Built to Last to go from great results to an enduring great company."
- It is much easier to become great than to remain great. If you ever stop doing any one of the key ideas, your organization will inevitably slide backward toward mediocrity.
- To create an enduring great company requires all the key concepts from both studies, tied together and applied consistently over time.
- Greatness does not depend on size. A small institution can be truly great if it is excellent at what it does.
- Profits and cash flow are like blood and water to a healthy body—essential for life, but not the point of life.

#### Security & Safety Notes

Not applicable.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Trying to apply Built to Last principles before the GtG foundation is in place.
  **Solution:** You must first achieve sustained great performance before you can make it endure. A company with mediocre results cannot have an enduring great core ideology—the core needs strong performance to sustain it.
- **Problem:** Confusing core ideology with strategy or practices.
  **Solution:** Strategies change; core does not. Test for durability: would these values still be valid 100 years from now?
- **Problem:** Changing core values to chase short-term opportunities or profits.
  **Solution:** The core is fixed. Everything else is up for grabs. If a change violates your core ideology, walk away from the opportunity.
- **Problem:** Setting BHAGs based on hype, fear, or competition rather than genuine understanding.
  **Solution:** A BHAG without understanding is dangerous; a BHAG with understanding is transformative. When you combine quiet understanding of the three circles with the audacity of a BHAG, you get a powerful, almost magical mix.
- **Problem:** Getting lazy after achieving breakthrough.
  **Solution:** Hasbro became the best-performing comparison company in the study because it understood all three circles, but it lost the discipline to stay within the three circles after the unexpected death of its CEO. If you successfully apply these ideas and then stop doing them, you will slide backward.
- **Problem:** Staying in work that is successful but meaningless.
  **Solution:** It is impossible to have a great life unless it is a meaningful life. And it is very difficult to have a meaningful life without meaningful work. If you have to ask "Why greatness? Isn't success enough?", you may be in the wrong line of work.
