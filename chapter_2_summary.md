# Chapter 2: Level 5 Leadership

## Overview

This chapter introduces **Level 5 Leadership** as the foundational finding from Jim Collins's research on what transforms good companies into great ones. Every good-to-great company in the study had Level 5 leadership at the pivotal time of transition. Level 5 leaders blend **extreme personal humility** with **intense professional will**. They are fiercely ambitious, but their ambition is for the institution, not for themselves. The chapter contrasts Level 5 leaders with comparison-group leaders who were often egocentric, celebrity-focused, and ultimately failed to sustain great results.

---

## Lessons

### 1. Develop the Dual Nature of Level 5 Leadership: Humility + Will

**What it is:**
Level 5 leadership is a paradoxical blend of personal humility and professional will. You must be modest and self-effacing in demeanor while simultaneously being ferociously resolved to do whatever it takes to build a great company.

**Examples:**
- **Darwin Smith** (Kimberly-Clark): Shy, awkward, dressed unfashionably, spent vacations rummaging on his farm—yet made the gutsiest decision in the company's history by selling the mills and investing in consumer products like Huggies and Kleenex.
- **Abraham Lincoln**: Never let ego get in the way of his ambition for an enduring great nation. Those who mistook his modesty for weakness paid dearly.
- **Colman Mockler** (Gillette): Quiet, reserved, courteous gentleman who fought off two hostile takeover bids (from Ronald Perelman and Coniston Partners) to protect Gillette's future investments in Sensor and Mach3.
- **George Cain** (Abbott Laboratories): Had no inspiring personality, but had inspired standards. Systematically destroyed nepotism by rebuilding the board and executive team with the best people, regardless of family ties.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. **Self-assessment**: Honestly evaluate whether your ego drives your decisions more than the company's long-term success.
2. **Cultivate humility**: Practice deflecting credit to others and accepting blame yourself.
3. **Build professional will**: Develop an incurable need to produce sustained results and an intolerance for mediocrity.
4. **Align ambition**: Redirect your personal ambition toward building something larger and more lasting than yourself.
5. **Practice under pressure**: Test your resolve during difficult decisions—do you waver when faced with personal gain versus institutional good?

**Best practices:**
- View your role as a steward of the company's future, not as a platform for personal aggrandizement.
- Measure your success by how well the company performs after you leave, not while you are there.

**Keep in mind:**
- Level 5 is not "nice" or "weak." The humility coexists with ironclad resolve.
- You can have ego and self-interest—just channel them away from yourself and into the larger goal.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Mistaking humility for weakness or meekness.
- Thinking Level 5 means being a "servant leader" or "selfless executive"—these labels miss the ferocious will side.
- Letting personal financial gain (golden parachutes, stock flipping) override long-term institutional success.

### 2. Practice the "Window and the Mirror" Principle

**What it is:**
Level 5 leaders look **out the window** to apportion credit to factors outside themselves when things go well (attributing success to luck, great colleagues, or external factors). When things go poorly, they look **in the mirror** and take full responsibility, never blaming bad luck. Comparison leaders do the opposite.

**Examples:**
- **Alan Wurtzel** (Circuit City): When asked for the top five factors in his company's transformation, he said #1 was luck: "We were in a great industry, with the wind at our backs."
- **Ken Iverson** (Nucor): Faced with cheap imported steel, he called it a blessing: "Aren't we lucky; steel is heavy, and they have to ship it all the way across the ocean."
- **Bethlehem Steel CEO**: Blamed imports for all problems: "Our first, second, and third problems are imports."
- **Joseph F. Cullman III** (Philip Morris): Wrote a book titled *I'm a Lucky Guy*, refusing to take credit for success.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. **After a success**: Before attributing results to your own brilliance, list at least three external factors or people who contributed.
2. **After a failure**: Do not blame market conditions, competitors, or bad luck. Ask yourself what you personally could have done differently.
3. **Credit luck generously**: When things go well, acknowledge good fortune as a significant factor.
4. **Reject victimhood**: When things go poorly, own the outcome fully before analyzing external factors.

**Best practices:**
- Make "luck" your default explanation for positive outcomes until proven otherwise.
- Use the mirror test: "If I were replaced tomorrow, would this entity still thrive?"

**Keep in mind:**
- The window and mirror often do not reflect objective reality—everyone sees the Level 5 leader as the key to success, and the Level 5 leader sees everyone else.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Preening in front of the mirror after success (taking excessive personal credit).
- Looking out the window to blame others or bad luck after failure.
- Believing your own press clippings.

### 3. Set Up Your Successors for Success, Not Failure

**What it is:**
Level 5 leaders want the company to be even more successful in the next generation and are comfortable with the idea that most people won't know the roots of that success trace back to their efforts. Egocentric Level 4 leaders often set up successors for failure or choose weak successors, because a failing company after they leave would diminish their legacy.

**Examples:**
- **David Maxwell** (Fannie Mae): Transformed the company from losing $1 million/day to earning $4 million/day, beating the market 3.8 to 1. Retired while still at the top of his game, handing the reins to equally capable Jim Johnson. When his $20 million retirement package became controversial, he instructed Johnson not to pay him the remaining $5.5 million and donated it to the Fannie Mae foundation for low-income housing.
- **Stanley Gault** (Rubbermaid): Built the company into #1 on Fortune's Most Admired list with 40 consecutive quarters of earnings growth, but chose a weak successor who lasted only one year. The company quickly disintegrated and had to be acquired by Newell.
- **Lee Iacocca** (Chrysler): Saved Chrysler from catastrophe, achieving 2.9x the market. But then turned himself into a celebrity—talk shows, 80+ commercials, autobiography selling 7 million copies, considering a presidential run. Postponed retirement so many times that insiders joked "I Am Chairman of Chrysler Corporation Always." Chrysler eventually fell to Daimler-Benz.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. **Identify successor talent early**: Start grooming capable successors years before you plan to leave.
2. **Choose the best person for the job**: Prioritize capability over loyalty or personal chemistry.
3. **Prepare the ground**: Ensure the company has strong systems, depth of management, and strategic clarity that will outlast your tenure.
4. **Retire at the right time**: Leave while you are still at the top of your game, not after decline has set in.
5. **Support your successor**: Publicly and privately back your chosen successor, even if they do things differently than you would.

**Best practices:**
- Ask yourself: "If I leave tomorrow, will the company be in better shape than when I arrived?"
- Measure your legacy by the sustained performance of the company after you're gone.

**Keep in mind:**
- The "biggest dog in the kennel" syndrome: You don't need to be the biggest dog; you need the kennel to produce great dogs long after you're gone.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Choosing a weak successor intentionally or unintentionally.
- Overstaying your welcome—refusing to retire until decline is inevitable.
- Demanding excessive post-retirement perks (private jets, stock options) that undermine the company's health.

### 4. Cultivate Compelling Modesty

**What it is:**
Level 5 leaders are self-effacing, understated, and modest. They deflect discussion about their own contributions, deflect praise onto others, and never seek hero status or executive celebrity. They are quietly producing extraordinary results without fanfare.

**Examples:**
- **Darwin Smith**: When asked to describe his management style, after a long, uncomfortable silence, he simply said, "Eccentric." He found companionship among plumbers and electricians, not other CEOs.
- **Ken Iverson** (Nucor): Got his dogs at the local pound, lived in a simple house with only a carport, used his credit card to scrape frost off his windows rather than enclosing the carport.
- **George Cain**: Refused to take credit, saying "We were blessed with marvelous people."
- **Alan Wurtzel**: Described himself as a "plow horse" rather than a "show horse."

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. ** deflect praise**: When complimented, immediately credit your team, your predecessors, or good fortune.
2. **Avoid self-promotion**: Do not seek speaking engagements, magazine covers, or autobiographical books about your achievements.
3. **Dress and behave simply**: Avoid status symbols that set you apart from other employees.
4. **Listen more than you talk**: In interviews and meetings, focus on the company and others' contributions rather than your own.
5. **Check your language**: Reduce use of "I" and increase use of "we" in your communications.

**Best practices:**
- Model the behavior you want to see in your organization.
- Let your results speak for themselves rather than marketing your personal brand.

**Keep in mind:**
- This is not false modesty. Level 5 leaders genuinely believe others deserve more credit than they do.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Overcorrecting into false humility that rings insincere.
- Thinking modesty means you cannot be ambitious or driven.
- Being so modest that you fail to provide necessary leadership direction.

### 5. Display Unwavering Resolve to Do What Must Be Done

**What it is:**
Level 5 leaders are fanatically driven with an incurable need to produce sustained results. They will make the hardest decisions—selling core businesses, firing family members, fighting raiders—because it is what the company needs. They do not waver, doubt, or second-guess.

**Examples:**
- **Darwin Smith**: Sold the mills at Kimberly-Clark despite business media calling it "stupid" and Wall Street downgrading the stock. Never wavered. Twenty-five years later, Kimberly-Clark owned Scott Paper outright and beat Procter & Gamble in six of eight categories.
- **Cork Walgreen III** (Walgreens): Drew a line in the sand: "We are going to be out of the restaurant business completely in five years." Had over 500 restaurants. Six months later, when someone mentioned they only had five years, he tapped the table and said, "Listen, you have four and a half years. I said you had five years six months ago. Now you've got four and a half years." Never wavered.
- **George Cain**: Fired family members at Abbott Laboratories who could not perform. Holiday gatherings were tense, but family members were pleased with their stock performance.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. **Identify the brutal facts**: Confront the reality of what the company must do, regardless of emotional attachment.
2. **Make the decision decisively**: Announce the decision clearly and stick to it.
3. **Communicate relentlessly**: Repeat the decision and timeline constantly to ensure the organization understands there is no ambiguity.
4. **Resist second-guessing**: Do not revisit decisions unless new facts emerge.
5. **Accept short-term pain for long-term gain**: Be prepared for criticism, stock downgrades, and media attacks.

**Best practices:**
- Frame difficult decisions as necessary for the company's survival or greatness, not as personal preferences.
- Use the "burn the boats" mentality: eliminate options that would allow retreat from necessary change.

**Keep in mind:**
- This resolve can appear cold or ruthless, but it is driven by ambition for the company, not personal sadism.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Confusing stubbornness with resolve—refusing to change course when new evidence warrants it.
- Making decisions based on ego rather than institutional need.
- Failing to communicate the "why" behind hard decisions, leading to organizational confusion.

### 6. Be a Plow Horse, Not a Show Horse

**What it is:**
Level 5 leaders display workmanlike diligence. They are consistent, steady, and focused on results rather than personal glory. They are the plow horse that quietly produces extraordinary results, not the show horse that seeks attention.

**Examples:**
- **Alan Wurtzel**: When asked about differences between himself and his counterpart at Silo (Circuit City's comparison company), he said: "The show horse and the plow horse—he was more of a show horse, whereas I was more of a plow horse."
- **Alan Wurtzel's results**: $1 invested in Circuit City under Wurtzel outperformed $1 invested in General Electric under Jack Welch by six times (from 1981 to 2000).

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. **Focus on the work**: Prioritize execution and results over image and publicity.
2. **Reject celebrity**: Do not seek or accept the spotlight for personal achievement.
3. **Maintain steady performance**: Avoid dramatic swings in strategy or personality for effect.
4. **Measure yourself by results**: Let stock performance and sustained excellence be your scorecard, not magazine covers.

**Best practices:**
- Avoid the "cult of the CEO"—do not allow your company to build a personal brand around you that could crumble when you leave.
- Invest in building systems and culture that produce results independently of any single leader.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Believing that charisma or showmanship is required for great leadership.
- Allowing boards and investors to pressure you into becoming a "celebrity CEO" when your natural style is quieter.

### 7. Attribute Success to External Factors and Luck

**What it is:**
Level 5 leaders consistently attribute their success to factors outside themselves—good luck, great colleagues, favorable industry conditions, or the work of predecessors. They never claim personal greatness as the primary driver.

**Examples:**
- **Alan Wurtzel**: "The number one factor was luck. We were in a great industry, with the wind at our backs." Later: "The first thing that comes to mind is luck... I was lucky to find the right successor."
- **Nucor executive**: When asked why the company had such a remarkable track record of good decisions: "I guess we were just lucky."
- **Joseph Cullman III**: Wrote a book titled *I'm a Lucky Guy*, beginning with: "I was a very lucky guy from the very beginning of my life: marvelous parents, good genes, lucky in love, lucky in business..."

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. **Start with luck**: When evaluating a success, begin by listing fortunate circumstances.
2. **Credit the team**: Acknowledge the contributions of colleagues, successors, and predecessors.
3. **Thank the industry**: Recognize that you may have been in the right place at the right time.
4. **Avoid self-congratulation**: Resist the urge to write autobiographies or give interviews trumpeting your personal genius.

**Best practices:**
- Develop a "luck inventory" for your major decisions—what external factors made this possible?
- Publicly thank others first, last, and always.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Believing your own hype—success can breed egotism if you attribute it solely to personal ability.
- Dismissing luck entirely because it feels unsatisfying or unscientific.

### 8. Prefer Inside Leaders; Avoid the Outside Savior Syndrome

**What it is:**
The evidence shows that going for a high-profile outside change agent is negatively correlated with a sustained transformation from good to great. Ten of eleven good-to-great CEOs came from inside the company. Comparison companies turned to outsiders six times more frequently and failed.

**Examples:**
- **Inside leaders (Good-to-Great)**: Darwin Smith (Kimberly-Clark), Colman Mockler (Gillette), George Cain (Abbott), Cork Walgreen (Walgreens), Alan Wurtzel (Circuit City), David Maxwell (Fannie Mae), Ken Iverson (Nucor), Joseph Cullman (Philip Morris).
- **Outside leaders (Comparison)**: Stanley Gault (Rubbermaid), Al Dunlap (Scott Paper), Lee Iacocca (Chrysler) rode in "as saviors from the outside, trumpets blaring."

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. **Look internally first**: Before searching externally for a CEO, thoroughly evaluate internal candidates.
2. **Evaluate track record inside**: Look for people who have demonstrated Level 5 traits within the organization over time.
3. **Resist board pressure**: Push back against directors who demand a "big name" outsider to signal change.
4. **Develop internal talent**: Build a deep bench of potential successors so you never need an external savior.

**Best practices:**
- Insiders know the culture, the history, and the business. They can make difficult decisions without the learning curve of an outsider.
- External hires often bring "change for change's sake" rather than the nuanced understanding needed for sustained transformation.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Hiring an outside celebrity to please the board or Wall Street, rather than because they are the best long-term fit.
- Believing the myth that only an outsider can shake up a stagnant culture.

### 9. Identify and Cultivate Potential Level 5 Leaders

**What it is:**
Potential Level 5 leaders are highly prevalent in society. The problem is not a dearth of them, but rather that boards and organizations rarely recognize them because they look for larger-than-life personalities instead. Look for situations where extraordinary results exist but where no individual steps forth to claim excess credit.

**Examples:**
- The research team initially tried to downplay leadership but kept finding something "consistently unusual" about the good-to-great executives.
- Darwin Smith, Colman Mockler, Alan Wurtzel—all seemingly ordinary people quietly producing extraordinary results.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. **Look for quiet excellence**: Identify people who produce great results without seeking the spotlight.
2. **Observe credit allocation**: Notice who gives credit to others versus who claims it for themselves.
3. **Test for resolve**: Put candidates in situations where they must choose between personal gain and institutional good.
4. **Provide development opportunities**: Offer mentorship, challenging assignments, and reflective practices.
5. **Create catalysts**: Significant life experiences, mentors, Level 5 bosses, or religious conversions can spark development.

**Best practices:**
- Do not dismiss modest, shy, or awkward people as leadership material.
- Look for the seed: people who already subjugate egoistic needs to building something larger.

**Keep in mind:**
- There is no "Ten-Step List to Level 5." The inner development of a person to Level 5 cannot be reduced to a formula.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Overlooking Level 5 potential because the person lacks charisma or executive presence.
- Assuming Level 5 leaders are born, not developed.
- Hiring for personality rather than the seed of Level 5 traits.

### 10. Channel Ego Needs Into the Larger Goal

**What it is:**
Level 5 leaders do have ego and self-interest—they are incredibly ambitious. But they channel those needs away from themselves and into building a great company. Their ambition is first and foremost for the institution, not for personal riches and renown.

**Examples:**
- **Darwin Smith**: After transforming Kimberly-Clark, he reflected simply, "I never stopped trying to become qualified for the job."
- **David Maxwell**: Donated his remaining $5.5 million retirement package to the Fannie Mae foundation rather than pocketing it.
- **Colman Mockler**: Would not have changed his approach even if he knew he would die in harness—it wasn't an option in his value system to take the easy path.
- **Lee Iacocca** (contrast): Diverted attention to making himself the most celebrated CEO in American business history, appearing on talk shows, starring in 80+ commercials, promoting his autobiography (7 million copies), and being mobbed by fans in Japan.

**Step-by-step instructions:**
1. **Examine your motives**: Ask why you want to lead—is it for the company's success or for personal fame and fortune?
2. **Reframe ambition**: If your drive is primarily personal, consciously redirect it toward institutional outcomes.
3. **Test decisions against the mirror**: Would you make the same decision if no one would ever know your name?
4. **Measure legacy by company endurance**: Ask whether the company will be great long after you are gone.
5. **Reject personal perks that harm the company**: Turn down golden parachutes, private jets, and other benefits that send the wrong signal.

**Best practices:**
- Use your natural ambition as fuel, but point it at the company rather than yourself.
- Find satisfaction in the work itself and the institution's success, not in personal accolades.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Thinking Level 5 means having no ego—it means transforming ego into institutional ambition.
- Allowing personal stock gains to override long-term shareholder value (e.g., taking a 44% premium to sell a company that would be worth far more later).
- Post-retirement demands that contradict the principle of putting the company first.

## Key Terms and Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Level 5 Leadership** | The highest level in a hierarchy of executive capabilities; leaders who blend extreme personal humility with intense professional will. |
| **Window and the Mirror** | The pattern where Level 5 leaders look out the window to credit others (or luck) for success, and look in the mirror to take responsibility for failure. Comparison leaders do the opposite. |
| **Compelling Modesty** | The trait of being self-effacing, understated, and humble; deflecting credit and never seeking hero status. |
| **Professional Will** | The fanatical drive and stoic resolve to do whatever is necessary to make the company great, regardless of personal cost. |
| **Plow Horse** | A metaphor for workmanlike diligence and steady, unglamorous results production (contrasted with "show horse"). |
| **Show Horse** | A metaphor for charismatic, attention-seeking leadership focused on personal image rather than results. |
| **Inside Successor** | A leader promoted from within the organization, preferred for sustained good-to-great transitions. |
| **Outside Savior** | A high-profile external leader brought in to transform a company; negatively correlated with sustained greatness. |
| **Ambition for the Company** | The core Level 5 motivation: building a great institution that endures beyond the leader's tenure. |
| **Gargantuan Ego** | Excessive self-regard and need for personal glory, common among comparison leaders and associated with company mediocrity or collapse. |
| **"Biggest Dog" Syndrome** | The tendency of egocentric leaders to want to remain the most important person in the organization, even if it harms the company. |
| **Luck** | A factor Level 5 leaders frequently cite for success; not because they were luckier, but because they looked out the window for credit rather than claiming personal greatness. |
| **The Seed** | The innate potential within some people to evolve into Level 5 leaders, given the right circumstances and development. |
| **Level 4 Leadership** | Capable leadership that produces results through personal effort and positioning, but lacks the humility to build enduring greatness. |
| **Good-to-Great Companies** | The eleven companies in Collins's study that achieved sustained, exceptional performance transformation. |
| **Comparison Companies** | Companies in the study that did not make the leap from good to great, used as a control group. |

## Chapter Summary

- Every good-to-great company had Level 5 leadership during the pivotal transition years.
- Level 5 is defined by the dualities of humility + will, modesty + fearlessness, and personal humility + professional resolve.
- Level 5 leaders set up successors for success; comparison leaders often set up successors for failure.
- Level 5 leaders display compelling modesty and workmanlike diligence (plow horse over show horse).
- Level 5 leaders look out the window for credit and in the mirror for blame; comparison leaders reverse this.
- Outside, celebrity CEOs are negatively correlated with sustained transformation.
- Potential Level 5 leaders exist all around us; the key is knowing what to look for (extraordinary results with no individual claiming excess credit).
- There is no guaranteed formula to become Level 5, but practicing the other good-to-great disciplines helps cultivate the traits.
