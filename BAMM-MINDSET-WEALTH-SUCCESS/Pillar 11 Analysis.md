# Pillar 11 Analysis: "Determining Your Instigators, Spectators, and Participators"
**Source:** *Building a Millionaire Mindset* by Johnny Wimbrey

---

## Overview

This chapter is one of the most emotionally charged and strategically intense in the book. Rather than offering gentle encouragement, Wimbrey shocks the reader out of complacency by forcing a brutal comparison between the dedication of a drug addict pursuing self-destruction and the commitment level required to build generational wealth. The chapter's core thesis: **if you are not willing to apply the same relentless, fear-driven, no-excuses energy to your success that a drug addict applies to getting high, you will fail — regardless of your circumstances.**

The chapter also reframes fear as the ultimate fuel for action, tells the story of a real-life transformation through fear, and closes with a mandatory, time-sensitive action step: make three real-world, face-to-face connections before the end of the day.

### Lesson 1: Adopt the Dedication of a Drug Addict (Redirected)

**Title:** Redirect the "Whatever-It-Takes" Mindset Toward Your Success

**Core Teaching:**
A drug addict wakes up every single day with one burning goal: to get high. They do so regardless of their circumstances — no job, no family, no money, no home. The fear of *not* getting high that day overrides the fear of not eating. Wimbrey saw this firsthand and recognized it as the purest form of "never give up" he had ever witnessed. His challenge to you: **tap into that exact same level of fear, urgency, and commitment — but direct it toward building your success and generational wealth instead.**

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Acknowledge the comparison honestly.** Do not dismiss this analogy. Sit with it. A drug addict starts from ground zero and *still* succeeds at their goal every day. Where are you starting from compared to them?
2. **Identify your version of "getting high."** What is the one outcome — your financial freedom, your children's future, your legacy — that must happen no matter what?
3. **Write down your non-negotiable goal.** Make it visceral. Make it emotional. Make it something that, if not achieved, would cause you more pain than any sacrifice required to get there.
4. **Elevate the fear of *not* succeeding above every other fear.** Let the thought of failing — of your children knowing poverty, of returning to a homeless shelter, of being called a failure — be more terrifying than the discomfort of the work itself.
5. **Begin each day by restating your non-negotiable.** Before you eat, before you check your phone, before you do anything else — remind yourself what you are moving toward and what you are running from.

**Best Practices:**
- Do not wait until you "feel motivated." Drug addicts don't negotiate with their feelings — they act. Treat your goals the same way.
- Anchor your commitment to something bigger than money. Wimbrey didn't chase millions; he chased a legacy for his children. Find your "something greater."
- Make your fear *visible*. Write it down, say it out loud, tell someone. Do not let it operate in the dark where it becomes abstract and manageable.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Treating this as a one-time exercise.** This is a daily practice. The addict's dedication is renewed every morning. Yours must be too.
- **Chasing money as the primary goal.** Wimbrey explicitly states he never wanted to be a millionaire — he wanted to be a "good man" who left an inheritance. Money is a byproduct; the real driver must be emotional and legacy-oriented.
- **Making excuses for limitations.** People at ground zero are already starting their day with nothing and finding a way to self-sabotage. Meanwhile, you are "contemplating your limitations and building a wall of excuses." Stop. Recognize that your excuses are a choice, not a circumstance.

### Lesson 2: Use Your Biggest Fear as Your Primary Fuel

**Title:** Pinpoint Your Greatest Fear and Harness It as a Daily Driver

**Core Teaching:**
Most people allow their fears to paralyze them and keep them from stepping toward their destiny. Wimbrey flips this entirely: **you must face your biggest fear, name it, and use it as the engine that drives you forward.** Through his interviews with elite performers across industries — professional athletes, billion-dollar entrepreneurs, award-winning entertainers — he found the common denominator: they were all *motivated by their known fears*. A true champion knows that running toward a known fear is more rewarding than running toward the comfort zone of mediocrity.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Identify your deepest, most visceral fear.** It must be specific. Wimbrey's fear was returning to a homeless shelter — triggered by a memory at age three of having a refrigerator door slammed in his face at a communal shelter. Dig until you find *your* version of that moment.
2. **Name it out loud.** Speak the fear to yourself in a mirror, to a trusted person, or in a journal. Do not let it live in vague anxiety.
3. **Write it down.** Physically write it. The act of writing externalizes it and makes it real.
4. **Tell someone else.** Share your fear with a person who will hold you accountable. This removes its power to hide.
5. **Bring it to the surface every single day.** Let this fear be the first thought that gets you out of bed and the last thought that keeps you working when you want to quit.
6. **Reframe running away as running toward.** When you feel the urge to avoid a difficult conversation, a risky opportunity, or an uncomfortable stretch — recognize that you are not just avoiding fear; you are also running away from your goal. Flip it: *run toward* the fear because on the other side is the life you want.

**Best Practices:**
- Choose a fear that is *yours*, not one borrowed from someone else's story. Your fear must be emotionally true to you or it will not sustain motivation.
- Pair fear with vision. Fear alone can burn you out. Fear + a compelling vision of what you're building creates sustainable drive.
- Study the stories of people who have already walked through your fear. If they could do it, so can you.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Picking a shallow fear.** "I'm afraid of public speaking" is not the same level of fuel as "I'm afraid my children will grow up in the same poverty I did." Go deep. Go personal.
- **Letting fear immobilize you instead of mobilizing you.** This is the default state for most people. You must consciously and deliberately *redirect* fear's energy toward action.
- **Forgetting that you're running toward something.** Fear without direction is just anxiety. Always pair it with the compelling "why" — the legacy, the freedom, the life you are building.

### Lesson 3: Commit Irrevocably — No Escape Hatch

**Title:** Quit Your Excuses Before They Quit You

**Core Teaching:**
The story of the young boxer from Brooklyn illustrates the power of **irreversible commitment**. He showed up to a social gathering he hadn't qualified for, was asked if he was coming to the seminar the next day, and initially said no — he had to work at 6 a.m. But when given a genuine opportunity, he didn't call in sick, didn't ask for the day off, didn't negotiate — he **quit his job on the spot**. That was his point of no return. The fear he found was the fear of missing out on something big — and that fear pushed him to take a risk that most people would never consider.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Audit your current commitments honestly.** Where are you half-in? Where are you maintaining an escape hatch — a side hustle that justifies not going all-in, a job that keeps you "safe" while you dream, relationships that anchor you to mediocrity?
2. **Identify what needs to be cut or radically elevated.** Be ruthless. If it is not serving your goal, it is costing you your future.
3. **Make the bold move.** You do not have to quit your job tomorrow — but you must create a moment where there is no going back. Sign a contract, make a public commitment, invest money you cannot afford to lose, tell someone you respect that you are all-in.
4. **Let the fear of missing out (FOMO) on your own life drive the decision.** The young boxer chose his future over his paycheck. What are you choosing?
5. **Refuse to look back.** Once you make the move, do not second-guess it. Wimbrey notes the young boxer "never once looked back."

**Best Practices:**
- Make your commitment *public* when possible. Telling someone raises the stakes and creates accountability.
- Commit to a process, not just an outcome. The young boxer didn't know he would become a millionaire — he just knew he was showing up and going all-in on the journey.
- Measure your commitment by what you are willing to sacrifice, not by what you are willing to claim.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Confusing interest with commitment.** Interest says "I'll try this when I have time." Commitment says "I'll make time." The young boxer had zero evidence that the seminar would change his life — he showed up anyway.
- **Leaving an escape hatch open.** If you keep your "safe" job, your old circle, and your old habits, you are not truly committed. You are hedging. And hedging produces hedging results.
- **Waiting for permission or certainty.** The young boxer was unqualified, unknown, and uninvited. He showed up anyway. Certainty is a luxury that people who are already successful can afford. You cannot wait for it.

### Lesson 4: Execute a Mandatory, Real-World Networking Action Step

**Title:** Make Three Confirmed, Face-to-Face Connections Before the Day Ends

**Core Teaching:**
Every deal — whether it is swapping baseball cards or a multibillion-dollar merger — starts with an introduction and a handshake. The strength and breadth of your personal network directly reflects the breadth of opportunities you will have on your path to wealth. Digital connections are not enough. Real relationships built face-to-face are the currency of opportunity. Wimbrey issues a direct, time-bound challenge: go out today, before the end of the day, and have confirmed meetings with three new people on your calendar.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Stop reading.** Physically put the book down. This is not optional — the action step is designed to force immediate, tangible movement.
2. **Get up and leave your house.** Digital outreach does not count for this step. You must be in the physical world.
3. **Identify three people.** They can be:
   - A mentor you have always considered out of reach
   - Someone in your field you have wanted to pitch for ages
   - A "maybe" connection — someone you haven't fully chased down yet
   - People you already know but haven't built a real bond with
4. **Reach out to them today.** Make the ask. Be specific: coffee, lunch, dinner, or drinks. Have a clear time proposal.
5. **Get confirmed meetings on your calendar before the end of the day.** Not "maybe," not "I'll follow up" — confirmed, locked-in meetings with dates and times.
6. **Document your results.** Fill in the names and meeting dates. Take a photo with the book and your completed answers.
7. **Share your progress publicly.** Tag Wimbrey on Instagram, Twitter, Facebook, and LinkedIn. Use the hashtags: #BAMM #WIMBREY #CoachJohnny. This creates social accountability and invites community reinforcement.

**Best Practices:**
- **Quality over quantity in your outreach.** Three real, confirmed meetings are infinitely more valuable than thirty unanswered emails.
- **Lead with value, not need.** When reaching out, frame the ask around what you can bring to the conversation, not just what you want from them.
- **Prepare before you reach out.** Know who they are, know what they care about, and have a specific, respectful reason for wanting to connect.
- **Treat this as non-negotiable.** The end-of-day deadline is intentional. It forces action and eliminates the procrastination that kills most people's networking goals.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Replacing real connections with digital ones.** "Connecting" on LinkedIn or sending a cold email is not the same as sitting across from someone. Do not substitute the easy for the effective.
- **Waiting for the "right" time or the "right" person.** Your three connections do not have to be billionaires. They have to be real people who can open doors, offer perspective, or become part of your network.
- **Skipping the public accountability step.** Sharing your progress is not vanity — it is a commitment device. When you post your three connections, you are making a promise to yourself and your community that you are doing the work.
- **Reaching out without a clear ask.** "Want to grab coffee sometime?" gets ignored. "I'd love 20 minutes of your time next Tuesday at 10 a.m. to discuss X" gets a yes.

### Lesson 5: Redefine Your Definition of Success

**Title:** Success Is Not a Bank Account Number — It Is a Legacy

**Core Teaching:**
Wimbrey explicitly challenges the default equation: success = money. He says, "I never wanted to be a millionaire. That was never my primary goal. All I wanted was to leave a legacy for my children." He anchors this in ancient biblical scripture: "A good man will leave an inheritance for his children's children." The real question he poses to the reader: **what is your excuse for why being a first-generation millionaire is not possible for you?** And while you entertain those excuses, remember that people at ground zero are already succeeding despite having nothing — and you are "still contemplating your limitations and building a wall of excuses."

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Write down your current definition of success.** Is it a number in a bank account? A title? A lifestyle? Be honest.
2. **Reframe it around legacy and obligation.** Ask yourself: what would I need to build so that my children's children are better off? What am I here to create that will outlive me?
3. **Identify your excuse.** What is the story you have told yourself time and time again for why this is not possible for you? Write it down in one sentence.
4. **Destroy the excuse.** Look at the evidence against it: people with less than you, with more obstacles than you, with fewer resources than you — are succeeding. Your excuse is not a fact. It is a choice.
5. **Replace the excuse with a commitment statement.** "Being a first-generation millionaire is my God-given obligation. My children's memory will not be of pain and poverty. I will build a legacy that outlives me."

**Best Practices:**
- Anchor your success definition in something that is bigger than you. Legacy, family, contribution — these produce sustained motivation in a way that personal luxury never will.
- Regularly revisit your "why." When the work gets hard, it is not the goal that sustains you — it is the reason behind the goal.
- Let ancient wisdom and proven frameworks (like generational inheritance) inform your thinking. You are not the first person to face these questions, and you do not have to figure it all out alone.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Defining success in isolation.** If your success only benefits you, it will be fragile. Build a "why" that is tied to others — your family, your community, future generations.
- **Confusing "I can't" with "I haven't yet."** Circumstance is not destiny. Wimbrey is proof. The young boxer is proof. People in adverse situations "still decide to win every day by hitting their goal through any means necessary." Reclassify your barriers as temporary conditions, not permanent identities.
- **Building a wall of excuses instead of a plan.** Every time you explain away why you can't succeed, you are investing in your own failure. Redirect that energy toward building instead of defending.

### Lesson 6: Maintain Hunger and Grit When the Road Gets Rough

**Title:** Combine Grit with Hunger — Stick It Out and Stay Driven

**Core Teaching:**
This chapter closes by circling back to two foundational concepts introduced earlier in the book: **grit** (the willingness to stick it out when the road gets rough) and **hunger** (the drive to create wealth). Having made it this far in the book shows dedication — but now is the moment to "flex some real drive." Grit gets you through the hard days. Hunger gets you started on the hard days. You need both.

**Step-by-Step Instructions:**
1. **Audit your current grit level.** When was the last time you wanted to quit and kept going? When was the last time you stuck with something past the point of excitement and into the point of grind? Be honest about where you are.
2. **Reignite your hunger.** What originally attracted you to building wealth? What is the life you are fighting for? Reconnect with that vision — not as a fantasy, but as a target.
3. **Pair grit and hunger in your daily routine.** Let hunger be your morning fuel (the vision that gets you out of bed). Let grit be your evening fuel (the discipline that keeps you working when motivation fades).
4. **Create a ritual for hard days.** When you feel like giving up, have a pre-planned response: call a mentor, reread your fear statement, revisit your legacy statement, or take one small action anyway.
5. **Track your consistency.** Measure yourself not by breakthroughs, but by how many days in a row you show up even when it is hard.

**Best Practices:**
- Build grit through small, repeated acts of sticking it out. Grit is a muscle — train it daily.
- Keep hunger alive by regularly revisiting your "why" and celebrating small wins along the way. Hunger dies when progress feels invisible.
- Surround yourself with people who have both grit and hunger. These qualities are contagious.

**Common Pitfalls:**
- **Having grit without hunger.** You can work hard for a long time and still burn out if you don't remember why you're doing it. Grit without purpose is just suffering.
- **Having hunger without grit.** Enthusiasm without follow-through produces nothing. You must be willing to stay in the arena when it's no longer fun.
- **Confusing a rough patch with a permanent state.** Grit is not about being tough every single day — it's about not quitting permanently because of a few bad days.
