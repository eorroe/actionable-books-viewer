# Chapter 19 Analysis: Wealth File #7

## Core Intent (1 sentence)
This chapter teaches that wealthy individuals accelerate their success by intentionally surrounding themselves with positive, successful people and modeling their mindsets and behaviors, while poor people unconsciously limit themselves by associating with negative or unsuccessful people, and the reader must actively curate their social environment and disengage from toxic influences to adopt a millionaire mindset.

## Actionable Lessons
### Lesson 1: Model Successful People Through Association and Study
- **Step 1:** When introduced to an extremely rich or successful person, create a way to get together with them to learn how they think, exchange contacts, and potentially build a relationship.
- **Step 2:** Read biographies of extremely rich and successful people (e.g., Andrew Carnegie, John D. Rockefeller, Mary Kay, Donald Trump, Warren Buffett, Jack Welch, Bill Gates, Ted Turner) for inspiration, specific success strategies, and to copy their mindset.
- **Step 3:** Join a high-end club (tennis, health, business, or golf) to mingle with rich people in a rich environment, or if unaffordable, have coffee or tea in the classiest hotel in your city to get comfortable in that atmosphere and observe that successful people are no different from you.
- **Best Practices:** Be grateful that others have succeeded before you so you have a proven blueprint to follow; view successful people as models to learn from rather than people to envy; remind yourself "If they can do it, I can do it."
- **Common Pitfalls:** Judging, criticizing, mocking, or trying to pull successful people down to your own level, which prevents you from learning from or being inspired by them; feeling unworthy or intimidated around highly successful people.

### Lesson 2: Refuse to Subject Yourself to Negative Energy and Toxic Situations
- **Step 1:** Do not try to get negative people to change or attend self-improvement courses; your job is to better yourself and be a model of success and happiness so they may see the light in you and want some of it.
- **Step 2:** Practice reframing other people's negativity as a reminder of how not to be, without condemning them for how they are, so you do not become what you judge.
- **Step 3:** Identify a situation or person who is a downer in your life and remove yourself from that situation or association; if it is family, choose to be around them less.
- **Step 4:** Stop watching trash TV and bad news, and avoid arguing, gossiping, and backstabbing, as these are toxic situations that infect you with poisonous energy.
- **Best Practices:** Recognize that energy is contagious—either you affect people or infect them, and the same holds true in reverse; view negative circumstances as a test to grow stronger, applying the principle "Everything happens for a reason and that reason is there to assist me"; if negativity is bringing you down to the point where you cannot grow, make courageous decisions about who you want in your life.
- **Common Pitfalls:** Trying to change negative people instead of focusing on your own growth; judging and criticizing negative people, which makes you no better than them; staying in toxic relationships or situations out of obligation; exposing yourself to mindless television or bad news that poisons your mindset.

### Lesson 3: Adopt the Mindset and Identity of a Wealthy Person
- **Step 1:** Change your inner blueprint to fully believe you are every bit as good as any millionaire or multimillionaire, recognizing that successful people are no different from you.
- **Step 2:** Make declarations daily: place your hand on your heart and say "I model rich and successful people," "I associate with rich and successful people," and "If they can do it, I can do it," then touch your head and say "I have a millionaire mind!"
- **Step 3:** Associate only with successful, positive people and disassociate from negative ones; remember that "birds of a feather flock together" and most people earn within 20 percent of the average income of their closest friends.
- **Step 4:** When watching sports or other media, focus on the mindset of champions—how they handle victory with humility and continuous improvement, and how they handle loss by refocusing on the next opportunity—to learn from their attitude.
- **Best Practices:** Understand that poor people are uncomfortable with highly successful people because they are afraid of rejection or feel they don't belong, and this discomfort leads to judgment and criticism; combat this by deciding you are just as good and worthy as they are; remember that "if you want to fly with the eagles, don't swim with the ducks."
- **Common Pitfalls:** Feeling unworthy or intimidated around rich people, thinking they are special or better than you; staying in environments or relationships that bring you down; allowing the ego to protect itself through judgment and criticism of successful people; measuring your self-worth by comparing yourself to others instead of focusing on your own growth.

## Key Examples
- **Example 1:** The author describes how he intentionally creates ways to get together with extremely rich people when introduced to them, wanting to learn how they think, exchange contacts, and become personal friends if possible, because he has no interest in subjecting himself to negative energy from unsuccessful people.
- **Example 2:** A woman called into a radio interview asking what to do if her husband was a downer while she was positive and wanted to grow; the author's answer was to not try to change him, be the model of success, and if his negativity is too toxic, make courageous decisions about the relationship.
- **Example 3:** The author cites Perdita Felicien, a Canadian world champion hurdler at the 2004 Olympic Games who fell on the first hurdle and was unable to complete the race, yet reframed the setback as fuel to work even harder for the next four years, demonstrating the mindset of a champion that can be learned from.
- **Example 4:** The author states that most people earn within 20 percent of the average income of their closest friends, illustrating why it is critical to carefully choose whom you associate with and spend your time with.
- **Example 5:** Rich people often join country clubs or high-end clubs not just for the activity (golf, tennis, etc.) but to connect with other rich and successful people, leveraging the principle "It's not what you know, it's who you know."

## Notes
- Energy is a central theme: the author emphasizes that energy is contagious and that negative thinking is like "measles of the mind" (bitching instead of itching, bashing instead of scratching, frustration instead of irritation), so you must protect yourself from toxic influences just as you would avoid physical illness.
- The chapter reinforces the earlier modeling concept from the book by showing that modeling is not just about childhood conditioning from parents but also about consciously choosing adult role models and social circles.
- The author shares his personal practice of watching sports interviews to learn from champions' mindsets, specifically noting how athletes handle both victory and loss with perspective, hard work, and refocusing on the next opportunity.
- The principle "Everything happens for a reason and that reason is there to assist me" is presented as a tool to remain positive and grow stronger even when surrounded by negative people or circumstances.
- The chapter closes with a powerful declaration to reinforce the wealth file: "I model rich and successful people," "I associate with rich and successful people," "If they can do it, I can do it," and "I have a millionaire mind!"
- Millionaire Mind Actions include reading biographies of successful people, joining high-end clubs or visiting upscale venues, identifying and removing downer people or situations from your life, and stopping consumption of trash TV and bad news.
- The author warns that poor people hang around with losers because it is a matter of comfort; rich people are comfortable with other successful people and feel fully worthy of being with them, while poor people are uncomfortable with highly successful people and may be afraid of rejection or feel they don't belong, leading the ego to protect itself through judgment and criticism.
- The author shares a provocative personal story about seminar participants asking to touch him because they have never touched a multimillionaire before, using it to illustrate that people often put successful individuals on a pedestal instead of recognizing they are no different from anyone else; his advice is that if you really want to touch a millionaire, become one.
