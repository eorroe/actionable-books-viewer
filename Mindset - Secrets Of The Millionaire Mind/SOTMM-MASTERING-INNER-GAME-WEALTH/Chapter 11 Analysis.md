# Chapter 11 Analysis: The Third Influence: Specific Incidents

## Core Intent (1 sentence)
This chapter teaches readers how specific emotional incidents experienced in childhood around money create subconscious financial blueprints that drive adult money behavior, and provides a structured process to identify, disassociate from, and reprogram those incident-based beliefs in order to build a new wealth-oriented future.

## Actionable Lessons
### Lesson 1: Identify and Reprogram a Specific Childhood Money Incident
- **Step 1:** AWARENESS — Consider a specific emotional incident you experienced around money when you were young.
- **Step 2:** UNDERSTANDING — Write down how this incident may have affected your current financial life.
- **Step 3:** DISASSOCIATION — Recognize that this way of being is only what you learned and isn’t you; acknowledge that you have a choice in the present moment to be different.
- **Step 4:** DECLARATION — Place your hand on your heart and say: “I release my nonsupportive money experiences from the past and create a new and rich future.” Touch your head and say: “I have a millionaire mind!”
- **Best Practices:** Write down the incident and its effects to make the unconscious conscious; focus on emotional incidents rather than just verbal or modeled patterns.
- **Common Pitfalls:** Staying identified with the incident as “who you are” rather than viewing it as a past file of information; skipping the disassociation step and remaining stuck in the old pattern.

### Lesson 2: Resolve Money Blueprint Conflicts in Relationships
- **Step 1:** Sit down with your partner and discuss the history each of you brings to your thoughts about money — what you heard, what was modeled, and any emotional incidents that occurred.
- **Step 2:** Find out what money really means to your partner (e.g., pleasure, freedom, security, or status) to identify each other’s current money blueprint and motivations/fears.
- **Step 3:** Discuss what you want today not as individuals, but as a partnership. Decide and agree upon your general goals and attitudes with regard to money and success.
- **Step 4:** Create a written list of the attitudes and actions you both agree to live by, post it prominently, and gently remind each other of this shared agreement when issues arise.
- **Best Practices:** Approach the conversation when both parties are objective and unemotional, rather than during an active fight; focus on understanding rather than assigning blame.
- **Common Pitfalls:** Arguing about money amounts instead of addressing the underlying blueprints; assuming your partner’s money blueprint is the same as yours; reacting emotionally instead of referring back to the agreed-upon shared blueprint.

## Key Examples
- **Josey (operating-room nurse):** At age 11 she watched her father die of a heart attack after a bitter money argument, causing her to link money with pain; as an adult she subconsciously spent all her income to avoid that pain. After revising her blueprint at the Millionaire Mind Intensive Seminar, she became a financial planner and is on her way to financial freedom.
- **Harv Eker’s wife:** Repeatedly observed that her father had money while her mother did not, leading her to believe men have all the money and women do not; she spent every dollar she received. The couple’s fights over spending versus saving were rooted in mismatched money meanings (immediate pleasure vs. future freedom) until they learned to revise their individual blueprints and create a joint relationship blueprint.
- **Deborah Chamitoff:** Trusted strangers with her finances, lost nearly everything in a stock market crash, and withdrew in shame until attending the Millionaire Mind Intensive, where she reclaimed her power and now enjoys 18 sources of passive income and financial freedom.
- **Relationship blueprint mismatch:** Statistics show money is the number one cause of relationship breakups; the real issue is not the money itself but the mismatch of each partner’s money blueprint, meaning, and motivations.

## Notes
- This chapter is the third of three primary conditioning influences (verbal programming, modeling, and specific incidents) described in the book’s framework.
- The same four-step change framework (Awareness, Understanding, Disassociation, Declaration) used in previous chapters is applied here to incident-based conditioning.
- Money-related trauma can lead people into professions (such as nursing) that are attempts to resolve the original incident rather than true vocation.
- Specific incidents often create the strongest and most emotionally charged links between money and pain or pleasure, making them particularly powerful but also highly reversible with conscious work.
- A key relationship insight from the chapter: when partners fight about money, they are usually fighting about their different underlying blueprints, not about dollars and cents.
