# Chapter 10 Analysis: The Second Influence: Modeling

## Core Intent (1 sentence)
This chapter teaches readers to identify how their parents' or guardians' money behaviors and attitudes were modeled during childhood, understand whether they unconsciously replicated or rebelled against those patterns, and take concrete steps to disassociate from any unproductive financial blueprint so they can consciously choose new, wealth-supporting behaviors.

## Actionable Lessons
### Lesson 1: Identify Your Money Modeling Patterns
- **Step 1:** Consider the ways of being and habits each of your parents or guardians had around money and wealth (spender vs. saver, risk taker vs. conservative, investor vs. non-investor, how they handled debt, whether money was consistent or sporadic).
- **Step 2:** Write down how you may be identical or opposite to either of them in the arena of money.
- **Step 3:** Write down the effect this modeling has had on your financial life.
- **Best Practices:** Be honest about both conscious and unconscious patterns; recognize that rebellion against a parent's money habits is still a form of being controlled by those habits.
- **Common Pitfalls:** Assuming you are completely different from your parents without deeper inspection; overlooking that being angry or rebellious about your parents' financial situation can still link money to negative emotions; motivating wealth-building from fear, anger, or the need to prove yourself.

### Lesson 2: Disassociate from Inherited Money Patterns
- **Step 1:** Recognize that your current way of being around money is only what you learned and is not fundamentally who you are.
- **Step 2:** Acknowledge that you have a choice in the present moment to be different from the model you observed.
- **Step 3:** Use the declaration process to formally state your intention to adopt your own way.
- **Best Practices:** Separate the behavior from your identity; understand that unresolved anger toward parents around money can cause self-sabotage even after achieving wealth.
- **Common Pitfalls:** Believing the modeled behavior is your own immutable nature; getting rid of money instead of resolving the underlying anger or fear.

### Lesson 3: Align Your Motivation for Wealth with Positive Roots
- **Step 1:** Examine your true motivation for wanting money and success.
- **Step 2:** Identify whether your motivation is rooted in fear, anger, or the need to prove yourself.
- **Step 3:** If the motivation is nonsupportive, consciously shift your intention toward purpose, contribution, and joy.
- **Best Practices:** Be ruthlessly honest about why you want wealth; understand that security driven by fear will not dissolve with more money.
- **Common Pitfalls:** Believing more money will fix fear or prove self-worth; linking money to negative emotions and then sabotaging success when the higher self seeks peace.

### Lesson 4: Avoid the "Rainy Day" Savings Trap
- **Step 1:** Notice whether you save money specifically for a rainy day or for difficult times.
- **Step 2:** Recognize that focusing on "rainy days" invites more rainy days by the law of intention.
- **Step 3:** Redirect your savings intention toward a joyous day or achieving financial freedom.
- **Best Practices:** Frame savings goals around positive, abundant outcomes; use the power of intention to attract what you focus on.
- **Common Pitfalls:** Saving out of fear of loss; using language that unconsciously calls forth the very hardships you are trying to avoid.

## Key Examples
- The ham-cutting story: A woman cuts off both ends of a ham because that is how her mother cooked it, and her mother did it because her pan was too small—illustrating how people blindly replicate observed behaviors without understanding the original reason.
- T. Harv Eker's personal story: His father was an entrepreneur with dramatic boom-and-bust cycles due to project-based home building, and Eker unconsciously replicated this yo-yo income pattern for nearly ten years before recognizing and reconditioning himself out of it.
- The 63-year-old Orlando seminar attendee: After decades of trying every strategy and earning an MBA, he discovered his financial struggles were due to modeling his Depression-era father's pattern of consistently coming home empty-handed; once he released this blueprint, he accumulated more wealth in 18 months than in the previous 18 years.
- The Orlando attendee's mother warning: "Rich people are greedy... You should have just enough to get by. After that you're a pig"—showing how verbal and modeling conditioning combine to create financial ceilings.
- WWII and Depression-era children: Those raised by parents who lived through scarcity either overspend because "you could lose it all anyway" or hoard because "save for a rainy day"—both are modeled responses to parental trauma.
- Anger and rebellion: People who become wealthy to spite poor parents often link money to anger, and when they eventually seek peace, their mind tells them to get rid of the money, leaving them broke and still angry.

## Notes
- The chapter introduces the four elements of change framework also referenced from Chapter 9: Awareness, Understanding, Disassociation, and Declaration.
- The "Steps for Change: Modeling" exercise uses the same four-element structure as the verbal programming chapter.
- Saving for a "rainy day" is specifically called out as counterproductive because intention shapes reality.
- The chapter emphasizes that motivation matters more than the amount of money: fear, anger, and proving oneself cannot be solved by money.
- Rebellion against parental money patterns is still a form of being ruled by those patterns; true freedom requires choosing one's own way consciously.
- The chapter includes a partner exercise for discussing each other's money histories and creating a shared relationship money blueprint.
